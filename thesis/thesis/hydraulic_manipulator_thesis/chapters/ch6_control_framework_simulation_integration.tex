\chapter{Control Framework and Simulation Integration}
\label{chap:control}

Chapter~\ref{chap:models} left three actuator representations sitting on a
bench, each with its own contract and its own offline evidence. This chapter
puts a controller in front of them and turns them into three experiments.

Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:dls} describes the classical
controller, because it is where the competence comes from even though it never
runs at deployment. Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo} specifies the policy: what it
sees, what it emits, what stops it from emitting something dangerous, and what
it is paid for. Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:training} then explains how the first
becomes the second, and reports a result about that transfer which changed the
training protocol of this thesis. The individual stacks appear only after those three are fixed, because
they differ in one place only.

\section{The DLS Teacher and Its Two Roles}
\label{sec:ctrl:dls}

\subsection{Scope and Role of the Controller}

The classical controller occupies a narrower position in this
architecture than its prominence suggests. There is no
damped-least-squares layer underneath the policy at runtime. Nothing is added
to the policy's output. If the policy emits zero, the arm does not move.

The DLS controller is nevertheless the reason the policies work at all, and it
appears twice, both times at training or collection time and never at
deployment (Figure~\ref{fig:ctrl:teacher-student}). It is the
\emph{privileged teacher} whose behaviour the policies are distilled from
(Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:training}), and it is the closed-loop controller that
drove the identification runs of Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}. Calling it a
nominal layer would misdescribe the deployed system and, worse, would make
every reported result a property of a controller pair rather than of the
policy.

  \centering

  contact signals drive the DLS teacher, which supplies DAgger labels to the

\subsection{Privileged Information}

The teacher is \emph{privileged} rather than better tuned: it reads two
quantities that no deployable controller on
this machine could obtain. The first is the exact terrain height directly under
the cutter, queried from the simulator rather than inferred from a forward-
looking scan. The second is the true cutter--ground contact force. Together
these turn the hardest part of the task, knowing where the ground actually
is, right now, underneath a $\unit[1.2]{m}$ flail head that is occluding it,
into a lookup.

The teacher uses that privilege in a specific way. Rather than commanding a
height, it runs a contact regulator: it maintains a vertical offset relative to
the ground and adjusts that offset to hold the measured contact force near
$\unit[250]{N}$ within a $\unit[100]{N}$ deadband, pressing down below the band,
backing off proportionally above it, searching downward when contact is lost
and retreating immediately on a force spike. The accumulated offset is clamped
to $[-0.30, -0.05]\,\mathrm{m}$ so the regulator can neither drive the head into
the ground nor lift it clear.

The teacher's advantage is therefore not kinematic. Its inverse is the same DLS
solution available to anything; what it has is contact sensing. That is the
quantity the student must infer from a noisy forward scan and its own contact
history, and it is a large part of why distillation is a nontrivial transfer
rather than a formality.

\subsection{From Task Error to Cylinder Command}

The rest of the teacher is conventional and is stated for completeness. A goal
point is maintained in the truck frame: laterally it tracks the verge centre
line, vertically it follows the measured ground height plus the contact
regulator's offset. The task error is the difference between that goal and the
measured cutter position, with the lateral component weighted by a factor of
three, because on this machine lateral error is what the acceptance gate
measures and vertical error is largely handled by contact.

That error is mapped to cylinder space through the damped least-squares inverse
of the measured cutter Jacobian $\bm{J}_c$ of
Section~\ref{sec:sys:task-jacobian},
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:teacher-dls}
  \begin{aligned}
    \bm{J}_c^{\#}(\lambda)
      &= \bm{J}_c^{\mathsf T}
         \left(\bm{J}_c\bm{J}_c^{\mathsf T}
               + \lambda^2\bm{I}\right)^{-1}, \\
    \Delta\bm{x}^{*}_{1:3}
      &= \operatorname{clip}\Bigl(
          g\, \bm{J}_c^{\#}(\lambda)\, \bm{W}\bm{e},\;
          \pm\, \Delta x_{\max}\Bigr),
      \quad g = 0.30,
      \quad \Delta x_{\max} = \unit[3]{mm}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
with $\bm{W}$ the lateral weighting and the per-step clamp bounding how far any
cylinder may be asked to move in one tick. Only cylinders~1--3 are solved.
Cylinder~4 is held at the reference extension that keeps the head down, for the
reason given in Section~\ref{sec:sys:platform}. The gain $g$ is small on
purpose: this is a stiff, heavy mechanism in contact, and a resolved-rate
controller that closes quickly on the task error simply excites it.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/ch6_dls_controller}
  \caption{Feedback realization of the damped least-squares teacher of
  \eqref{eq:ctrl:teacher-dls}. The weighted task error is scaled by $g$, mapped
  to the first three cylinder coordinates by the measured damped inverse
  $\bm{J}_c^{\#}(\lambda)$, and clipped to $\pm\Delta x_{\max}$ per tick. Cylinder~4 is

\end{figure}


\subsection{Execution Through the Student Interface}

One implementation detail determines whether distillation converges to
something usable. The teacher's output $\Delta\bm{x}^{*}$ is a desired cylinder
target, and it is \emph{not} written to PhysX directly. It is converted into the
same normalized four-value action the student emits and pushed through the
student's entire action chain and into the same plant
(Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo}), so every label the student is asked to imitate is
by construction an action the student can produce. A teacher writing targets
directly to the plant would generate labels outside the student's reachable set,
giving the imitation error a floor no amount of training could remove. That
failure mode was measured here: when an earlier action-rate limit sat below the
teacher's own structural bound, $0.43\,\%$ of labels became unreachable by
construction and acted as exactly such a floor.

\section{Policy Formulation}
\label{sec:ctrl:ppo}

\subsection{Policy Definition}

The deployed controller is a memoryless Gaussian multilayer perceptron. It maps
a $66$-value observation to four normalized actions, one desired motion command
per hydraulic cylinder, and that is the entire control law. Actor and critic
are separate networks with hidden widths $256$--$128$--$64$ and ELU
activations, over empirically normalized observations. This description is of
the base policy, and it is shared by all three stacks: the actor width is the
same $66$ in each, and \ppoAAMFD{} deploys this network frozen and unmodified
rather than a widened variant of it. Two things differ for \ppoAAMFD{} and are
treated where they arise. Its four actions are interpreted as valve commands
rather than as desired cylinder increments
(Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:af-fd-sufficient}), and the deployed controller is this
policy plus an additive residual whose own encoder reads a history window
carried alongside the observation, leaving the base actor's input unchanged
(Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:af-residual}).

Three things this base policy is \emph{not} must be stated, because each was a
design option that was considered and rejected. It is not itself a residual:
it has no nominal command to correct, and where a residual does appear in
\ppoAAMFD{} it is added around this policy rather than built into it, which is
what keeps the frozen base recoverable exactly. It is not recurrent: temporal
inference is placed in a separate encoder, offline in the module of
Chapter~\ref{chap:models} and jointly trained with the residual in
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:af-encoder}, and giving the policy its own memory would
confound the two. And it does not command the vehicle: the truck's forward
motion is scripted, so the policy cannot solve a tracking problem by driving
differently.

\subsection{The Observation Vector}

Figure~\ref{fig:ctrl:observation} lays out the $66$ channels by group. The
grouping tells the story of what this controller has to reason about: nine
terrain-height samples ahead of the cutter, three task-error channels, sixteen
channels of cylinder state and remaining travel, sixteen describing the
actuator interface, ten of pose and context, and twelve of model error.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/ch6_observation}
  \caption{The $66$-value policy observation, drawn to scale by group. The
  channels combine measurements, derived quantities and actuator-model
  estimates. No trajectory-family
  label, physical factor vector or latent estimate enters the actor. AF keeps
  this $66$-channel input unchanged and carries its causal history separately.}
  \label{fig:ctrl:observation}
\end{figure}

The last group uses twelve of the sixty-six channels, so its role needs to be
explicit. These channels describe the actuator rather than the task.
Those channels carry the actuator's standing error: the cylinder tracking
residual, the actuator prediction residual, and the normalized cylinder effort.
They exist as a direct consequence of the policy being memoryless. Forming an
error requires differencing position across two steps and comparing against a
prediction issued on the previous step, and a feedforward network can construct
neither from a single observation, so without these channels the model error the
policy is asked to compensate is not merely hard to see but absent from the
input. This was measured rather than reasoned about: across ten training blocks
the p01 head alignment sat at $0.79$--$0.80$ and did not respond to changes in
the feedforward source or the levelling gain, because the policy had no
observable evidence of the error it was being asked to correct.

This group is also what makes the matched pair comparable. Under the ROM the
tracking residual is near zero, because the ROM reaches what it is told. Under
a learned ID--FD path it is the model's standing error. The same observation
contract therefore gives each policy an honest view of its own actuator's
shortcomings, which is what the comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:matching} requires. The contract is shared by
\ppoAAMFD{} as well, but comparability there rests on the paired construction
against its own frozen base rather than on the observation alone.

\subsection{The Action and Its Bounds}

The policy emits $\bm{a} \in \mathbb{R}^4$, sampled from its Gaussian at
training time and taken at the mean for evaluation. Between that sample and the
PhysX cylinder target sit five bounding stages. Figure~\ref{fig:ctrl:action-chain}
places them in the complete PPO actor--critic loop. The critic is used only
during training; the actor and bounded action interface form the deployed
controller. Each bounding stage can only \emph{reduce} authority and cannot
introduce motion the policy did not request.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/ch6_action_chain}
  \caption{PPO actor--critic formulation and deployed control path. The actor
  maps the observation to a four-dimensional Gaussian action. Rate limiting,
  exponential filtering, the travel shield, command scaling and the target-lead
  cap bound that action before the selected R, IF or AF actuator path drives
  the PhysX arm. The critic and reward update are training-only; measured state
  closes the deployed loop.}
  \label{fig:ctrl:action-chain}
\end{figure}

The rate limit bounds $|\Delta \bm{a}|$ at $0.25$ per control step and is held
\emph{constant} across every curriculum stage. It is not a curriculum knob, and
the reason is the reachability argument of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:dls}: the
teacher's own command stream has a structural per-step bound near $0.20$, so a
student limit below that makes part of the label set unreachable. The
exponential filter ($\beta = 0.70$ at the final stage) smooths the command. The
limit shield scales the command down,
and only down, and only in the direction that consumes headroom, over the last $\unit[20]{mm}$ of cylinder
travel, which is the soft replacement for the teacher's hard clamp into the
learned range. The command scale converts the filtered action into
$\unit[4]{mm}$ of cylinder travel per unit action. Finally the target
integrator accumulates the increments in reference space, with the integrated
target permitted to lead the measured extension by at most $\unit[18]{mm}$.

This chain describes the cylinder-increment interpretation of the action, which
is the one \ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} use. Under \ppoAAMFD{} the same
four-dimensional bounded action is read as a valve command and scaled to span
exactly the admissible valve range, so that no further clamp binds at nominal,
and the inverse-model anchor and the adapter residual of the earlier design are
both held at zero. The bounding stages that act in cylinder space therefore do
not have the same meaning on that stack, which is one of the reasons its
results are read against its own frozen base rather than across the stacks
(Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:matching}).

That last cap is an anti-windup device and it is load-bearing. Relaxing it from
$\unit[18]{mm}$ to $\unit[50]{mm}$ halved the lateral error on the two families
demanding a sustained standing offset (\texttt{lane\_change\_hold} from $0.4833$
to $0.2310$, \texttt{boom\_sweep} from $0.3745$ to $0.1602$), showing that the
clamp was bounding the standing effort those paths need, while the same
relaxation on cylinder~4 drove head alignment from $0.9507$ to $0.4636$. The
final task therefore resolves the trade per axis, and this is the configuration
every reported \ppoROM{} result was produced under: $\unit[50]{mm}$ on
cylinders~1 to~3 and $\unit[20]{mm}$ on cylinder~4.

Two properties of this chain follow. It is what replaces the residual band,
since exploration is bounded by how much motion a single action can command
rather than by proximity to a baseline. And it fixes the plant's capability
ceiling: four millimetres of cylinder travel per step is the entire authority
budget, which produces the $\unit[0.240]{m/s}$ lateral rate limit of
Section~\ref{sec:sys:capability} from which the reference paths are derived.

\subsection{The Reward Function}

The reward of the final \ppoROM{} task has ten bounded terms, listed in
Table~\ref{tab:ctrl:reward}. Three are positive and describe the task. Seven
are penalties and describe how the machine must behave while doing it. All are
bounded, and all retain a live gradient beyond the acceptance boundary, so that
a policy already inside a gate still has a direction to improve in.

It replaces an inherited formulation carrying $53$ weighted reward fields.
Isaac Lab's own locomotion task uses eleven. The reduction was not
cosmetic. At $53$ terms the optimum is not something a designer can reason
about, and three separate terms turned out to be working against the task
without being noticed for thirteen training campaigns. The three rules below
were each adopted after a measured failure.

\paragraph{No Gaussian Shaping}
The earlier reward priced tracking as $w\exp[-(e/s)^2]$, which is effectively
flat beyond about $2s$. Measured on the lateral term, at an error of
$\unit[0.50]{m}$ the gradient is $-2.07$ at $s=0.30$ but $-0.004$ at $s=0.15$:
a factor of $520$ weaker where the failing path families live. Every
shaping term is therefore linear or Huber in the error. Writing $x = |e|/s$,
the two shapes used are
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:shapes}
  B(e;s,c) = \min(x,\,c),
  \qquad
  H(e;s,c) = \min\Bigl(c,\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}, & x \le 1\\
    x - \tfrac{1}{2},  & x > 1
  \end{cases}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
%
$B$ has the same gradient at $\unit[0.5]{m}$ of error as at $\unit[0.05]{m}$,
so a policy far from the reference is still told which way to move; $H$ buys
quadratic precision near zero where cut height needs it, without a vanishing
far field.

\paragraph{Bounded Terms}
A term that can grow without limit can dominate the sum and destabilize the
critic. Each contribution is clipped, which gives the reward a published budget:
$6+12+4 = 22$ for the task terms against $-24$ for the safety terms. A
non-finite value is replaced by $-25$, just below that worst case, so a
numerical fault can never outscore legitimate behaviour.

\paragraph{Fixed Sign Convention}
The three task terms are non-negative and the seven safety terms non-positive.
This is what makes worst-case (CVaR) weighting safe to add later: scaling a
non-positive term by a factor $\ge 1$ can only lower the return, whereas
up-weighting a \emph{positive} reward for the worst environments pays the
policy to be bad. Measured in the earlier stack, weighting the positive contact
reward four-fold for the worst quarter of environments made a contact quality
of $0.49$ pay $7.84$ against $2.00$ for a quality of $0.50$, and collapsed
worst-family contact within a single training block.

Three terms of the earlier formulation are absent, and the reasons are results
rather than preferences. The \emph{action-magnitude} penalty on
$\operatorname{mean}(a^2)$ was removed outright: a terrain action is a desired
cylinder motion rather than a pose offset, so what the term actually charged for
was the sustained offset that \texttt{lane\_change\_hold},
\texttt{offset\_return} and \texttt{boom\_sweep} structurally require. The
policy correctly learned to return to neutral, which is the one thing ``hold an
offset'' forbids. Removing it produced the first training block in a full
session that did not degrade, improving the held-out score from $1.2058$ to
$0.3678$. The \emph{head barrier} was replaced, because in its earlier form the
head group dwarfed lateral tracking and line following could never compete for
the optimizer's attention. And the \emph{clearance barrier} was disabled, since
measured contact, cylinder headroom and cut-height error proved more reliable
signals of the same condition.

One apparent inconsistency is deliberate. The tool-acceleration term saturates
at $\unit[300]{m/s^2}$ while the corresponding gate sits at
$\unit[120]{m/s^2}$, anchored to the measured physical floor of the stage-6
environment near $\unit[100]{m/s^2}$. The reward scale is a saturation scale for
a bounded penalty rather than a target, so the gate remains the binding
criterion and the reward's role is only to keep a gradient against gross
excursions. The action-rate term is held mild for a related reason:
cylinder~4 owns head pitch, so a rate tax falls hardest on the fast corrections
that keep the head down as boom attitude shifts, and under a heavier tax the
optimizer chose head drift over paying it.

Every term is logged individually and by name during training. The earlier stack
logged an aggregate and a partial subset, with the two largest lateral terms not
logged at all, which is part of why a reward term opposing the task survived
thirteen campaigns. Reward weights on this system are measured trades rather
than preferences, and several natural-looking choices are actively harmful. That
is also why the reward is \emph{not} the result criterion: promotion uses the
eight physical gates of Section~\ref{sec:sys:evaluation-criteria}, and a policy
may raise total reward while failing the thing the reward was written to
encourage.

\subsection{Optimization}
\label{sec:ctrl:optimization}

Policies are optimized with clipped-surrogate PPO over generalized advantage
estimation, in the form of Section~\ref{sec:fund:rl-residual}. The maintained
configuration uses clip parameter $\epsilon = 0.2$, discount $\gamma = 0.99$,
GAE parameter $\lambda = 0.95$, five learning epochs over four minibatches per
update, an adaptive learning-rate schedule targeting a KL divergence of $0.02$
from a base rate of $3\times10^{-4}$, gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$, and a
clipped value loss with coefficient $1.0$. The terrain task collects $32$ steps
per environment per update. At $512$ environments that is $16\,384$ transitions
per update, chosen because the inherited $128$-step horizon exceeded two
minutes per update on the available GPU without adding useful batch diversity.

The final fine-tuning blocks depart from that configuration because they run
under the reference anchor of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:anchored} rather than an
adaptive learning-rate schedule: clip $\epsilon = 0.1$, discount
$\gamma = 0.97$, three learning epochs, fixed learning rates of $10^{-5}$ for
the actor and $10^{-4}$ for the critic, entropy coefficient $0.001$, an
exploration standard deviation fixed at $0.15$ and excluded from the gradient,
and frozen observation normalizers.

Three of those departures are structural rather than tuning. The discount is
reduced because the episode is long in simulation steps but locally controlled,
so an action should be credited for correcting the terrain state it faces rather
than for a return hundreds of steps later that the critic cannot resolve. The
actor learning rate sits an order of magnitude below the critic's so that the
actor is deliberately the slow variable and trust-region budget is not spent on
steps driven by a value function that has not yet converged. And the adaptive
schedule is dropped because the anchored formulation already constrains the
update through an explicit divergence bound; two competing step-size controllers
would make neither attributable.

The exploration settings changed for a reason specific to this architecture. A
residual policy explores around a controller that already tracks, whereas here a
zero action means the arm does not move at all, so the initial standard
deviation was raised from $0.05$ to $0.30$ and the entropy coefficient with it,
to let the policy discover the command directions before refining them. On the
terrain task, where distillation supplies a competent starting point, both are
reduced again and the standard deviation is \emph{held fixed} rather than
initialized, for a further reason taken up in Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:anchored}.
Exact values for any reported run must be read from that run's frozen manifest,
because they changed during development.

\section{Training Protocol and the Distillation Finding}
\label{sec:ctrl:training}

\subsection{Distillation by DAgger}

Policies are initialized by imitation, not by reward. The privileged teacher of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:dls} is rolled out in the student's own environment, and
the student is fitted to the teacher's raw action by regression, in the DAgger
scheme of Ross et al.~\cite{ross2011reduction}: the teacher's probability of
\emph{executing} the action, as opposed to merely labelling it, is annealed
from one to zero over the first $70\,\%$ of iterations, so the later part of
training collects states the student itself visits rather than states the
teacher would have visited. Labels are accumulated in a replay buffer of
$262\,144$ transitions and refitted over three epochs per iteration in
minibatches of $8\,192$.

Figure~\ref{fig:ctrl:distillation} shows a complete distillation run for the
ROM stack, taken from the stored record. Panel~(a) is the handover schedule.
Panel~(b) is the imitation error: the behaviour-cloning loss falls by more than an order of magnitude while the
teacher is still driving, reaches its minimum around the handover, and then
\emph{rises} once the student is alone. That rise is not a failure. It is the
distribution shift DAgger exists to expose: the student is now visiting states
the teacher never chose, and the teacher's advice on those states is new
information rather than a repetition of what has already been fitted.
Panel~(c) shows the corresponding closed-loop task metrics. They remain near
the teacher's preflight values without matching them.

\subsection{Objective Mismatch in Unconstrained Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:ctrl:finding}

The initial plan was to use the distilled actor as the PPO initialization and
improve it without further constraints. On this task, that approach failed.
The result motivates the anchored formulation of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:anchored}, which is used for the final policies.

PPO fine-tuning was run repeatedly from distilled checkpoints, and the outcome
was consistent enough to be treated as a property of the problem rather than as
bad luck. The critic was healthy throughout, with explained variance in the
$0.85$--$0.95$ band, so the failure is not a value-estimation failure. Total
reward rose. And the primary tracking objective degraded, in the worst case by
a factor of $12.9$.

The mechanism follows from placing the reward and the gates side by side. The reward pays for ground contact and cut height as well as lateral
tracking, and the optimizer discovers that it can buy the first two by spending
the third. That trade increases total reward and moves the policy away from the gate that decides acceptance. The reward's optimum is simply not where
the tracking gate is. Four earlier fine-tuning campaigns on a related
configuration failed the same way in a different currency: every block degraded
head alignment, with p01 falling from $0.9854$ to the $0.48$--$0.78$ range while
safety terminations rose from $1.3\,\%$ to $3.6$--$7.7\,\%$.

This is a claim about two functionals over the same policy space rather
than about the optimizer. Training maximizes the expected return
$J(\vartheta)$, while acceptance is decided by the vector of physical gates
$\bm{G}(\vartheta) \preceq \bm{g}^{\star}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:sys:evaluation-criteria}. Writing
$\mathcal{F} = \{\vartheta : \bm{G}(\vartheta) \preceq \bm{g}^{\star}\}$ for the
feasible set, the measured content of the finding is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:mismatch}
  \arg\max_{\vartheta} J(\vartheta) \notin \mathcal{F},
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  \vartheta_{\mathrm{distilled}} \in \mathcal{F}.
\end{equation}
%
Distillation lands inside $\mathcal{F}$ because the teacher was constructed to
satisfy the gates, not to maximize $J$. Unconstrained ascent on $J$ from that
point therefore leaves $\mathcal{F}$ by construction. This is the optimizer
working correctly on the wrong functional, and it is why the remedy is a
constraint on the ascent rather than a repair of the reward.

Two consequences follow, and both are protocol rather than interpretation.
Unconstrained fine-tuning is inadmissible, and both the pre- and post-fine-tune
checkpoints are reported in every case; the matching rules that this imposes on
the three-stack comparison are stated in Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:constraints}.
And reward decomposition, critic explained variance, policy entropy and the
gate metrics themselves are logged \emph{during} training rather than only at
the end, because the whole point is that total reward went up while the system
got worse. A training curve reporting only the scalar reward would have shown a
success.

The general form of the result applies beyond this machine. When a competent
teacher exists and the acceptance criterion is not the reward, reward-based
fine-tuning is not automatically an improvement step: PPO optimizes the written
objective, that objective is a proxy, and the teacher was constructed to do the
task. Distillation transfers the intent; PPO then optimizes the proxy, and here
the two point in measurably different directions. Since that divergence grows
with distance travelled from a policy where proxy and intent were measured to
agree, the remedy is not to abandon reward optimization but to bound the
distance explicitly, which is the subject of the next section.

\section{Reference-Anchored PPO}
\label{sec:ctrl:anchored}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ctrl:mismatch} says that reward ascent leaves the feasible
set. It does not say that reward ascent is useless, only that it is unbounded.
The construction of this section keeps PPO and constrains how far it may travel
from a policy already known to satisfy the gates. Writing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$
for a frozen reference policy and $J$ for the expected return, each training
block solves
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:constrained}
  \max_{\vartheta} J(\vartheta)
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad
  \mathbb{E}_{\bm{o}\sim d_{\pi_\vartheta}}
  \bigl[ \mathrm{KL}\bigl( \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\,|\,\bm{o})
  \,\|\, \pi_\vartheta(\cdot\,|\,\bm{o}) \bigr) \bigr]
  \le \delta_{\mathrm{ref}},
\end{equation}
%
with the expectation taken over the states the policy itself visits.

\subsection{Critic Warm-Up}

A resumed checkpoint carries an actor $\vartheta$ and a critic $\psi$. After
distillation the actor already behaves sensibly, but the critic has never been
fitted to the long terrain return, so the first PPO updates would move a useful
actor using a value estimate that is not yet meaningful. The actor is therefore
frozen for $150$ iterations while only the value loss is optimized, under the
asserted invariant $\vartheta_{\text{after}} = \vartheta_{\text{before}}$. The
observation normalizers are frozen at the same point: without that, the same
raw observation is normalized differently from one iteration to the next, and
the actor's behaviour changes while its weights do not.

\subsection{The Reference Anchor}

Let $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be a frozen copy of the accepted warm actor. It is
queried only on states the live policy visits and never supplies an executed
action, so PPO remains the sole controller of the plant. The penalty is the
\emph{forward} divergence, which for diagonal Gaussians is available in closed
form,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:kl}
  D_{\mathrm{ref}}
  = \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} \,\|\, \pi_\vartheta\bigr)
  = \sum_i \left[
    \log\frac{\sigma_{\vartheta,i}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref},i}}
    + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref},i}^{2}
      + (\mu_{\mathrm{ref},i}-\mu_{\vartheta,i})^{2}}
      {2\sigma_{\vartheta,i}^{2}}
    - \frac{1}{2}\right],
\end{equation}
%
at the cost of one additional forward pass and no sampling. Because both scales
are fixed at $\sigma = 0.15$, this collapses to a pure quadratic on the drift of
the commanded action,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:kl-fixed}
  D_{\mathrm{ref}}
  = \sum_i \frac{(\mu_{\mathrm{ref},i}-\mu_{\vartheta,i})^{2}}{2(0.15)^{2}},
\end{equation}
%
which is the second reason for fixing $\sigma$: with a free standard deviation,
entropy collapse shrinks $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ while the commanded action moves, so
the constraint would report compliance during the drift it exists to prevent.

The direction is load-bearing. Forward KL is mass-covering: it charges
$\pi_\vartheta$ wherever $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ has density and $\pi_\vartheta$
does not, and therefore penalizes the \emph{abandonment} of reference
behaviour. The reverse direction is mode-seeking and would permit the policy to
collapse onto a subset of reference behaviour at no cost, which is the observed failure, in which one competence was dropped to buy another. It
follows that the term is a behaviour-cloning anchor evaluated on the policy's
own state distribution, rather than the reverse-KL regularizer of soft
reinforcement learning. Its lineage is demonstration-regularized policy
gradient~\cite{rajeswaran2018dapg}, not the Boltzmann-optimum family.

The actor objective is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:anchored-loss}
  \mathcal{L}(\vartheta)
  = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PPO}}
  + \mathcal{L}_{V}
  - \beta\,\mathcal{H}(\pi_\vartheta)
  + \lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}\,
    \mathbb{E}_k\bigl[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} \| \pi_\vartheta)\bigr],
  \qquad
  \lambda_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0.10 .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Trust Regions and Rollback}

A penalty is not a constraint: at a fixed coefficient, a sufficiently large
advantage always buys its way past. Feasibility is therefore also verified on the update taken. Two bounds are imposed,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:bounds}
  D_{\mathrm{update}} = \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\mathrm{old}} \| \pi_\vartheta)
  \le 0.01,
  \qquad
  D_{\mathrm{ref}} \le 0.05,
\end{equation}
%
and they do different work. $D_{\mathrm{update}}$ is the classical step-size
control that keeps the surrogate a valid local model of $J$.
$D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the new object: because the anchor is \emph{fixed} within
a block rather than moving with the policy, it bounds cumulative drift. A
sequence of updates each individually satisfying $D_{\mathrm{update}}$ can
still walk arbitrarily far from feasibility, and that is the trajectory the earlier campaigns took, monotonically, with the reward rising the whole
way. Only a constraint referred to a fixed feasible point can observe it.

Enforcement is in two stages. During minibatch optimization the epoch is
early-stopped as soon as $D_{\mathrm{update}}$ exceeds its bound. After the
update both divergences are recomputed over the full rollout. If either bound
fails, or any quantity is non-finite, the actor and its optimizer state are
restored \emph{atomically}. On a divergence rejection the critic fit is
retained when finite, since the bounds constrain the policy and not the value
function, and rewinding valid critic fits computed on the same on-policy
rollout was wasting most batches near the boundary. On a non-finite update both
networks are restored. In the final block, five of one hundred logged updates
were rejected, all of them on the reference bound and none on the update
bound. That is, the drift caught was of the kind that ordinary PPO
clipping does not see.

\subsection{The Block Ratchet}

The reference is not fixed forever. Within a block it is frozen. When a
checkpoint is scored and passes the gates, it is promoted to reference,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:ratchet}
  \vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(m+1)} = \vartheta^{\star},
  \qquad
  \vartheta^{\star} \in \mathcal{F},
  \quad
  \vartheta^{\star} \in
  B_{\delta_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigl(\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(m)}\bigr),
\end{equation}
%
and the actor's optimizer moments are reset, because momentum accumulated
relative to the old anchor is not meaningful relative to the new one. The
critic's are retained: it is fitting the same return, and its calibration is
expensive to rebuild.

This is the structural point of the construction. The policy is at all times
within a bounded divergence of a checkpoint that has been verified against
$\bm{G}$, and the anchor advances only through verified checkpoints. The
acceptance criterion therefore enters the training loop instead of appearing
only at the end. The optimizer still ascends the proxy. It simply can never
travel far from the last point at which proxy and intent were measured to
agree. In this construction distillation is no longer the endpoint: it is
$\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(0)}$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ctrl:anchored} summarizes the procedure in five stages.
The first two produce the initial verified anchor. Stages~3 and~4 repeat for
each PPO block, and Stage~5 returns the last promoted checkpoint.

\begin{algorithm}[!ht]
  \small
  \DontPrintSemicolon
  \SetKwInOut{Input}{input}\SetKwInOut{Output}{output}
  \Input{teacher $\pi_T$; gate test
    $\bm{G}(\vartheta)\preceq\bm{g}^{\star}$; $M$ PPO blocks; anchored loss
    and divergence bounds from Equations~\eqref{eq:ctrl:anchored-loss}
    and~\eqref{eq:ctrl:bounds}}
  \Output{last promoted actor $\vartheta^{\star}$ satisfying the gate test}
  \tcp{\textbf{Stage 1 -- Distil a feasible actor}}
  $\mathcal{D}\leftarrow$ DAgger states labelled by teacher mean action
    $\bm{y}^{T}$\;
  $\vartheta \leftarrow \arg\min_{\vartheta}
    \mathbb{E}_{(\bm{o},\bm{y}^{T})\sim\mathcal{D}}
    \bigl[\|\mu_{\vartheta}(\bm{o})-\bm{y}^{T}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]$\;
  remove $\pi_T$ from the controller
    \tcp*{all later rollouts are teacher-free}
  \tcp{\textbf{Stage 2 -- Warm the critic and set the first anchor}}
  freeze $\vartheta$ and the observation normalizers\;
  \For{$j\leftarrow1$ \KwTo $150$}{
    roll out $\pi_{\vartheta}$ and set
    $\psi \leftarrow \psi - \eta_{\mathrm{critic}}
      \nabla_{\psi}\mathcal{L}_{V}(\psi)$\;
  }
  verify $\vartheta_{\mathrm{after}}=\vartheta_{\mathrm{before}}$\;
  $\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}\leftarrow\vartheta$;
    $\vartheta^{\star}\leftarrow\vartheta$
    \tcp*{initial verified anchor}
  \For{block $m\leftarrow0$ \KwTo $M-1$}{
    \tcp{\textbf{Stage 3 -- Train inside the frozen reference bound}}
    freeze $\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ for the whole block\;
    \For{each PPO iteration in block $m$}{
      collect an on-policy rollout with $\pi_{\vartheta}$ as sole controller\;
      compute and normalize GAE; save snapshot $\mathcal{S}$;
        set $\vartheta_{\mathrm{old}}\leftarrow\vartheta$\;
      \For{$3$ epochs over the rollout minibatches}{
        \lIf{$\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}\|\pi_{\vartheta})
          >\delta_{\mathrm{upd}}$}{stop the update epochs}
        update actor and critic using Equation~\eqref{eq:ctrl:anchored-loss}\;
      }
      compute $(D_{\mathrm{upd}},D_{\mathrm{ref}})$ on the full rollout
        \tcp*{full rollout}
      \uIf{$D_{\mathrm{upd}}$ or $D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is non-finite}{
        restore the complete snapshot $\mathcal{S}$\;
      }
      \uElseIf{$D_{\mathrm{upd}}>\delta_{\mathrm{upd}}$ \textbf{or}
        $D_{\mathrm{ref}}>\delta_{\mathrm{ref}}$}{
        restore the actor and its optimizer moments from $\mathcal{S}$;
          retain the new critic fit\;
      }
    }
    \tcp{\textbf{Stage 4 -- Evaluate the block and promote a passing checkpoint}}
    score every saved checkpoint on at least three held-out terrain meshes\;
    aggregate each gate to the worst mesh and, for tracking gates, the worst
      path family\;
    $\mathcal{C}\leftarrow
      \{\vartheta_c:\bm{G}(\vartheta_c)\preceq\bm{g}^{\star}\}$\;
    \eIf{$\mathcal{C}\neq\varnothing$}{
      choose $\vartheta^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}$ using the fixed checkpoint
        selection rule\;
      load $\vartheta\leftarrow\vartheta^{\star}$ and set
        $\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}\leftarrow\vartheta^{\star}$\;
      reset the actor optimizer moments; retain the critic state\;
    }{
      leave $\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}$ unchanged; do not promote the block\;
    }
  }
  \tcp{\textbf{Stage 5 -- Return the deployable policy}}
  \Return{$\vartheta^{\star}=\vartheta_{\mathrm{ref}}$}\;
  \caption{Five-stage reference-anchored PPO procedure. The teacher is used
  only in Stage~1. All PPO rollouts are controlled by $\pi_{\vartheta}$ alone,
  and the frozen reference moves only when a checkpoint passes every gate.}
  \label{alg:ctrl:anchored}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Theoretical Status}

The components are standard and citable. The bound on $D_{\mathrm{update}}$ is
conservative policy iteration and its trust-region
descendants~\cite{kakade2002approximately,schulman2015trust}; the atomic
rollback is that literature's backtracking line search reduced to a single
trial point; the penalty in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ctrl:anchored-loss} is a Lagrangian relaxation of the
constrained problem~\eqref{eq:ctrl:constrained} evaluated at a fixed multiplier,
with the rollback supplying the feasibility enforcement a fixed multiplier
cannot.

What does not follow is a certificate. Bounding the divergence from a verified
policy does bound gate degradation in form: the chain rule of KL over
trajectories, Pinsker's inequality and the boundedness of every gate metric give
$|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\vartheta}[g] - \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[g]|
\le 2 G_{\max}\sqrt{H \delta_{\mathrm{ref}}/2}$ for a metric $g$ with
$\|g\|_\infty \le G_{\max}$ over a horizon $H$, a bound available only because
the reward of Table~\ref{tab:ctrl:reward} is bounded term by term and one that
an exponential term would destroy. At the production horizon of $2808$ steps
and $\delta_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0.05$, however, the right-hand side exceeds eight,
which for a metric bounded in $[0,1]$ is vacuous. There is also a measure
mismatch, since the constraint is taken under the live policy's state
distribution while the chain rule requires the reference's.

The honest claim is therefore narrower. The construction is a principled
restriction of reward ascent to a neighbourhood of a policy where proxy and
acceptance criterion were measured to agree, assembled from standard
trust-region and demonstration-regularized results, with feasibility
re-established empirically at every block boundary. The gates, not the theory,
certify each promotion.

\section{R: Reference Actuator Baseline}
\label{sec:ctrl:ppo-rom}

\ppoROM{} is the reference condition of this thesis. The policy emits desired
cylinder motion, the frozen command ROM converts it to a cylinder position
target, and PhysX closes the loop. There is no online adaptation anywhere in
the stack. The truck is scripted over a $\unit[26]{m}$ strip at one constant
speed drawn per episode from $2$ to $\unit[7]{km/h}$, the eleven path families
of Section~\ref{sec:sys:task} are distributed across environments within a
single run, and the terrain mesh is drawn per environment so that no policy can
memorize one ground surface.

Its status is that of a reference condition rather than a competitor, as
developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}: the command ROM is not a pure
Simscape artefact but a Simscape-trained model with a bounded closed-loop
calibration against IsaacLab and PhysX, and ROM-plus-PhysX \emph{is} the
deployment plant. \ppoROM{}'s actuator therefore coincides with the plant. Its
tracking advantage over \ppoIDFD{} is consequently not evidence that a reduced
order model approximates the actuator better than an identified ID/FD pair: one of the two
defines the target.

The policy is produced by the three-layer construction of this chapter:
distillation from the privileged DLS teacher to a feasible student, critic
warm-up with the actor frozen, and reference-anchored PPO in blocks under
Equations~\eqref{eq:ctrl:anchored-loss} and~\eqref{eq:ctrl:bounds}. The final
block produced checkpoints $280$ to $329$. The last iterate was not taken,
because PPO can raise mean return while damaging a single path family.
Candidates were scored instead on held-out meshes against the eight gates,
aggregated to the worst mesh and, for the tracking-critical gates, the worst
path family.

The gate table for that selected checkpoint is deliberately not printed here,
and the reason is a limitation rather than an omission. The numbers on record
were measured on the superseded $\unit[16]{m}$ rollout, and the checkpoint's own
selection record marks them provisional for exactly that reason; that
checkpoint's own full-length six-mesh revalidation was never run. Quoting a
certified gate result for \ppoROM{} on the strength of a shorter rollout would
be the kind of claim this thesis is written to avoid, so \ppoROM{} is described
here as a reference \emph{condition} whose acceptance evidence is pending, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reports the revalidated figures for all three
stacks under one protocol.

Three \ppoROM{} acceptance records exist in the project and they disagree. The
thesis therefore distinguishes them explicitly. The
pipeline's own documentation reports all eight gates passing across the $2$ to
$\unit[7]{km/h}$ range on three meshes, which is a selection-time record for a
different checkpoint than the one described above. An earlier six-mesh
evaluation of the rebuilt pipeline reports seven gates passing and
\emph{ground contact failing}, at $0.6486$ against the $0.700$ threshold and a
margin of $-0.0514$, which was at the time the one genuine performance gap in
the stack. Neither record certifies the checkpoint this chapter selects, and
neither is the evidence used in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}: the closed-loop
comparison there re-scores a single checkpoint per stack under one protocol, at
one speed, over the full path, on the same meshes and the same eleven families.
The earlier ground-contact failure is still the right expectation to carry into
that chapter. It is measured on a worst-mesh, worst-family aggregation,
which is the statistic that the pooled per-cell means of
Table~\ref{tab:eval:tracking} do not report, and it identifies ground contact on
the ROM plant as the gate most likely to bind, a prediction
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} is able to check rather than assume.

\section{IF: Non-Adaptive Learned Baseline}
\label{sec:ctrl:ppo-idfd}

\ppoIDFD{} subclasses the \ppoROM{} task and changes exactly one thing. The
policy still emits desired cylinder motion, but that command is now realised by
the frozen inverse model of Section~\ref{sec:models:id}, which converts it to a
valve command, followed by the frozen strict forward model of
Section~\ref{sec:models:fd}, which converts the valve command to a predicted
extension increment. PhysX receives the same bounded cylinder target as before.
Task, terrain families, meshes, observation contract, action chain, reward,
gates and the PPO update are inherited unchanged, so the actuator
representation is the only free variable between the two stacks.

The stack matters to the argument twice over. It is the honest control for
\ppoROM, whose actuator coincides with the deployment plant, and it is the
stack in which the inverse model is carried in the command path and is adequate
there. It is not the reference against which \ppoAAMFD{} is measured: the two
do not share the action contract, and the adaptive claim is made against that
stack's own frozen base instead (Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:matching}).

\emph{Robust} is used here in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:fund:domain-randomization}: the stack was trained across the
randomised plant family and applies one fixed response to whichever member of
that family it meets, performing no online estimation of plant characteristics
and conditioning on no plant identity. It does not mean the policy is blind to
plant variation. The twelve model-error channels of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo} carry the actuator's standing residual, and that
residual is itself plant-dependent: under a given plant the actuator misses its
commanded increment by a characteristic amount, and the policy acts on that
miss. What the stack lacks is not information about the consequences of plant
variation but any representation of \emph{which} plant produced them.

Two properties of the composed path shape its behaviour, and both were
established offline. The valve command is accumulated rather than issued
absolutely and is clipped to the per-axis limits $[0.95, 0.95, 0.80, 0.80]$, so
the actuator path carries its own saturation boundary independently of the
action chain. And only the predicted extension increment is integrated, since
the forward model's velocity output failed validation on two axes
(Section~\ref{sec:models:fd}) and is carried as a diagnostic. This stack's
standing model error is therefore visible to the policy through the twelve
model-error channels of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo}, which is what makes the
shared observation contract fair across actuators rather than merely
identical.

The promoted policy's run manifest records all eight gates passing across the
$2$ to $\unit[7]{km/h}$ speed range on three held-out meshes. That is the
selection-time evidence on which the checkpoint was promoted, not a certified
result: it was measured on three meshes rather than the six the final claim
uses, and it is superseded by the single-protocol revalidation of
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, where the per-stack tracking numbers belong. One protocol point belongs here: the reported
policy was obtained under the reference-anchored construction of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:anchored} with a lower actor learning rate than
\ppoROM{} and a fixed exploration standard deviation of $0.05$, which is read
from the run manifest rather than assumed to match.

\label{sec:ctrl:ppo-aamfd}

\ppoAAMFD{} places online adaptation on the forward model alone. The policy
emits the valve command directly, the frozen strict forward model of

increment, and PhysX receives the same bounded cylinder target as the other two
stacks:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:af-path}
  \pi_{\mathrm{frozen}} + \text{history residual}
  \;\longrightarrow\; \bm{u}
  \;\longrightarrow\; f_{\mathrm{FD}}
  \;\longrightarrow\; \text{PhysX} .
\end{equation}
%
The inverse model is absent from the command path. This is the one structural
difference from \ppoIDFD{}, and it is deliberate: the name of the stack records
the two models that remain, \AAM{} and \FD{}. Task, terrain families, meshes,

so the actuator representation and the adaptation path are the only free
variables against the baselines.

That the inverse model is genuinely out of the loop is a verified property
rather than a claim about intent. Under the direct-valve configuration the
adaptation-site instrumentation reports zero valve elements attributable to the
inverse model and zero adapter applications at the inverse-model site across a

\texttt{id\_aam\_fd}; it is retained so that previously trained checkpoints

path. The reported counters are \texttt{valve\_elements} $= 0$,

\subsection{Why the Inverse Model Is Omitted}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-no-id}

The three stacks are not three attempts at one thing, and the inverse model's
place in two of them follows from what each is for. The command ROM together
with PhysX \emph{is} the deployment plant
(Section~\ref{sec:models:rom-calibration}), and it is the only actuator
representation calibrated against that plant, so \ppoROM{} uses the plant
itself and fixes the reference condition. The inverse and forward models are
identified independently of it, from the high-fidelity Simscape data of
Chapter~\ref{chap:data}, and neither has seen the deployment plant during
training. \ppoIDFD{} therefore measures something specific: the cost of
substituting an independently identified pair for the plant itself.

That question requires the inverse model. A policy that commands desired
cylinder motion needs a map from that intent to a valve command, and supplying
that map from identification data alone is exactly the substitution being
priced. Composing the inverse model with the strict forward model is thus the
right construction for the non-adaptive stack, and the R--IF gap is
interpretable only because the composition is complete. The inverse model earns
its place in \ppoIDFD{} on that reasoning and keeps it; nothing established
below withdraws it.

The adaptive stack asks a different question, and the inverse model does not
survive the change. It was not discarded on suspicion: it was adopted only
after it had been shown to be trustworthy, and it was disqualified later for a
reason that the trust analysis itself uncovered.

Three findings established the trust. Per-valve supervised validation reached
$R^2 = 0.9973$--$0.9996$. Paired control fine-tuning
(Section~\ref{sec:models:id-paired}) repaired the one property the supervised
score could not certify: the composite normalised control error fell from
$1.0011$, which is exactly the zero-response baseline, to $0.1086$, with all
per-axis regression slopes above $0.8$ and all correlations above $0.92$. The
frozen checkpoint then transferred to the $118$-run deployment collection, which
lies outside its Simscape training distribution
(Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}), with teacher-forced one-step extension
error of $\unit[2.36]{mm}$ on the combined domain against $\unit[3.36]{mm}$ on
nominal, and a held-out group no worse than the domains used in training. That
is the regime in which the model is actually used, because the policy closes the
loop at every tick. On that evidence the inverse model was adequate for
\ppoIDFD, and \ppoIDFD{} was built on it. \ppoIDFD{} remains in
this thesis for exactly that reason: it is the stack in which the inverse model
is used and is adequate, and it is reported as such.

The same transfer study recorded the failure mode that later disqualified the
model for adaptation. Fed its own predictions instead of the measured state, the
composite left the reference by more than a metre in \emph{every one} of the
$118$ runs, and the mechanism was saturation: its pre-clip valve command sat at
the limit on $89.1\,\%$ of elements, while the ROM valve recorded on the same
runs never reached its bound at all.
Instrumentation of the composed path in the closed loop measures the same thing
directly, and in the terms that matter for adaptation rather than for rollout
stability. Over the $33$-cell panel the inverse model's command sat at its
pre-clip limit on $79.9\,\%$ of valve elements, a figure consistent with the
per-cylinder occupancies reported in
Section~\ref{sec:models:adapter-null}; $34.5\,\%$ of the corrections the
adapter computed were clipped away after being computed; and on $37.9\,\%$ of
elements the correction could not move the command at all. A bounded residual
cannot unsaturate an anchor that is already on its stops, and the consequence
is not merely a small effect but an uninterpretable one: a null measured
through such a path cannot distinguish a task that is not adaptation-limited
from credit that was clipped before it could reach the metric.

The mechanism is visible in the model's own output contract. The inverse model
predicts a valve-command \emph{increment}, and the deployed command is the
accumulated, clipped quantity
$\bm{u}_k = \operatorname{clip}(\bm{u}_{k-1} + \Delta\bm{u}_k, -\bm{u}_{\max},
\bm{u}_{\max})$ with per-axis limits $[0.95,\, 0.95,\, 0.80,\, 0.80]$. Under
sustained demand the accumulation walks the command onto its own clip and holds
it there.

This is what removes the headroom that online adaptation requires, and it
explains why the measured clipping above is structural rather than incidental.
The quantity being adapted and the quantity being saturated are the same, so the
saturation is not an occasional excursion that a bounded residual can work
around: it is the operating condition under precisely the sustained demand for
which adaptation was introduced. A mechanism that needs the command to move
cannot be demonstrated on a command that is at its stops nine times in ten.

The disqualification is therefore specific, and it is not a retraction of
anything claimed for \ppoIDFD{}. The inverse model remains adequate wherever
its command is consumed as issued, because saturation of a command that nothing
further must be added to is a limit on authority rather than a defect of the
map. It fails only where a second signal has to be added to that command and
survive the same clip. The non-adaptive stack is the first case and the
adaptive stack is the second, which is why one keeps the inverse model and the
other cannot.

Repairing the inverse model rather than removing it was considered and
rejected on cost. The repair would have required a new data collection, and the
scale of that collection is known from the precedent in this same pipeline: the
forward model suffered an analogous identifiability failure, and it was
repaired only by collecting a purpose-built rank-4 open-loop valve-plateau
dataset over four plant draws (Section~\ref{sec:models:fd-identifiability}).
Two further limitations pointed the same way. The inverse model is trained on
Simscape data alone, and deployment already substitutes the training-mean
pressure because supplying the measured pressure raises extension error from
$\unit[1.311]{mm}$ to $\unit[1.907]{mm}$. A model that must be fed a
counterfactual input to remain accurate is a poor foundation for a mechanism
whose entire purpose is to react to the true plant.

The decision follows from the direct-valve formulation. Once the policy emits
the valve command itself, the inverse model has no remaining function: its sole
role in \ppoIDFD{} was to convert desired cylinder motion into a valve command,
and a policy that outputs the valve command performs that conversion inside its
own learned mapping. The saturation is not thereby abolished, since the
command is still bounded, but it is no longer produced by an accumulator
outside the policy's control, and the residual acts on the same quantity the
policy itself has learned to keep within range. The inverse model was
therefore removed rather than repaired, and no new data collection was
required.

\subsection{Whether the Forward Model Alone Is Sufficient}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-fd-sufficient}

Removing one of the two learned actuator models raises the question of whether
the remaining one carries the path. Sufficiency here is a specific claim: the
forward model must convert a commanded valve position into a one-step extension
increment with genuine valve sensitivity, because that increment is the only
quantity the controller integrates.

The evidence for that claim is the strict retraining of
Section~\ref{sec:models:fd-identifiability}, and it is the reason the
retraining was necessary in the first place. Before it, the supervised score
was misleading: at a $\unit[0.5]{s}$ horizon, zeroing the valve input changed
the rollout-error ratio to only $0.94$, meaning the network had extrapolated
smooth closed-loop kinematics rather than learned the valve's effect. After
strict retraining on the open-loop plateau data the valve-zero error ratio
rises to $1.81$, extension rollout RMSE falls from $\unit[75.2]{mm}$ to
$\unit[12.4]{mm}$ at $\unit[0.5]{s}$ and from $\unit[157.9]{mm}$ to
$\unit[27.3]{mm}$ at $\unit[1.0]{s}$, and per-axis one-step extension $R^2$
improves from $[-5.7,\, 0.60]$ to $[0.76,\, 0.98]$. The kinematic consistency
term holds in the strict data with $R^2 = 0.994$--$0.9997$.

Sufficiency is therefore established on the channel that is used and is not
claimed beyond it. Three qualifications are carried forward explicitly. Only
the extension increment is integrated; the velocity output remains weak on two
axes and is retained as a diagnostic. Zero-order hold remains competitive below
$\unit[0.25]{s}$, so the forward model earns its place at the horizons the
controller actually spans rather than uniformly. And the valve authority the
model represents is strongly operating-point dependent, varying by a factor of
$15$--$75$ across pressure quartiles and capable of changing sign, which is why
a constant valve Jacobian was rejected and why the reduced-order structure is
retained elsewhere in the pipeline. Within those limits the forward model
supplies what the stack requires, and the composite no longer depends on a
second learned model whose saturation was its dominant failure mode.

\subsection{The Residual Controller}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-residual}

Adaptation enters as an additive, bounded residual on the valve command:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ctrl:af-residual}
  \bm{u} = \operatorname{clip}\bigl(
    \bm{u}_0 + \alpha \tanh
    \bigl( g_\psi(\bm{o},\, \phi(\bm{H}_t)) \bigr),\, -1,\, 1 \bigr),
  \qquad
  \bm{u}_0 = \pi_{\mathrm{frozen}}(\bm{o}) ,
\end{equation}
%
where $\phi$ is a temporal-convolution encoder over a window of
$(\text{state},\, \text{action})$ pairs and $g_\psi$ is a small
multilayer perceptron producing a per-cylinder correction. Only $\phi$ and
$g_\psi$ are trained: $53\,836$ parameters against a $59\,588$-parameter frozen
base.

Three construction choices make the comparison in
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} a single-variable one, and each was verified
rather than assumed. The final layer of $g_\psi$ is zero-initialised and
$\tanh(0) = 0$, so at construction the residual is exactly zero and the wrapped
policy reproduces the frozen base bit for bit, at a measured maximum absolute
action difference of $0.0$. Every arm of an ablation therefore begins as the
identical policy, and any end-state difference is attributable to the residual
having been learned rather than to the task having been relearned. The
correction is bounded by $\alpha\tanh(\cdot)$ regardless of what the head
produces: with head weights drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0,\, 50)$ the output
saturates at exactly $\alpha$ and remains finite, so a bad update degrades
accuracy instead of emitting a non-finite action, which matters because a
non-finite action written into the history buffer would poison the entire
window. And the residual scale can be set to zero at evaluation time without
touching any other part of the stack, in which case the stack reduces to the
frozen base exactly.

This last property is a strict improvement on the arrangement it replaces. In
the earlier latent-conditioned design the actor was itself a $68$-input
network, so disabling the residual restored the baseline actuator path but not
the baseline actor, and the ablation was never quite single-variable. Under
\ppoAAMFD{} the base actor is the distilled policy unmodified, the residual
carries the whole of the context dependence, and disabling it recovers the
baseline in full.

The history window is carried in the observation vector rather than in a buffer
held inside the module. PPO calls the actor many times per update epoch, and a
module-held buffer would be advanced by the optimisation loop and desynchronise
from the simulation; carrying it in the observation means the rollout buffer
holds exactly the history under which each action was taken. At evaluation the
opposite convention is correct, since the policy is called once per step. The
causal convention is fixed throughout: the estimate at step $t$ uses
$\bm{x}_{t-k:t-1}$ and $\bm{a}_{t-k:t-1}$ and does not include step $t$.

\subsection{Why the Base Policy Is Frozen}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-frozen}

Freezing the base is structural rather than a tuning preference, and the
alternative was closed by measurement before the residual route was adopted.

Under a trajectory-regularised fine-tune with a reference-KL anchor the policy
is stable but cannot acquire a new input dependence, because the anchor's
reference is context-blind by construction and any context-dependent behaviour
therefore increases KL to that reference and is penalised. A gradient
multiplier of $1000$ applied to the context columns alone moved action
sensitivity from $0.001\,\%$ to $0.004\,\%$ of action scale over $400$
iterations. Masking the anchor at $\bm{z}=\bm{0}$, so that context dependence
costs nothing in KL, was tried on a criterion fixed in advance and also failed,
reaching $0.005\,\%$ of action scale with context weights four orders of
magnitude below the base columns. The anchor is not the blocker. Without the
anchor, PPO is free to walk away from the distilled behaviour and does,
trading line tracking for ground contact because that raises its objective.

A residual head has no anchor. It is new capacity whose only function is to
depend on the latent, so nothing opposes that dependence. Measured on the same
stack, the trained residual's action responds to its history at $3.35\,\%$ of
action scale, roughly $840$ times the anchored route. The residual formulation
is adopted on that measurement rather than on preference.

\subsection{How the Adaptation Encoder Is Trained}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-encoder}

The encoder $\phi$ and the head $g_\psi$ are trained together under the
reinforcement objective, not fitted offline to an auxiliary target and handed
to a controller afterwards. This is the concurrent scheme of Ji et
al.~\cite{ji2022concurrent}, and its consequence is that the latent is
control-relevant by construction rather than by hope: the only gradient
reaching $\phi$ is the one that improves the task.

The alternative was tried in this project and failed in a way worth recording,
because it is the failure the concurrent scheme is designed to avoid. An
encoder frozen after being fitted offline to a dynamics objective carried about
$0.4\,\%$ plant identity into the controller and scored $7.1\,\%$ worse than
simply supplying the mean context. Feeding a policy a near-noise estimate is
worse than feeding it a constant. A latent that is accurate against a dynamics
target is not thereby useful to a controller, and the offline route provides no
mechanism that would make it so.

This changes the status of the adaptation module relative to the earlier
design, and the change is one of kind rather than degree. The module is no
longer a standalone model with its own supervised validation score that can be
quoted independently of the controller; it has no meaning apart from the policy
it was trained with. Its quality is therefore reported behaviourally in
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, through the sensitivity of the action to the
history and through the closed-loop contrast against the frozen base, and not as
a latent reconstruction error.

\subsection{Consistency With the Plant Dynamics}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-consistency}

Two consistency requirements follow from the system model of
Chapter~\ref{chap:system}, and both are met by construction rather than by
observation.

The first is causality. Each history row carries observable quantities only:
joint positions, joint velocities, cylinder pressure differences and the
previous valve command. The privileged factor vector, the true latent, the
reference, future rows and any hidden simulator state are excluded, and the
estimate at step $t$ is formed without step $t$. Nothing enters the deployed
path that would be unavailable on the physical machine.

The second concerns what the pressure entries represent, and it is a
qualification rather than a guarantee. Under \ppoAAMFD{} the pressure
differences supplied to the encoder are recursive states of the frozen forward
model, not measurements of the plant. The encoder therefore observes the plant
through the forward model's representation of it, and its estimate is
consistent with the true dynamics only to the extent that the forward model is.
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:af-fd-sufficient} bounds that extent: strong on the
integrated extension channel, weak on velocity for two axes, and
operating-point dependent in valve authority. This is a real limit on the
interpretation of the latent and is carried into the discussion of
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} rather than resolved here.

What the stack does not claim is a physically named estimate. The residual is
trained to improve the task, and the quantity $\phi$ extracts is whatever
serves that objective; it is not constrained to correspond to seal condition,
compliance, supply pressure or any other named factor, and it is not reported
as though it were. Recovering named physical quantities would require an
explicit plant model and a different estimator, which is noted as future work
in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.

\subsection{What This Section Claims}
\label{sec:ctrl:af-claims}

The stack is complete and its interfaces are verified. The residual authority
$\alpha$ is a genuine tuned hyperparameter and is reported as one rather than
presented as a natural constant. The deployed value is $\alpha = 0.5$. At
$\alpha = 0.15$ the residual is not merely less effective but actively worse
than the base it corrects, at $\unit[10.041]{mm}$ cut-height error against the
base's $\unit[7.964]{mm}$. A bounded correction needs enough authority to
matter, and below that threshold it contributes noise rather than adaptation; no
value of $\alpha$ should therefore be read as incidental.
Whether the adaptation path contributes anything to closed-loop task
performance is a separate question, settled by the paired comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation} and not asserted here.

\section{IsaacLab Integration and Timing}
\label{sec:ctrl:integration}

Four rates run concurrently and Table~\ref{tab:sys:rates} lists them: PhysX
integrates at $\unit[120]{Hz}$, the policy acts at $\unit[60]{Hz}$ under a
decimation of two, the learned actuator models advance on a $\unit[20]{Hz}$
grid of $\unit[0.05]{s}$ model ticks, and the AAM updates on that same grid. No
single one of these is ``the control frequency'', and reporting a scalar rate
for this system would misdescribe every one of the three stacks.

The relationship between the $\unit[60]{Hz}$ and $\unit[20]{Hz}$ layers is
where integration errors hide, so it is fixed by construction rather than by
interpolation. A learned actuator model is evaluated on every third control
step and its predicted increment is held across the two intervening steps. The
action presented to the model is the action of the tick on which it fires, not
an average, and the target integrator accumulates in reference space so that a
held prediction cannot re-anchor to a sagging measured position. Chapter~3
records why that reference-space integration is mandatory: integrating against
the measured extension turns a static hold into a slow ratchet.

Order within a tick is fixed and asserted at run time. The history encoder
consumes the window ending at the previous tick and produces its estimate
before the actuator path runs, ahead of the inverse model in \ppoIDFD{} and of
the residual correction in \ppoAAMFD{}, so no quantity computed at the current
tick can influence its own estimate. History buffers are per-environment and cleared on
episode boundaries, which matters because the vectorised environments reset
asynchronously and a shared buffer would leak one episode into the next. Both
properties are checked rather than assumed: a runtime check replays a trace and
asserts bit-exact agreement between the online estimate and an offline causal
recomputation, and truncating the future of the input leaves the prefix of the
output unchanged. Checkpoint loading then compares the stored normalisation
statistics, feature contract and history length of every learned component
against what the environment expects, and aborts on a mismatch rather than
degrading silently.

\section{Constraints and Fair-Comparison Rules}
\label{sec:ctrl:constraints}

\subsection{Limits and Safeguards}

Authority is bounded at four independent places, and the redundancy is
deliberate because each bound protects against a different failure. The action
chain of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo} limits the rate of change of the command,
fades authority as cylinder travel headroom is consumed, and caps how far the
integrated target may lead the measured extension. The actuator path clips the
valve command to its per-axis limits. The environment terminates an episode on
the safety conditions of Section~\ref{sec:sys:evaluation-criteria} rather than
letting it accumulate reward in a state an operator would stop. And every
learned component falls back to a defined behaviour on a non-finite output.

Preflight runs before any training or evaluation and refuses to start on a
violated precondition. The check that does the most work asserts that the
commanded reference stays inside the measured actuator authority ceiling of
Section~\ref{sec:sys:capability}: the cylinders move the cutter laterally at
most $\unit[0.240]{m/s}$, so a path family demanding more is infeasible by
construction and no reward, critic or optimiser change could recover it.
Terrain is derived from that ceiling, and the preflight assertion keeps the two
from drifting apart.

\subsection{Matching Rules and What Each Comparison Carries}
\label{sec:ctrl:matching}

This thesis does not present a three-way ranking of the stacks, and the reason
is structural rather than presentational. Two distinct comparisons are made,
each with its own matching rule, and each carries one claim. Conflating them
would produce a table whose rows are not commensurable.

\paragraph{The Matched Pair: R Against IF}
The representation claim rests on \ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD, which agree on every
recorded training field and differ only in the actuator path. Held identical
across the pair are the verge task and its eleven path families, the terrain
generator and the held-out mesh set, the reward function of
Table~\ref{tab:ctrl:reward}, the action chain and its authority budget, the
eight acceptance gates and their aggregation to the worst mesh and worst path
family, episode length, actor and critic capacity, and the three-layer training
construction of distillation, critic warm-up and reference-anchored PPO. That
pair is a controlled single-variable comparison and is the only place an
across-stack number is read as such.

One departure is declared. \ppoROM's actuator is the command ROM, and the
command ROM plus PhysX \emph{is} the deployment plant
(Section~\ref{sec:models:rom-calibration}). Its tracking advantage over
\ppoIDFD{} is therefore not evidence that a reduced-order model approximates
the actuator better than an identified pair, because one of the two defines the
target. \ppoROM{} is a reference condition, not a competitor.

\paragraph{Why AF Is Not Ranked Against IF}
\ppoAAMFD{} does not share the action contract of the other two. Under the
direct-valve construction of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo-aamfd} the policy emits
the valve command itself, where \ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} both emit a bounded
desired cylinder increment that a downstream actuator path realises. The
quantity the policy chooses is not the same quantity in the two cases, so the
action chain that makes the other comparison fair does not extend to this one.
This is a declared property of the implementation rather than an observation
about results, and it is the reason no row of any table ranks \ppoAAMFD{}
against \ppoIDFD{} on task error. The adaptive claim is not weakened by this,
because it was never a claim about beating the non-adaptive stack.

\paragraph{The Paired Comparison: AF Against Its Own Frozen Base}
The adaptation claim is made within the adaptive stack, against the frozen base
policy that stack was built from, and the matching there is exact rather than
approximate. The arms share the frozen base policy, the residual architecture,
the trainable capacity of $53\,836$ parameters, the training budget and the
seed. Because the head of $g_\psi$ is zero-initialised and $\tanh(0)=0$, every
arm begins as bit-for-bit the same policy, verified at a maximum absolute
action difference of $0.0$. The single manipulated variable is whether the
residual may see the $(\text{state},\, \text{action})$ history it is
conditioned on.

That construction gives the comparison a property the across-stack contrasts
cannot have: the arms are paired environment by environment, so scenario
variation is differenced out rather than averaged over, and any separation is
attributable to online sensing rather than to added parameters, a different
optimiser trajectory or a different checkpoint. The result is reported on this
basis in Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}.

\paragraph{Fine-Tuning Budgets Must Be Matched or Reported}
Comparing a distilled baseline against a separately optimised adaptive policy
and attributing the difference to adaptation is not permitted, for the reason
established in Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:finding}: reward ascent moves a policy off
the acceptance criterion, so an unmatched budget is itself a treatment. The
paired construction above satisfies this rule by design rather than by
bookkeeping. The base policy is frozen and identical across the arms, no arm
receives a fine-tuning budget the others do not, and the only optimisation that
occurs is of the residual, which every arm trains under the same budget and
seed. The rule remains stated because it constrains any future stack added to
this comparison, not because the present one is at risk of violating it.
