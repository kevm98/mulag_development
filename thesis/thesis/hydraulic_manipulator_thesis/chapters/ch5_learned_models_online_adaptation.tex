\chapter{Learned Models and Online Adaptation}
\label{chap:models}
\raggedbottom

Chapter~\ref{chap:data} established how the datasets were generated and why
both closed-loop operation and open-loop valve excitation were required. This
chapter develops the learned representations that consume them: the
inverse-dynamics (ID) and forward-dynamics (FD) networks of the \ppoIDFD{}
actuator path, the command-conditioned reduced-order model (ROM) that replaces
them in \ppoROM, and the system-dynamics surrogate, privileged factor encoder
and Arm Adaptation Module (AAM) that together carry the adaptation argument.

Two commitments organise the chapter. The first is that the surrogate is kept
strictly separate from the deployed actuator: the system-dynamics model is an
instrument for establishing whether a compact dynamics latent exists and is
useful, never a component of any control stack. The second is that no model is
accepted on a supervised score, because a recurring result of this work is that
such scores can be not merely optimistic but uninformative: a forward model
reached a one-step $R^2$ near $0.999$ while having learned almost nothing about
valve authority (Section~\ref{sec:models:fd}). Every model is therefore
presented with its contract, training objective, and the offline evidence that
accepted or refused it. All models operate on cylinder-space signals; the
mapping to task space belongs to Chapter~\ref{chap:system}, the control
integration to Chapter~\ref{chap:control}, and deployment-level results to
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Modelling Overview and Common Notation}
\label{sec:models:overview}

All learned models act on the four hydraulic cylinders of the MULAG arm,
indexed $i \in \{1,\dots,4\}$ in the fixed order slew cylinder
(\emph{Drehzapfen}), boom cylinder~I (\emph{Ausleger~I}), boom cylinder~II
(\emph{Ausleger~II}) and head pivot cylinder (\emph{Messerkopf-Schwenk}). The
passive fifth joint is not actuated and enters no model input. At the
learned-model sample time $T = \unit[0.05]{s}$ the discrete index $k$ refers to
the cylinder extension $\bm{x}_k$, the extension velocity $\dot{\bm{x}}_k$, the
chamber pressure difference $\Delta\bm{p}_k$ and the logged rod force
$\bm{F}_k$, each in $\mathbb{R}^4$. The valve command
$\bm{u}_k \in [-1,1]^4$ is the normalised spool command of the four
proportional valves. The desired cylinder command produced by the task
controller is the pair
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:command}
  \bm{c}_k = \bigl[\Delta\bm{x}^{*}_k,\; \Delta\dot{\bm{x}}^{*}_k\bigr]
  \in \mathbb{R}^{8},
\end{equation}
%
the extension and extension-velocity increments to be realised over the next
sample. Predictions carry a hat, and $\Delta(\cdot)_k = (\cdot)_{k+1} -
(\cdot)_k$ denotes a within-run one-step increment throughout.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/models_overview}
  \caption{Common interface of the two alternative actuator paths. Both
  receive the desired cylinder command and measured hydraulic state, and both
  produce the same bounded PhysX cylinder target. The \ppoIDFD{} path composes
  the inverse and forward models; the \ppoROM{} path uses one
  command-conditioned reduced-order model.}
  \label{fig:models:overview}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:models:overview} places the two actuator paths side by side. In
\ppoIDFD, the ID network maps the hydraulic state and a desired increment to a
valve command and the FD network maps a valve command and the state to the
predicted increment, so their composition
$f_{\mathrm{FD}} \circ f_{\mathrm{ID}}$ predicts what a commanded increment
will do. In \ppoROM, one command-conditioned ROM replaces that composition: it
receives the same $\bm{c}_k$, predicts the valve internally, and propagates
pressure, velocity and extension through explicit physical updates. Both paths
terminate in the same bounded PhysX cylinder target inside
IsaacLab~\cite{mittal2023orbit} and are driven by the same policy under the
same action contract~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

Three conventions apply to every model. All dynamic targets are within-run
increments rather than absolute states, which removes the dominant static
component and makes the persistence solution $\Delta\hat{\bm{x}} = \bm{0}$ an
explicit, testable baseline rather than a hidden local minimum. Every
checkpoint stores and applies the normalisation statistics of its own training
data, never those of another checkpoint, because mixing them silently
invalidates any comparison. And inputs at step $k$ contain only quantities
available at step $k$, including the previously applied command, with targets
the measured transition to step $k+1$.

One point about the toolchain applies to every model in this chapter. Simscape
is a \emph{data source} and nothing else: it generates the identification
campaigns of Chapter~\ref{chap:data}, which are exported, audited and frozen as
plain arrays. Every learned model reported here is implemented and trained in
PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} outside the MATLAB environment, against those
exported datasets. No model is fitted inside Simscape, no optimisation runs
through the Simscape solver, and no Simscape component is present at deployment,
where the actuator is one of the learned models of this chapter driving PhysX.

Two dataset families from Chapter~\ref{chap:data} are consumed. The
\emph{closed-loop} family is the aligned Simscape
export~\cite{mathworks2024simscape} used for supervised ID and FD training and,
after exact alignment, for the command-ROM dataset: $452\,400$ transitions at
$T=\unit[0.05]{s}$ from $1\,040$ runs, split by complete runs into $936$
training, $52$ validation and $52$ test runs. The \emph{open-loop} family is
the strict valve-excitation export used to repair the FD model: four $64$-s
runs at $\unit[0.01]{s}$ in which the same rank-4 multi-input excitation is
applied to four distinct plant draws.

\section{Inverse Dynamics Model}
\label{sec:models:id}

The ID network answers the question the low-level controller asks: given the
current hydraulic state, which valve command realises a desired cylinder
increment? Its contract is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:id-contract}
  \Delta\bm{u}_k = f_{\mathrm{ID}}
  \bigl(\bm{x}_k,\, \dot{\bm{x}}_k,\, \Delta\bm{p}_k,\, \bm{F}_k,\,
        \Delta\bm{x}^{*}_k,\, \Delta\dot{\bm{x}}^{*}_k,\, \bm{u}_{k-1}\bigr)
  \in \mathbb{R}^4 ,
\end{equation}
%
a 28-dimensional input mapped to a valve-command \emph{change}. The deployed
command is the accumulated
$\bm{u}_k = \operatorname{clip}(\bm{u}_{k-1} + \Delta\bm{u}_k,\,
-\bm{u}_{\max},\, \bm{u}_{\max})$ with per-axis limits
$\bm{u}_{\max} = [0.95,\, 0.95,\, 0.8,\, 0.8]$ stored in the checkpoint.
Predicting the change rather than the absolute command conditions the problem
on the previous operating point of the valve, which matters because
proportional valves are direction- and history-dependent around their dead zone
(Section~\ref{sec:fund:actuation-chain}). The desired increments in the
training data are the \emph{achieved} transitions of the logged runs, used as a
proxy because no independent cylinder-space reference was logged.

The network is a multilayer perceptron with an input projection to width 512
followed by four residual blocks~\cite{he2016deep} with SiLU
activations~\cite{elfwing2018sigmoid}, layer normalisation~\cite{ba2016layer}
and dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}, implemented in
PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}. Training
minimised the smooth-$L_1$ loss~\cite{huber1964robust} on normalised targets
using Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam}, with samples in valve saturation up-weighted
so the rare saturated regime is not ignored.

Figure~\ref{fig:models:id-architecture} separates the three input groups and
shows the four residual blocks explicitly. The network predicts a four-axis
valve-command increment, which is accumulated and clipped before use.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[page=1,width=\textwidth]{images/ch5_network_architectures}
  \caption{Inverse-model architecture. Current hydraulic state (16), desired
  motion (8) and the previous valve command (4) form the 28 inputs. A
  512-wide, four-block residual MLP predicts $\Delta\bm{u}_k$, which is
  accumulated and clipped per axis to form the bounded valve command.}
  \label{fig:models:id-architecture}
\end{figure}

The supervised result was, on its face, excellent: per-valve validation $R^2$
between $0.9973$ and $0.9996$. That number means the network reproduces the
logged valve commands from the logged states and achieved transitions. It does
not mean the composite map has authority over the plant, because the achieved
transitions in closed-loop data are themselves produced by the logged commands,
so a model can score highly by exploiting that correlation without ever being
asked to \emph{cause} a transition absent from the data
(Section~\ref{sec:fund:identifiability}).

\subsection{Paired Control Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:models:id-paired}

The deficiency became measurable when the two networks were composed. For
control, the relevant object is the \emph{paired differential response}, the
difference between what the FD predicts under the ID's command for the actual
target and under its command for a zero target,
%
\begin{align}
  \bm{u}^{\mathrm{tgt}}_k &= f_{\mathrm{ID}}(\cdot,\, \bm{c}_k,\, \bm{u}_{k-1}),
  \qquad
  \bm{u}^{0}_k = f_{\mathrm{ID}}(\cdot,\, \bm{0},\, \bm{u}_{k-1}), \notag \\
  \Delta\hat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{pair}}_k &=
    f_{\mathrm{FD}}\bigl(\bm{u}^{\mathrm{tgt}}_k, \cdot\bigr)
  - f_{\mathrm{FD}}\bigl(\bm{u}^{0}_k, \cdot\bigr).
  \label{eq:models:paired}
\end{align}
%
Before fine-tuning this quantity did not track the requested increment at all:
the normalised control error of the composite was $1.00$, exactly the score of
predicting nothing, with per-axis regression slopes of at most $0.004$
(Table~\ref{tab:models:paired}). Both networks were therefore fine-tuned
jointly on
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctl}} = \| \Delta\hat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{pair}}
- \Delta\bm{x}^{*} \|^2_{\mathrm{norm}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{ID}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ID}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{FD}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FD}}$,
retaining the supervised terms to limit regression from the original data
contracts. Crucially, the target blocks were shuffled across valid states
during this stage, so the networks could no longer succeed by reconstructing
the logged trajectory and had to respond to targets the logged controller never
issued in that state. After 30 epochs the composite control error fell to
$0.109$ and all four axes reached slopes above $0.8$ and correlations above
$0.92$. The resulting checkpoint is the ID model deployed, frozen, in every
\ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMIDFD{} experiment.

\subsection{Transfer of the Frozen Inverse Model}
\label{sec:models:id-trust}

The adaptation architecture of Sections~\ref{sec:models:aam}
and~\ref{sec:models:adapter} freezes this model and places a small correction
around it. That design is only defensible if the frozen model is itself robust
across the plant family, so the question was tested directly rather than
assumed. The frozen ID was evaluated on a separate $64$-run IsaacLab collection, made
through the same deployment plant and spanning the same factor manifest
(Section~\ref{sec:data:factors}): $2$ nominal, $16$ hydraulic-only, $12$
mechanical-only and $34$ combined, the last being that collection's own $14$
training, $4$ validation and $16$ unseen runs. It is not a subset of the
$118$-run adaptation collection and shares no run with it. All $64$ are held
out in the strict sense, because the ID is trained on Simscape exports alone
and has never seen an IsaacLab transition, so the entire collection lies
outside its training distribution by construction; the internal split is named
only to show the result does not rest on one part of it. Runs were
teacher-forced at each ROM tick and scored on the quantity the model actually
produces, the valve command.

Table~\ref{tab:models:id-trust} reports the outcome. Against a recorded valve
signal of root-mean-square magnitude $0.4350$, the ID reproduces the command
with an error of $0.1739$, so it accounts for roughly $84\,\%$ of the
valve-command variance. More importantly for the architecture, that accuracy is
almost flat across domains: the spread between the best and worst domain mean
is a factor of $1.09$, and the ordering does not follow plant severity, since
the randomised-hydraulics domain scores better than the nominal one. Robustness
here rests on all $64$ runs rather than on a ratio to the nominal domain, which
contributes only two runs and is far too thin to anchor one.

Three limitations qualify that conclusion and are carried forward rather than
buried. First, judged as a one-step state predictor rather than as an inverse
model, the ID is only marginally better than a trivial baseline: the mean
extension error of $\unit[1.002]{mm}$ compares with $\unit[1.143]{mm}$ for a
zero-order hold over the same $\unit[50]{ms}$ step, an improvement of $12\,\%$,
and on velocity the zero-order hold is in fact $8\,\%$ better. This is not a
contradiction but a statement about which metric is meaningful for an inverse
model, and both are reported so the reader can judge. Second, recursive
open-loop rollout of the composite is unstable in $48$ of the $64$ runs.
Divergence is bimodal and is discriminated by valve saturation, whose mean
occupancy is $0.989$ in diverging runs against $0.223$ in stable ones, and not
by domain; it is a property of open-loop integration into the stroke limit and
does not bear on the one-step, closed-loop use the controller makes of the
model. Third, supplying the measured pressure difference instead of the
training mean makes the model \emph{worse}, $\unit[1.907]{mm}$ against
$\unit[1.311]{mm}$, so the mean-pressure deployment contract is a deliberate
choice rather than an omission.

\section{Forward Dynamics Model}
\label{sec:models:fd}

The FD network predicts the one-step cylinder response to a valve command,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:fd-contract}
  \bigl[\Delta\hat{\bm{x}}_k,\; \Delta\hat{\dot{\bm{x}}}_k,\;
        \Delta\widehat{\Delta\bm{p}}_k\bigr]
  = f_{\mathrm{FD}}\bigl(\bm{u}_k,\, \bm{x}_k,\, \dot{\bm{x}}_k,\,
    \Delta\bm{p}_k,\, \bm{F}_k\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{12},
\end{equation}
%
using the same residual trunk as the ID network. Supervised pretraining on the
closed-loop export, with an auxiliary recursive rollout
loss~\cite{nagabandi2018neural}, reached grouped one-step $R^2$ near $0.999$
for the extension increments.

Figure~\ref{fig:models:fd-architecture} shows the forward model separately
because its input and output contracts differ from the inverse model despite
the shared residual trunk.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[page=2,width=\textwidth]{images/ch5_network_architectures}
  \caption{Forward-model architecture. The applied valve command and cylinder
  state condition a one-step hydraulic transition through a 512-wide,
  four-block residual MLP. Its 12 outputs are extension, velocity and pressure
  increments. Extension is the integrated controller output; velocity and
  pressure are diagnostic outputs.}

\subsection{Assessment of the Supervised Score}
\label{sec:models:fd-identifiability}

That score was misleading in a way worth stating precisely, because it is the
clearest instance of the chapter's organising caution. A model intended to give
a policy gradient information about actuation must respond to the \emph{valve},
not merely to the state history. Zeroing the valve input of the pretrained FD
barely changed its long-horizon rollout error, a valve-zero error ratio of
about $0.94$ at a $\unit[0.5]{s}$ horizon: removing the input the model exists
to explain made it very slightly \emph{better}. The network had learned to
extrapolate the smooth kinematics of closed-loop operation, in effect a
sophisticated persistence model, because in feedback data the controller makes
command and state history nearly redundant. Ablations on the source exports
confirmed that the valve was not causally identifiable from the closed-loop
distribution at all, so no amount of retraining on that distribution could
repair it. The data had to change.

The repair dataset is the strict open-loop excitation export. All four valves
receive a rank-4 multi-input sequence of long, full-authority plateaus, exciting
one cylinder at a time in the first four segments and all four simultaneously in
the last, applied to four distinct plant draws. Validation established the
properties the closed-loop export lacked: all four state trajectories are
distinct, valve history alone predicts the pressure response with $R^2 \approx
0.80$--$0.85$, the valve regressors have variance-inflation factors of only
$7$--$10$, and pressure-relief saturation occupies $9$--$22\,\%$ of samples, so
the saturated regime is represented but not dominant. An earlier excitation
export was rejected outright because half its runs duplicated state
trajectories and the valve was again not identifiable, a reminder that
excitation data must itself be audited rather than assumed
informative~\cite{isermann2011identification}.

Starting from the paired-control checkpoint, strict retraining minimised
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:fd-strict-loss}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FD}} =
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Huber}}^{\mathrm{strict}}
    + 0.25\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Huber}}^{\mathrm{MAT}}
    + 2.0\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{roll}}^{(10)}
    + 10.0\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{kin}},
\end{equation}
%
combining the one-step loss on the strict data, a replay term on the original
closed-loop data, a $\unit[0.5]{s}$ recursive rollout loss, and a kinematic
consistency term enforcing the trapezoidal identity
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:trapezoid}
  \Delta\bm{x}_k = T\,\dot{\bm{x}}_k
  + \tfrac{1}{2}\, T\, \Delta\dot{\bm{x}}_k ,
\end{equation}
%
which holds in the measured strict data with $R^2 = 0.994$--$0.9997$ and ties
the predicted extension and velocity increments to each other. One initially
attractive regulariser was evaluated and rejected: anchoring the model to a
constant valve Jacobian. Measured valve authority varies by a factor of
$15$--$75$ across pressure quartiles and can change sign, so pinning a single
global gain produced a pleasing mean response while collapsing one-step
accuracy. This is consistent with the strongly operating-point-dependent
valve--flow--pressure characteristics of hydraulic servo
systems~\cite{merritt1967hydraulic} and motivates the pressure-dependent
structure adopted in the ROM.

Strict retraining reduces the $\unit[0.5]{s}$ extension rollout RMSE from
$\unit[75.2]{mm}$ to
$\unit[12.4]{mm}$ and the $\unit[1.0]{s}$ RMSE from $\unit[157.9]{mm}$ to
$\unit[27.3]{mm}$, and, decisively, raises the valve-zero error ratio from
$0.94$ to $1.81$: removing the valve input now roughly doubles the rollout
error, which is the sensitivity a control-relevant forward model must exhibit.
Per-axis one-step extension $R^2$ improved from the range $[-5.7,\,0.60]$ to
$[0.76,\,0.98]$. Two limitations remain. At horizons below
$\unit[0.25]{s}$ the zero-order hold is still competitive, simply because
extension does not move far in $\unit[50]{ms}$; the learned model earns its
value at the horizons over which a target must be committed. And the
velocity-increment outputs remain poorly predicted on two axes, dominated by
relief transients a memoryless $\unit[50]{ms}$ model cannot resolve. The
deployed controller therefore integrates only $\Delta\hat{\bm{x}}$, the
accurately modelled and directly validated quantity, and treats the velocity
output as diagnostic. That restriction becomes important again in
Section~\ref{sec:models:latent}.

\section{Reduced-Order Model}
\label{sec:models:rom}

The \ppoIDFD{} path needed three repair stages before its composition had
authority. The \ppoROM{} path instead replaces the composition with a single
command-conditioned reduced-order model trained end to end on the quantity the
controller consumes. Two decisions distinguish it from a generic sequence
model. It is \emph{grey-box}: pressure, force, velocity and extension are
propagated through explicit physical updates with trainable effective
parameters, and neural components supply only what the physics cannot, namely
the valve realisation, the unmodelled load and bounded
residuals~\cite{karniadakis2021physics,lutter2019deep}. Encoding the
orifice-flow and continuity structure~\cite{merritt1967hydraulic} makes the
pressure dependence of valve authority architectural rather than something to
be rediscovered from data. It is also \emph{recurrent}: a GRU
encoder~\cite{cho2014learning} over $\unit[0.25]{s}$ of history replaces the
memoryless state input of the FD, in the spirit of learned actuator
networks~\cite{hwangbo2019learning}, because hydraulic response depends on
recent pressure and command history that a single sample cannot represent. The
model consumes six samples of a 24-dimensional vector comprising the cylinder
state, the \emph{previously predicted} valve command fed back as recurrent
state, and the current desired command; the actual valve appears only as a
training target for the internal valve head, and at runtime no valve signal
enters the model.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[page=3,width=\textwidth]{images/ch5_network_architectures}
  \caption{Command-conditioned ROM. A recurrent neural proposal encodes a
  six-step history. Its actuation, bounded-residual and load heads condition
  the explicit hydraulic update: orifice flow, pressure, force, velocity and
  position. The explicit chain constrains the predicted transition.}
  \label{fig:models:rom-structure}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:models:rom-structure} shows the computation. The encoder latent
and the command feed an actuation head whose output passes through a learnable
smooth dead-zone element with per-cylinder trainable width and asymmetric
gains, reflecting the dead band and direction-dependent gain of proportional
valves. Model-based designs treat the same nonlinearity by inverting an
identified dead band analytically~\cite{yang2026stateconstrained}; training its
parameters per cylinder instead removes the need for a separate identification
experiment. The resulting internal actuation $\hat{a}_i \in [-1,1]$ drives an
orifice-flow and continuity update per cylinder,
%
\begin{align}
  q_i &= \hat{a}_i\, k_{q,i}\,
    \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(p_{s,i} - \operatorname{sgn}(\hat{a}_i)\,
    \Delta p_i\bigr)_{+}}, \notag \\
  \Delta \hat{p}_{i,k+1} - \Delta p_{i,k} &=
    \frac{4\beta_i}{V_i(x_i)}
    \bigl(q_i - A_i \dot{x}_i - k_{\ell,i}\, \Delta p_i\bigr)\, T
    \;+\; r_{p,i},
  \label{eq:models:rom-pressure}
\end{align}
%
with effective bulk modulus $\beta_i$, supply pressure $p_{s,i}$, flow gain
$k_{q,i}$, leakage coefficient $k_{\ell,i}$, piston area $A_i$ and
extension-dependent chamber volume $V_i(x_i)$; the smoothed pressure margin
$(\cdot)_+$ keeps the square root differentiable near the supply limit.
Pressure then produces force and force produces motion,
%
\begin{align}
  \hat{F}_i &= \Delta\hat{p}_{i,k+1} A_i + r_{F,i}, \qquad
  \ddot{x}_i = \frac{\hat{F}_i - c_{v,i}\,\dot{x}_i - \ell_i(\bm{h}_k)}{m_i},
  \notag \\
  \dot{\hat{x}}_{i,k+1} &= \dot{x}_{i,k}
    + \operatorname{sat}\bigl(T\,\ddot{x}_i + r_{v,i}\bigr), \qquad
  \hat{x}_{i,k+1} = x_{i,k}
    + \tfrac{T}{2}\bigl(\dot{x}_{i,k} + \dot{\hat{x}}_{i,k+1}\bigr)
    + r_{x,i},
  \label{eq:models:rom-motion}
\end{align}
%
where $c_{v,i}$ is a viscous friction coefficient, $m_i$ an effective modal mass
and $\ell_i(\bm{h}_k)$ a neural load head capturing the configuration-dependent
gravitational and coupling load. All physical parameters are trained in
log-space with hard clamps to plausible ranges, so they adapt to the data
without leaving their meaning behind. The neural residuals are $\tanh$-bounded
to $10\,\%$ of the pressure step limit, $10\,\%$ of the velocity step limit and
$\unit[1]{cm}$ respectively: large enough to absorb model error, too small to
let the network bypass the physics. This bounded-residual layering is the
central robustness argument of the grey-box design, because even a badly
trained residual cannot produce an unphysical state jump.

Training follows a four-stage rollout curriculum with horizons
$H \in \{1, 5, 20, 50\}$, each stage optimising weighted one-step losses plus a
recursive rollout loss at its horizon, with scheduled sampling ramping from
teacher-forced to $75\,\%$ self-fed~\cite{bengio2015scheduled}. The residual
heads are penalised explicitly so that explanatory burden stays on the physical
terms, and the logged force channel receives zero supervised weight. Excluding
it is a design choice: at deployment the PhysX plant generates rod force
internally, so the actuator model is not required to reproduce it. The logged
channel also fails a piston-area consistency audit on this export, and
supervising a head on a label that violates the model's own structure would
corrupt the pressure chain.

Three findings from the offline acceptance report
(Figure~\ref{fig:models:rom-validation}) matter for the control experiments.
The model has \emph{command authority}: with the true command, mean one-step
$R^2$ is $0.998$ for the internal valve, $0.64$ for velocity and $0.97$ for
extension, while zeroing or shuffling the command collapses the velocity score
below zero and extension to about $0.5$; the $4\times4$ Jacobian of predicted
extension increment with respect to the commanded increment is diagonally
dominant with condition number $2.14$. It is \emph{stable} in rollout, with
terminal extension $R^2$ of $0.998$, $0.998$, $0.84$ and $0.91$ per cylinder at
$\unit[5]{s}$, so \ppoROM{} training rollouts do not ride on a diverging model.
The pressure channel is the exception: one-step pressure-increment $R^2$ is only
$0.06$--$0.09$, so the internal pressure state is best understood as a latent
that supports the velocity chain rather than as a calibrated pressure
estimator. That property recurs in Section~\ref{sec:models:latent} and turns
out to matter there.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Calibration Against the Deployment Plant}
\label{sec:models:rom-calibration}

Everything above identifies the ROM against Simscape, but at runtime the model
does not drive Simscape. It drives a PhysX articulation whose contact
behaviour, solver and effective compliance are its own, and a small systematic
difference between the extension the ROM commits to and the extension PhysX
realises is invisible from Simscape data by construction. The promoted
checkpoint therefore adds a bounded, command-gated correction to the extension
update,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:rom-correction}
  \hat{x}_{i,k+1} \leftarrow \hat{x}_{i,k+1} + \delta_i(\bm{h}_k, \bm{c}_k),
  \qquad |\delta_i| \leq \unit[3]{mm},
  \qquad \delta_i\big|_{\bm{c}_k = \bm{0}} = 0,
\end{equation}
%
trained on $4\,084$ closed-loop transitions collected in IsaacLab on the five
plant-reachable reference shapes, with the Simscape dataset retained as a
replay term. Every other parameter, the whole flow, continuity, force and
integrator chain, is frozen during this stage, so the identified hydraulics are
preserved and only a deployment offset is placed on top of them. The gating
keeps the stationary hold contract intact: at zero command the correction is
exactly zero, so a commanded hold remains a hold.

This is why the command ROM, and not the ID--FD pair, serves as the deployment
plant of Section~\ref{sec:sys:rom-is-plant}. It is also a genuine asymmetry
between the two actuator paths, since the ROM is the one model that has seen
the plant it is deployed in, and it must be restated whenever their tracking
numbers are compared in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}. The command ROM is
frozen for the final comparison and conditioned on no latent; the adaptation
mechanism below acts on the inverse model of the \ppoIDFD{} path and leaves
this model untouched.

\section{The Privileged Latent and Its Limits}
\label{sec:models:latent}

The three actuator models above are alternatives for the same job. The
system-dynamics model is not: it is an \emph{instrument}, a learned
approximation of the deployment plant itself, the command ROM followed by
PhysX, trained from scratch on the IsaacLab collection of
Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab} and never used as the actuator of any stack.
Its purpose is to answer whether a compact factor-dependent context exists at
all. If it does not, there is nothing for an online estimator to recover and
the adaptation argument stops.

The contract is a $\unit[50]{ms}$ transition. From the observable state, the
applied action and a causal history the model predicts the next increment of
the twelve-dimensional hydraulic state, with joint position and velocity as
context. The action is the pair of consecutive valve commands
$[\bm{u}_{k-1}, \bm{u}_k]$, which is what the command ROM itself consumes at a
tick, so the model is asked the same question the plant is. It carries a belief
module over a six-tick history, because a single sample cannot summarise the
pressure state that drives the next transition, and it is trained with a
five-transition unroll so that it is optimised for recursive use. The matched
control $SD_0$ is identical except that no latent is supplied.

Figure~\ref{fig:models:sd-architecture} shows the two conditioning paths and
the transition model. The privileged factor encoder is used during training;
the belief GRU uses only causal state-action history.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[page=4,width=\textwidth]{images/ch5_network_architectures}
  \caption{System-dynamics surrogate. The transition model combines a
  two-dimensional static plant context and a 16-dimensional causal dynamic
  belief with the hydraulic state, consecutive valve commands and joint
  context. A three-layer, 256-wide MLP predicts the transition; training uses
  a five-step unroll.}
  \label{fig:models:sd-architecture}

The privileged encoder $\bm{z} = \mu(\bm{e})$ compresses the six-dimensional
physical factor vector of Section~\ref{sec:data:factors} into a two-dimensional
latent. It is trained \emph{jointly} with the latent-conditioned model $SD_z$,
so the representation is shaped by what helps predict rollout rather than by a
reconstruction objective. At a horizon of $20$ ticks, one second of plant time,
$SD_z$ is evaluated with the true latent against five controls constructed from
the training pool alone: zero, shuffled, random, the mean encoded latent, and
the nominal factor setting. The test is paired per run and reported with
bootstrap confidence intervals on validation and on the sixteen reserved test
runs separately.

On the pooled state error the true latent wins on both pools. Validation
rollout error falls by $7.20\times10^{5}$ with a $95\,\%$ interval of
$[-1.03\times10^{6},\, -4.30\times10^{5}]$ over $20$ runs, and the reserved
test runs fall by $4.41\times10^{5}$, interval
$[-7.61\times10^{5},\, -1.74\times10^{5}]$ over $16$ runs. Neither interval
crosses zero.

That result, taken alone, would overstate the case, and the per-channel
decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:models:latent-channels} is the reason. The
pooled statistic weights extension, velocity and pressure equally in normalised
units, but the raw pressure magnitudes are around six orders larger, so the
pooled figure is effectively a pressure statistic. Decomposed by channel, the
advantage of the true latent lives entirely in pressure. On extension and
velocity, the two channels the deployed controller actually integrates
(Section~\ref{sec:models:fd}), the paired intervals straddle zero on both
pools, and the velocity mean difference even favours the no-latent control.

What this establishes must therefore be stated narrowly, and the narrowness is
specific to the comparison. A two-dimensional privileged context does carry
information about held-out hydraulic rollout that the state and action do not,
which justifies freezing the representation and attempting online inference. The
confinement to pressure is a property of \emph{this} contrast, between a
latent-conditioned model and a separately trained no-latent one, which differ in
their weights as well as in their input. Substituting latents within a single
model isolates the latent alone, and under that comparison extension and
velocity do respond (Section~\ref{sec:eval:predictive-utility}). Both readings
are reported because they answer different questions. It is not evidence that the AAM works, because it
uses the true $\bm{z}$ rather than an estimate, and it is not evidence of any
control improvement. Most importantly, the information it carries is about the
channel the ROM already predicts worst and the controller deliberately does not
integrate. Section~\ref{sec:models:adapter} returns to this, because it is the
best available explanation for what the closed-loop experiments later find.

The representation is then frozen. The encoder, $SD_z$, the belief module, the
normalisers, the input mask, the split and the rollout protocol are written to
a frozen store with a recorded protocol stamp, and everything downstream reads
that copy.

\section{Arm Adaptation Module}
\label{sec:models:aam}

The AAM estimates the latent from history that exists at deployment,
$\hat{\bm{z}} = \mathrm{AAM}(\bm{H}_t)$. Its input row is eighteen-dimensional,
comprising five joint positions, five joint velocities, four cylinder pressure
differences and the four previous valve commands. Twenty consecutive
$\unit[50]{ms}$ rows form the window, so the estimator reads $\unit[1.0]{s}$ of
past and $360$ inputs in total, and the promoted architecture is a causal
one-dimensional convolutional network of width $64$ and depth $4$ over that
window.

The pressure entry deserves a precise description, because it carries most of
the hydraulic factor information and it is not a measurement. The four pressure
differences are the learned actuator's own recursive pressure state, produced by
the command ROM in \ppoROM{} and by the frozen forward model in \ppoAAMIDFD,
and they are available at every deployment step because the actuator model that
generates them runs inside the deployed loop. Nothing privileged enters: the
factor vector, the true latent, the desired reference, future rows and the
model's hidden recurrent state are excluded by construction, and the previous
rather than the current valve command is used so that the row is causal at the
instant the estimate is needed. History length was selected from a predeclared
set of $5$, $10$, $20$ and $40$ ticks on validation runs only, taking the
shortest history that remains statistically comparable with the best downstream
rollout. The selected checkpoint has a validation latent RMSE of $0.802$ and a
reserved-test RMSE of $0.734$, a substantial reduction against a
constant-prediction baseline on runs the estimator never saw.

Deployment behaviour is fixed by the online runtime. Inference runs every three
control steps, placing it on the $\unit[20]{Hz}$ model-tick grid inside a
$\unit[60]{Hz}$ control loop, and the first valid estimate appears only after
twenty complete rows have accumulated; until then the adapter is bypassed and
the frozen inverse model's action is returned unchanged. A past-only rolling
mean over five predictions smooths the output, adding $\unit[250]{ms}$ of lag.
That smoother is causal by construction, unlike an earlier variant that scored
the estimator using a full-run mean of held-out predictions, which is not a
deployable estimator because it uses the whole run to predict any instant
within it. Every number reported here comes from the corrected causal score,
and keeping the superseded verdict visible and labelled is deliberate: the
failure mode is easy to reintroduce.

Figure~\ref{fig:models:aam-architecture} shows the causal convolutional stack
and the bounded adapter that consumes its estimate. The upper part is the AAM;
the lower part shows where adaptation enters the frozen inverse-model path.

\subsection{Information Content of the Estimated Latent}
\label{sec:models:aam-content}

A two-dimensional latent cannot represent a six-dimensional factor vector, and
it is worth measuring what it does represent rather than assuming a distributed
code. Linearly decoding the six physical factors from the per-run mean estimate
gives $R^2 = 0.956$ for the seal-condition factor and little else: $0.448$ for
compliance, $0.114$ for flow, $0.081$ for supply pressure, $0.056$ for the
centre-of-mass offset and $-0.008$ for the mass scale. The deployed estimator is,
to a good approximation, a seal-condition fingerprint. It is also
\emph{static}: the estimate is stable within a run, which the temporal
consistency term encourages, but it does not refine as evidence accumulates.

Two consequences follow. The first is that no latent dimension is named after a
physical parameter anywhere in this thesis on the basis of a scatter plot; the
one relationship asserted here is the one that was measured. The second is that
this is a recognised failure mode rather than an idiosyncrasy of this system:
history-based adapters that compress many factors into a single low-dimensional
latent are known to lose robustness precisely where the factors act through
different channels~\cite{liu2025frma}. The original rapid-motor-adaptation
argument treats the low-dimensional projection as a virtue, on the grounds that
parameters which covary with identical effects on observables need not be
separated and perfect system identification is not
required~\cite{kumar2021rma}. That defence holds only when the surviving
direction is the one the controller needs. Here the surviving direction is seal
condition, which acts through pressure, and Section~\ref{sec:models:latent} has
already shown that pressure is the channel the controller does not integrate.
Work that instead estimates named physical quantities in a belief filter,
including seal leakage and valve gain degradation, retains that interpretability
by construction and pays for it with an explicit plant
model~\cite{hu2026bayesian}.

\section{Latent-Conditioned Inverse-Model Adapter}
\label{sec:models:adapter}

The adapter is where adaptation reaches the plant. The frozen inverse model
produces a base command $\bm{u}_{\mathrm{base}} = \mathrm{ID}(\bm{s},\bm{r})$
and the adapter adds a small bounded residual conditioned on the estimated
latent,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:models:adapter}
  \bm{u} = \operatorname{clip}\bigl(
    \bm{u}_{\mathrm{base}} + g_\psi(\bm{s}, \bm{r}, \bm{u}_{\mathrm{base}},
    \hat{\bm{z}})\bigr).
\end{equation}
%
The adapter is trained on the recorded valve command: its objective is the
mean squared error between the clipped sum $\bm{u}_{\mathrm{base}} +
g_\psi(\cdot)$ and the valve command recorded at the exact ROM tick, with the
residual bounded to $0.15$. The inverse model, the forward model and the policy
stay frozen \emph{while the adapter is trained}. The deployed policy of \ppoAAMIDFD{} is itself conditioned on the estimated latent and is trained separately, so the stack does not reduce to \ppoIDFD{} when the residual is disabled. The residual is
bounded by construction and scaled by a declared factor, so a warm-up period or
an invalid latent zeroes the correction and restores the robust actuator
path rather than producing a third, untested behaviour. The latent-conditioned
policy is unchanged by that fallback, so the resulting system is \ppoIDFD{} in
its actuator path but not in its actor; recovering \ppoIDFD{} exactly requires
switching in the $66$-input baseline policy as well. What the fallback buys is a
single-variable ablation \emph{within} the adaptive checkpoint, which is the
controlled comparison used in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\subsection{Open-Loop Evidence}

One property of the adapter bounds every number in this subsection and is
stated before them. The residual is capped at $0.15$ and is added to a frozen
inverse model that already reproduces roughly $84\,\%$ of the valve-command
variance (Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}). The room available to the latent
on this path is therefore small by construction, and effects of a few tenths of
a per cent are what the design permits rather than what the latent is worth. The
predictive comparison of Section~\ref{sec:eval:predictive-utility}, where the
latent conditions a model with no such ceiling, is the better measure of its
information content.

Isolated from the control loop, the adapter does benefit from the latent, and
the effect is small but consistent. Three information-matched arms were trained
under identical conditions, differing only in what occupies the latent input:
zeroed, the AAM estimate, and the privileged true latent. Across five seeds the
privileged latent improves validation loss over the zeroed arm in $5$ of $5$
seeds, with a mean paired improvement of $0.0037$, and the AAM estimate does so
in $5$ of $5$ seeds with a mean paired improvement of $0.0118$. The differences
amount to a few tenths of a percent, so the sign, reproduced across every seed
in a paired design, is the result rather than the magnitude.

A companion experiment tested the natural hypothesis that the latent should
help more once the observation is made insufficient, by masking the pressure and
force features so that plant identity can no longer be read off the input
directly. It did not survive reseeding: under masking the privileged latent
helps in only $2$ of $5$ seeds and the seed-to-seed spread grows by roughly an
order of magnitude. A single-seed pilot had shown a clean reversal of the
ordering, and it is recorded here as noise rather than as a finding, because the
paired five-seed design does not reproduce it.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Evidence and Its Bound}
\label{sec:models:adapter-null}

Inside the control loop the picture changes. Evaluation-time ablations on one
trained checkpoint compared the full stack against a zeroed latent, a shuffled
latent, a frozen latent, an in-distribution mean latent and a sham plant change,
which together separate whether any effect is due to the latent's presence, its
content, or its online updating. No contrast produced a consistent improvement:
the differences in lateral error, cut-height error and impact counts are small
relative to run-to-run variation and change sign across the comparisons.

The explanation is a property of the plant rather than of the estimator, and it
was found by measuring before drawing a conclusion. Across the scored window the
first cylinder's valve command is pinned at its limit for roughly $92\,\%$ of the
episode, the second for $67$--$82\,\%$ and the fourth for $50$--$57\,\%$, each
measured against its own per-axis limit rather than a common one. Control-effort
ratios before and after the scheduled plant change lie between $1.005$ and
$1.009$, confirming that no headroom is released. A bounded additive correction
cannot express itself through an actuator that is already at its limit, so the
experiment as posed could not have detected adaptation regardless of the quality
of $\hat{\bm{z}}$. This is the classical interaction between adaptation and
input saturation, in which an adaptive law loses the authority it needs exactly
when the plant most requires it and dedicated anti-windup compensation becomes
necessary~\cite{sofrony2024antiwindup}.

One further measurement shows that the saturation is not an artefact of a poor
setting. Reducing the lead cap to relieve the windup does lower the first
cylinder's saturation from $0.91$ to $0.14$, but degrades cut height by a factor
of three: the actuator is pinned because the task sits close to the plant's
capability limit (Section~\ref{sec:sys:capability}), not because a gain was
chosen badly.

\subsection{Propagation of the Latent Advantage}
\label{sec:models:chain}

The chain gate asks whether the deployable estimate retains a worthwhile share
of the advantage the true latent buys, by comparing
$SD_0(\bm{s},\bm{u})$, $SD_z(\bm{s},\bm{u},\bm{z})$ and
$SD_z(\bm{s},\bm{u},\hat{\bm{z}})$ under the frozen protocol with paired
bootstrap intervals. The chain does not carry the advantage to the controller,
and the measurement locates where it stops. On extension and velocity neither the
true latent nor the estimate beats the no-latent control with an interval
excluding zero, on either pool. The estimate is not worse than the true latent,
and the retained fraction of the true advantage is positive on both channels, so
the failure is not that the AAM estimates badly; the AAM recovers the latent
about as well as the latent can be used. The failure is upstream, and
Table~\ref{tab:models:latent-channels} located it: the advantage the estimator
faithfully recovers is a pressure-channel advantage.

Taken together, the three subsections above describe a chain that is sound
component by component, with the end-to-end loss localised to a single channel.
That verdict is narrowed by Section~\ref{sec:eval:predictive-utility}, which
finds the estimate indistinguishable from the privileged latent and ahead of an
uninformative one on extension and velocity when the two are compared inside one
model. The estimator is not where the chain loses the advantage.
The inverse model is robust
across the plant family (Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}); the latent is
recoverable from causal history alone; the adapter uses it when it is trained in
isolation. What is missing is a demonstrated path from a pressure-channel latent
to the extension the controller integrates, through an actuator with enough
headroom to act on it. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} reports the deployment
measurements in full, and Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} takes up what would have
to change for the question to be answered rather than deferred.

\section{Training and Model-Selection Protocol}
\label{sec:models:training}

All adaptation-chain models share one split. Runs are divided $82$ / $20$ / $16$
into training, validation and reserved test, by complete run \emph{and} by
factor configuration, so no factor key appears on both sides of a boundary, and
windows never cross run boundaries. The sixteen reserved runs entered no decision at any stage: they are recorded
as unused in the adapter's manifest, and no history length, checkpoint or
hyperparameter was selected on them. They are scored, once, after each model is
fixed, so every reserved-test number in this chapter is reported next to its
validation counterpart rather than instead of it
(Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}). Selection is by held-out criterion rather than
training loss throughout: $SD_z$ and $SD_0$ on validation rollout, the AAM on
downstream rollout quality rather than on latent error alone, and the adapter on
validation command error. Table~\ref{tab:models:frozen} records the resulting
checkpoint provenance.

One selection hazard is worth recording because it cost a false conclusion
during development. An earlier adapter variant minimised next-state error
through the frozen forward model, while the stage-level promotion gate scored
the predicted valve command. The two objectives disagree, and scoring that
variant on the gate's objective produced a uniformly negative verdict which said
nothing about the goal it had actually been trained for. The deployed adapter of
Section~\ref{sec:models:adapter} was subsequently trained directly on the
recorded valve command, so for that checkpoint the training objective and the
promotion criterion coincide and the hazard does not arise. The general rule it
produced is retained: each model is scored on the objective it was trained for,
and Table~\ref{tab:models:frozen} states for every component whether it is
frozen or trained from scratch, since the comparison of
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} depends on that answer being unambiguous.
