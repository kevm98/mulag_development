\chapter{Experimental Evaluation: Representation, Robustness, and the Value of Adaptation}
\label{chap:evaluation}
\chaptermark{Experimental Evaluation: Representation, Robustness, and the Value of Adaptation}
\fancyhead[RE]{\headfont\small\nouppercase{\leftmark}}

This chapter evaluates the closed-loop stacks defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:control}
as an evidence chain. Sections~7.2 to~7.4 first ask whether the actuator
representation matters in closed loop and how much plant variation the trained
controllers already absorb. Section~7.5 then asks what remains for a residual to
recover inside \ppoAAMFD{}, whether live history explains that recovery, and
whether any component varies with cutter mass. Section~7.6 states the conditions
under which the evidence supports online adaptation.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:eval:hypotheses}

\subsection{Test Conditions and Comparisons}
\label{sec:eval:matrix}

All closed-loop results use held-out terrain meshes, eleven trajectory families,
a truck speed of \unit[4]{km/h}, a \unit[26]{m} scoring window, and sixteen
reserved plant configurations. The matched comparison is between \ppoROM{} and
\ppoIDFD{}. Adaptation is assessed only inside \ppoAAMFD{}, by comparing a
frozen base, a capacity-matched residual without history, and a
history-conditioned residual.

The results are paired by environment or by mesh and trajectory family. Error
metrics are reported separately for lateral tracking and cut height because the
two errors describe different aspects of the task. Confidence intervals are
percentile bootstrap intervals over the paired
units~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}, at $B=20\,000$ resamples. Where one channel is
required only not to get worse, the bound is a non-inferiority margin declared
before the comparison~\cite{piaggio2012noninferiority} rather than a failure to
reject. Success rules and checkpoint rules are fixed in writing before the arms
are trained, in the sense of a pre-registered
analysis~\cite{nosek2018preregistration}; the registrations are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}. Checkpoints are selected using the validation or
training criterion specified for each experiment and are then evaluated once on
the held-out set.

\subsection{Comparative Scope and Scoring}
\label{sec:eval:standing}

\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} form the cross-stack comparison. \ppoAAMFD{} has a
different action contract and is therefore not ranked against that pair. Its
results are interpreted only through within-stack comparisons.

One training run exists per stack, so the cross-stack numbers describe two
deployed controllers and carry no estimate of policy-training variance. The
$33$ paired cells quantify terrain and trajectory variation, not retraining
variation, and they are not a substitute for independent policy seeds. The
matched pair differs only in the actuator path, which is what supports the
representation claim, and that claim is therefore about the two realised
controllers rather than about what retraining either representation would
produce. The seed-level statements in Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation} come
from three context-column training runs on one shared frozen base and are
paired within seed.

\subsection{Scoring Horizon}
\label{sec:eval:horizon}

All reported closed-loop results use the full \unit[26]{m} trajectory rather
than a shorter selection window. The earlier \unit[8.9]{m} selection window
covered only about one third of an episode and is therefore treated as an
evaluation-protocol limitation, not as evidence about adaptation.

\label{sec:eval:tracking}

\section{Actuator Representation and Dynamic Response}
\label{sec:eval:delay}

The same matched pair is now examined in the actuation channel: transport
delay, closed-loop step response, and command effort.

\subsection{Plant Delay and Step Response}
\label{sec:eval:delay-plant}

Open-loop excitation measured a command-to-pressure delay of approximately
\unit[117]{ms}. Cylinder extension and joint motion began at the same time,
which confirms that the dominant delay is hydraulic rather than kinematic. The
similar values across excitation amplitudes indicate that this delay is a stable
property of the simulated actuation chain.

limits and policy dynamics dominate the response time. The actuator path changes
the shape of the step, not the main settling time.

\section{Generalisation, Mechanical Robustness and Authority Margin}
\label{sec:eval:robustness}

The preceding sections use the held-out evaluation set. This section establishes
the robustness already present before adaptation is considered, and then applies
a common authority reduction to all stacks.

What is tested here is narrower than the section title, and the limit is stated
first because it bounds every number below. Four of the six randomised factors
are hydraulic (Table~\ref{tab:data:factors}). On the inverse and forward path the
hydraulic factors are not applied at all, so the variation that reaches
\ppoIDFD{} is mechanical: cutter mass and centre-of-mass height. On \ppoROM{}
the hydraulic factors are applied but constrained to the model's identification
range. The authority sweep that follows is a generic reduction of realised
actuator motion, not plant variation. Nothing in this section therefore supports
a claim of robustness to hydraulic variation, and the summary in
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} is scoped to match.

Within that scope the matched stacks generalise to the held-out meshes,
trajectory families and reserved plant configurations used in this chapter, and
the randomisation sweep shows little change in tracking over the tested
mechanical variation.

The held-out evaluation therefore establishes transfer within the tested
simulation population. It does not establish performance on arbitrary hydraulic
conditions or physical hardware.

\subsection{Delivered Stress}
\label{sec:eval:delivered-stress}

The sweep therefore supports robustness to the tested changes in tool mass and
centre-of-mass height. It does not establish robustness to unbounded hydraulic
variation. A common authority perturbation was used to compare all stacks more
directly. This separates a reduction in realised actuator motion from the
specific randomisation factors used during deployment.

\subsection{Actuator Authority}
\label{sec:eval:boundary-design}

Actuator authority was reduced by scaling the realised actuator displacement:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eval:authority}
  \Delta x^{\text{applied}}_k = \kappa\,\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}_k,
  \qquad \kappa \in (0,1].
\end{equation}

Here, $\kappa=1$ represents nominal authority and smaller values reduce the
motion delivered to the plant. The same perturbation is applied after each
stack's actuator representation, which makes the comparison independent of the
individual factor parameterisations.

  \centering

  $\kappa$ is reduced, on mesh $1337$ at $4.0$\,km/h. Left: nominal payload,
  where every stack degrades as authority is withdrawn. Right: $4.0\times$
  payload, where the frozen controller is \emph{worst at full authority} and

The learned controllers retain a broad margin under authority loss. A hard
failure boundary was not reached within the tested range, so the experiment
establishes an authority margin rather than a breakdown threshold. \ppoIDFD{}
has the lowest lateral error at every rung of the cross-stack comparison.

No stack reached the contact or safety gate, so at nominal payload the sweep
bounds an authority margin rather than locating a breakdown.

Under load the same axis behaves differently, and the difference is the useful
part. At $4.0\times$ payload the frozen controller is worst at \emph{full}
authority, at $330.9$\,mm lateral, $184.9$\,mm cut and $0.475$ ground contact,
and improves monotonically as authority is withdrawn, reaching $105.9$\,mm,
$11.7$\,mm and $0.829$ at $\kappa=0.1$. Withdrawing authority is not a stress
under load; it is a remedy. That is the impact-and-rebound failure mode of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:robustness} seen from the actuator side: the heavy tool
carries too much authority into the ground, and throttling it damps the
overshoot.

The payload-conditioned arm reaches the same place without giving anything up.
At full authority it reads $115.7$\,mm lateral and $0.824$ contact, within
$10$\,mm and $0.005$ of the best the frozen controller achieves anywhere on the
authority axis, and it stays flat as $\kappa$ falls. The two curves cross near
$\kappa \approx 0.6$: below that the frozen controller is already damped, has
stopped losing ground, and the conditioning costs a little lateral accuracy,
which is the same regime dependence the payload sweep shows at $3.0\times$.

The distinction that matters for deployment is that authority is a fixed setting
and payload is not. De-rating the actuator to $\kappa=0.1$ buys the loaded
improvement and charges for it permanently at nominal load, where the same stack
degrades from $45$\,mm to $72$\,mm lateral. Conditioning on an estimated payload
buys the loaded improvement at full authority and, through the deadband, leaves
the nominal case bit-for-bit unchanged. One is a compromise; the other is
conditional.

\subsection{What Robustness Leaves for Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:robustness-residual}

The robustness result sets the scale for the following experiment. At nominal
payload the frozen controllers absorb substantial variation and the authority
sweep reaches no contact or safety failure, so there is little there for
adaptation to recover. That is precisely why the adaptation study sweeps
payload instead: it locates the regime where the frozen controller does fail,
and measures the mechanism where a deficit actually exists.

\section{Evaluation of Online Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation}

The deployed mechanism conditions the frozen policy on a payload it estimates
online from the task observation it already reads
(Section~\ref{sec:models:payload}). This section measures what that conditioning
is worth, in which payload regime it holds, and how much of it depends on the
estimate being correct rather than merely present.

Three arms are compared, all of them the same network at a different context
value. \emph{Frozen} zeroes the estimate, which by
Equation~\eqref{eq:ctrl:af-residual} recovers the base policy exactly rather
than approximating it. \emph{Adaptive} supplies the online estimate and is the
deployable arm. \emph{Oracle} supplies the true payload and is included as a
reference point, not as a bound.

\subsection{Experiment and Measurement}
\label{sec:eval:instrument}

Scoring uses held-out mesh 3141, $176$ environments and $900$ steps per arm,
paired on the environment index so that every comparison holds terrain,
trajectory and payload fixed. Each arm runs in its own process, because an
earlier layout that swept arms inside one process failed its own A/A check. The
statistic is the paired per-environment median difference with a percentile
bootstrap at $B=20000$, reported with the fraction of environments improved.

The median log-ratio is not used as the primary statistic here. It disagrees in
sign with the marginal medians in several cells, because with an even sample and
a two-lobed paired distribution $\operatorname{median}(x-y)$ and
$\operatorname{median}(x/y)$ need not fall on the same side of their null
values. The difference and the win fraction are the honest summaries of an
effect this heterogeneous.

Three payloads are scored, at $3.0\times$, $4.0\times$ and $5.0\times$ nominal
cutter mass, and three independently trained context-column seeds. The seeds
share one frozen base, so they test the adaptation mechanism's sensitivity to
initialisation rather than the whole controller's, and are described that way
throughout.

\subsection{What the Instrument Can Resolve}

Before reading any effect, the instrument is measured against itself. Two arms
are run at $4.0\times$, both the frozen policy, differing only in the fifth
significant figure of the payload, a $0.0025\,\%$ mass change that is physically
negligible but forces a different random draw. Any difference reported is noise,
and it bounds what this campaign may call an effect.

generates on its own.

One detail of this table justifies the choice of statistic. The two arms'
marginal medians differ substantially on cut, $134.83$ against $153.71\,$mm,
while their paired difference is $-0.65\,$mm. Marginal medians move under
resampling in a way the paired difference does not, which is why the paired
difference is the primary statistic throughout.

A caveat travels with it. This floor is measured at $4.0\times$, where the
frozen controller is failing and its variance is correspondingly large. It
therefore bounds the claims made at $4.0\times$ and $5.0\times$ but does not
bound the smaller lateral regression reported at $3.0\times$, which would need
its own A/A on a stable plant.

Online payload conditioning improves cut height, lateral tracking and ground
contact together at $4.0\times$ and $5.0\times$, every channel with its interval
excluding zero. Pooled cut-height error falls by $71.5\,$mm against a frozen
baseline of $167.5\,$mm and by $82.2\,$mm against $267.4\,$mm, a reduction of
$43\,\%$ and $31\,\%$ of the frozen error, while ground contact rises by $0.12$
and $0.09$. Both task channels improve at once, which a single trade-off along
the cut--lateral curve cannot produce.

The mechanism is regime-selective, and the sweep resolves the regime exactly.
At $3.0\times$ the frozen controller still holds the ground and conditioning
costs lateral accuracy; from $4.0\times$ it gains on every channel. The
discriminating variable is ground contact, and the deadband of
Section~\ref{sec:models:payload} is what confines the mechanism to the loaded
side of it.

\subsection{One Seed of Three Does Not Benefit}

Seeds 17 and 101 carry the pooled effect on the vertical channels, improving
cut height by $187.6\,$mm and $110.1\,$mm at $5.0\times$. Seed~23 does not:
neutral on cut at $4.0\times$ and $-46.68\,$mm at $5.0\times$, with contact
following the same pattern on both meshes. Its lateral behaviour is not a
seed effect, since it improves lateral significantly at both payloads on mesh
8191 (Table~\ref{tab:eval:mesh8191}).

The cause is locatable rather than mysterious. All three oracles sit near
$750\,$mm of lateral error, so every seed has the same trade-off curve
available, and what differs is where the online estimate places the policy on
it: $113\,$mm for seed 17, $150\,$mm for seed 101, $368\,$mm for seed 23. The
variation is in the fitted estimator, not in the policy it serves, which makes
it a tractable target for future work rather than a property of the
mechanism.

\subsection{The True Payload Is Not an Upper Bound}

The deployable arm outperforms the privileged one. Handing the policy the true
payload is worse than letting it estimate it, and at the largest load
substantially so: seed 101's oracle at $5.0\times$ reads $652.29\,$mm of cut
error at a ground contact of $0.220$, while its adaptive arm on the same
checkpoint reads $40.46\,$mm at $0.803$. Estimation is not a concession made for
deployability here; it is the better controller.

The context enters as $\bm{W}_{\mathrm{ctx}}\hat{\bm{c}}$ with the payload
channel reaching $8$ at $5.0\times$, and seed 101 carries the largest context
weight of the three. The largest weight meeting the largest input over-drives
the policy off the ground. The norm does not order the trade-off at $3.0\times$,
where the smallest-weight seed sits in the middle, so this is a statement about
the extreme rather than a monotone law.

The deployable arm escapes it because the estimate is damped: it starts each
episode at nominal and arrives through an exponential moving average, so it
never reaches the region the true value walks straight into. The gradual arrival
is part of the mechanism, not an implementation detail.

\subsection{Correct Payload Against Wrong Payload}
\label{sec:eval:attribution}

The remaining question is whether the benefit comes from the payload
\emph{information} or merely from the presence of a plausible context. The
control permutes the true payload across environments on a mixed-payload plant,
which preserves its distribution and magnitude exactly and destroys only its
pairing with the environment it describes. The permutation is redrawn every step
and is a derangement, so no environment keeps its own value. At a fixed payload
this control would be vacuous, because every environment shares one mass and a
permutation is the identity; the mixed plant is what gives it content.

\section{What Determines Effective Online Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

Three conditions govern whether online adaptation adds value beyond a robust
learned controller, and the experiments above separate them.

\textbf{A control-relevant deficit must remain.} This is the condition the
payload sweep isolates most cleanly. The same mechanism, on the same
checkpoints, improves every channel at $4.0\times$ and costs lateral accuracy at
$3.0\times$, and the discriminating variable is whether the frozen controller is
losing ground contact. Adaptation is not uniformly good; it is good where
robustness has run out.

\textbf{The correction path must retain authority.} The earlier explicit
mechanism produced no closed-loop effect because clipping removed the correction
before it reached the plant, although its estimate was informative
(Section~\ref{sec:models:adapter-null}). Conditioning the policy rather than
correcting its command avoids that interface entirely: there is no bounded term
to clip, because the estimate changes what the policy computes rather than what
is added to its output.

\textbf{The estimate must be delivered at a rate the plant tolerates.} The
oracle result shows that correct information supplied instantaneously can be
worse than an approximation supplied gradually. Damping is not a safety margin
bolted on afterwards; it is the reason the deployable arm outperforms the
privileged one.

The result is that a controller estimating its own payload from signals it
already carries improves cut height, lateral tracking and ground contact
together, in the loaded regime where a robust frozen controller is failing, and
outperforms the same controller handed the true value. The conditions above say
where it works and the controls say why, which is what makes it deployable
rather than merely favourable.
