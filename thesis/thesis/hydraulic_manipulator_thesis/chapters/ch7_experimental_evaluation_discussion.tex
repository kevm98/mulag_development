\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:eval:hypotheses}

This chapter evaluates the three control stacks assembled in
Chapter~\ref{chap:control} and the learned actuator models they act through. It
answers the last two research questions of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions}:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ5] How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking,
    command behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?
  \item[RQ6] Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable
    from recent observations, and does online adaptation by latent
    identification improve closed-loop performance?
\end{description}

The two differ in kind. RQ5 asks what a design choice changes and is answered by
direct measurement on the matched pair \ppoROM{} (R) and \ppoIDFD{} (IF), which
agree on every recorded training field and differ only in the actuator path.
RQ6 asks whether a mechanism pays, could fail in ways worth declaring in
advance, and is therefore carried by predeclared hypotheses and by
within-checkpoint substitutions rather than by the position of \ppoAAMIDFD{}
(AIF) in a table. The short labels R, IF and AIF of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions} are used interchangeably with the full
names. RQ6 also decomposes into a chain of dependent questions, each worth
testing only if the one before it holds: is plant identity present in the data
at all; can an estimator recover it; does the estimate follow the plant when the
plant moves; does it improve a model that consumes it; does that improvement
reach the controller; and if it does not, what stops it? The first four links
hold and the fifth does not, which is why the chapter spends its last
experiments on the sixth.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:eval:hyp-list}

Four hypotheses are declared in advance, the first two settled at model level
and the last two in the control loop.

\begin{description}
  \item[H1] The inverse model trained on randomised Simscape data is
    sufficiently robust to the gap between Simscape and the IsaacLab
    reduced-order hydraulic system that it can stay frozen. Support: a state
    predictor conditioned on an estimated latent, trained without the inverse
    model in the loop, closes the residual gap and beats a matched no-latent
    predictor on the same data.
  \item[H2] The value of the estimated latent lies in its variation over time,
    not in a static plant identity. Support: substituting a per-episode
    constant latent costs materially more than substituting a wrong constant.
  \item[H3] The closed-loop effect of plant-identity conditioning, estimated
    under a common evaluation protocol. H3 is an \emph{estimand} rather than a
    hypothesis: its product is an interval, informative wherever it is narrower
    than the baseline mean it is measured against. No practical-equivalence
    margin was predeclared, so a narrow interval bounds the effect and does not
    establish equivalence.
  \item[H4] The effect estimated in H3 is boundable before an estimator is
    built, from what a privileged latent read from the true factors achieves.
\end{description}

H1, H2 and H4 carry rejection criteria fixed before the experiment runs: H1
fails without a majority of seeds, H2 if the constant-latent and wrong-latent
substitutions cost the same, and H4 if a privileged latent does not outperform
an uninformative one. H3 carries an adequacy criterion instead -- a matched
protocol and a calibrated interval -- whether or not that interval excludes
zero.

\subsection{Test Conditions and Experimental Matrix}
\label{sec:eval:matrix}

Every closed-loop experiment scores exactly three rows -- \ppoROM{},
\ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMIDFD{} -- under one common condition: three held-out
terrain meshes, the eleven trajectory families of the library, a pinned truck
speed of $\unit[4]{km/h}$, a $\unit[26]{m}$ scored window, $32$ environments and
a fixed evaluation seed. Model-level experiments use the open-loop excitation
collection \texttt{rom\_openloop\_v1} of Section~\ref{sec:eval:excitation}.

scored individually rather than pooled, and the plant is drawn from sixteen
reserved factor configurations whose keys are disjoint from training and
validation by construction (Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}), so a scored run is
a plant the stack has never seen and not merely an episode it has not seen. How
much of that factor draw reaches each stack's plant differs, and is audited in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}.

\subsection{Metrics, Statistics and Fairness}
\label{sec:eval:metrics}

The closed-loop primary metrics are mean absolute lateral error of the cutter
against the commanded line and mean absolute cut-height error, with ground
contact, hard impacts, head alignment and tool acceleration reported alongside
them and the eight-gate acceptance contract of Section~\ref{sec:sys:gates} used
as the selection instrument. The shared estimand for every model-level
experiment is the free-running accumulated normalised state error over a
five-tick horizon, with no teacher forcing: the model integrates its own
predicted increments and is charged for where it lands, which is the quantity a
controller depends on, whereas a one-step error would flatter every arm equally
and hide the compounding behaviour that distinguishes them.

Primary metrics and seeds are fixed before each experiment is run. Every
comparison is paired and reports the sign count over the paired unit alongside
the mean, because a mean can be carried by a single outlying pair while a
unanimous sign cannot. Uncertainty is quantified by a paired bootstrap rather
than a parametric test, intervals are reported as ranges rather than converted
into a verdict, model selection uses the validation split only, and reserved
test runs are scored once.

The unit of replication differs between the two kinds of experiment.
Model-level experiments replicate over training seeds. The closed-loop
evaluation is deterministic -- fixed mesh, deterministic policy, no variation in
initial conditions -- and five evaluation seeds returned bit-identical numbers
to nine decimal places, so the seed is \emph{not} a unit of replication and a
sign count over seeds would report one observation five times. The closed-loop
unit is therefore the (mesh, family) cell, and metrics that exist only per mesh
are labelled $n=3$ so that the weaker evidence stays visible.

Three reporting rules follow from failures met during this work: numbers from
different collections are never compared, even for the same estimand, because
the state distribution and the normaliser differ, so a baseline is retrained
instead; every gate is reported with the measured floor of the quantity it
constrains and a positive control, since a threshold no run could violate
produces a passing verdict carrying no information; and every closed-loop result
names its checkpoint and its scoring horizon
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:horizon}).

Fairness is enforced by construction, and the guarantee differs by experiment.
All model-level and within-checkpoint ablations are \emph{paired}: the arms
share the dataset, the split, the objective, the selection criterion and the
seed, and differ in exactly one declared respect. The across-stack comparison is
matched on evaluation conditions but not on PPO training realisation.

work.

\subsection{What the Three Stacks Can and Cannot Be Compared For}
\label{sec:eval:standing}

The three stacks do not stand in the same relation to one another, and the
distinction governs every figure and table that follows.

\paragraph{The matched pair.}
\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} come from runs that agree on every recorded manifest
field -- seed $17$, terrain seed $17$, $256$ environments, stage $6$, actor
learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, entropy coefficient $0.001$, frozen normaliser
and a $150$-iteration frozen-actor warm-up -- and differ only in the actuator
path. A difference between them \emph{is} attributable to the actuator
representation, and this is the pair that answers RQ5.

\paragraph{The third condition.}
\ppoAAMIDFD{} is adaptive in the ordinary sense: its actor is
$\pi(\bm{a}\mid\bm{o},\latenthat)$, conditioned at every model tick on an online
estimate of the current plant, which also reaches the actuator through a bounded
adapter. That it is adaptive \emph{in operation} and not only in architecture is
established rather than assumed: substituting what occupies those two extra
observation channels moves closed-loop behaviour by $20.7\,\%$ on lateral error
and $39.3\,\%$ on cut height (Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}), which an actor
indifferent to the latent could not produce. It is nonetheless a separately
trained run at a different seed ($41$), an actor learning rate two orders of
magnitude lower ($3\times10^{-6}$) and no frozen-actor warm-up, so a difference
between it and either of the others may be a difference of the adaptation path
or of its training run, and this evidence cannot separate the two. It is
therefore reported as a deployed controller running end to end under the common
protocol and \emph{not} as a ranked competitor; the remaining asymmetries are
set out in Section~\ref{sec:eval:validity}.

\paragraph{Where the adaptation claims live.}
Every causal statement about the adaptation path rests instead on a
within-checkpoint ablation: one input is substituted while the policy, the
actuator path, the plant, the mesh, the trajectory family and the evaluation
seed are held fixed. The four-way latent substitution of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}, the bandwidth sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:bandwidth}, the predictive comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:predictive-utility} and the input ablations of
Chapter~\ref{chap:models} are all of this form.

\subsection{Checkpoint Selection and the Scoring Horizon}
\label{sec:eval:horizon}

One protocol decision turned out to be load-bearing and is reported rather than
resolved silently. The project's checkpoint-selection utility defaults to a
$480$-step window, which at the $\unit[4]{km/h}$ evaluation speed covers $8.9$
of the $\unit[26]{m}$ course, so a candidate can satisfy it on the opening third
alone. Every candidate was therefore re-scored over the complete path against
the same eight-gate contract.

The adaptive stack is therefore represented throughout this chapter by
\texttt{headroom\_priced\_v1} at iteration $299$, which passes seven of the
eight gates on three meshes and fails only the lateral gate, at $0.326$ against
a threshold of $0.300$. Its name records the mechanism: headroom was priced into
that run's reward, and a quantity that is gated but not priced will be traded
away. Two measurements fix what the selection is sensitive to. The adapter's
residual scale is not the operative variable, since zeroing it moves ground
contact by $0.001$; and the adaptive stack does not reduce to \ppoIDFD{} when
that residual is disabled, because its policy is separately trained and
conditioned on the latent. Each stack is therefore selected on its own terms.
The $480$-step default was used for \ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} as well, so the
caveat was checked for all three rows; those two hold ground contact above
$0.95$ over the full path and are unaffected.

One earlier checkpoint, \texttt{stage31\_idfd\_aam\_adapt\_v1}/$24$, records all
eight gates passing on six held-out meshes and appears to beat the one promoted
here. It was scored under an easier protocol -- $\unit[2]{km/h}$ rather than
$\unit[4]{km/h}$, and eight of the eleven families, omitting three of those that
Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} shows carrying the pooled means -- and a
worst-family gate evaluated over a subset that excludes the hard families is a
weaker test by construction. That comparison is therefore not available: no
checkpoint of any stack has been shown to pass all eight gates under the
protocol used here, and the $7/8$ checkpoint scored correctly is reported in
preference to the $8/8$ checkpoint scored under conditions the rest of the
chapter does not use.
\section{Closed-Loop Task Performance}
\label{sec:eval:tracking}

The chapter begins with the machine doing the task, because everything after it
either explains these numbers or asks how far they hold. This is the only
unperturbed comparison of the three stacks, it carries the answer to RQ5 on
tracking, and it is where the estimand H3 is answered. All three stacks are scored on three held-out terrain meshes and
eleven trajectory families under identical conditions: the same pinned truck
speed of $4$\,km/h, the same $26$\,m scored window, the same $32$ environments, distributed over eleven trajectory families,
and the same evaluation seed. The unit of analysis is the (mesh, family) cell,
which gives $33$ paired conditions per stack; Section~\ref{sec:eval:metrics}
explains why the evaluation seed is not a unit of replication here.

Figure~\ref{fig:eval:paths} shows what the aggregate numbers summarise, on the
three families the stacks track best. On these the commanded line is followed
closely by all three, and the visible differences are oscillations about the
reference rather than departures from it. Those oscillations are a property of
the closed loop and not of the plot: they have a wavelength of one to two metres,
which at $4$\,km/h is a period of one to two seconds, so they are slower than any
filter that could be applied without removing the signal itself. Their amplitude
is what the $26$ to $28$\,mm mean absolute error records, and reducing it is a
control problem rather than a presentation one. That is the honest picture of the
typical case, and it is worth stating plainly because the aggregate means are
not dominated by it: the suite also contains families such as the double
S-curve and the Fourier meander, on which errors are an order of magnitude
larger, and those cells carry the means reported below.

The distinction matters for how Table~\ref{tab:eval:tracking} should be read.
The stacks differ in the \emph{tail} of the error distribution rather than in
their typical behaviour, and the worst excursion over all cells is roughly
$0.25$\,m for \ppoIDFD{} against about $1$\,m for the adaptive stack and $2$\,m
for \ppoROM{}.

The paired contrast closest to isolating adaptation is \ppoAAMIDFD{} against
\ppoIDFD{}. The two share the frozen inverse and forward models and the
evaluation protocol, but they are not a single-variable pair: the adaptive actor
reads two extra observation channels and was trained from a different seed and
learning-rate schedule (Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:constraints}). What follows is a
comparison of two deployed controllers, not of two training procedures.

On the three tracking metrics the two are separated by at most a few per cent.
Lateral error differs by $-1.6\%$ with the adaptive stack ahead in $9$ of $33$
cells, cut-height error by $+53.8\%$ ahead in $25$ of $33$, and ground contact
by $-0.4\%$ ahead in $25$ of $33$. All three paired bootstrap intervals span
zero, so \textbf{the effect of plant-identity conditioning is smaller than this
protocol resolves}. The measurement H3 returns is therefore its size, not its
sign.

The interval is the product, and it is uneven across the three metrics.
The paired intervals are $[-0.044, +0.040]\,$m on lateral error against a
\ppoIDFD{} mean of $0.070\,$m, $[-0.0034, +0.0108]\,$m on cut height against
$0.0063\,$m, and $[-0.024, +0.015]$ on ground contact against $0.953$. Read as a
bound, only the last is informative: ground contact differs by less than about
$2.4$ percentage points either way. On the two tracking errors the interval is
comparable to or wider than the baseline mean itself, so the experiment does not
constrain the effect to anything smaller than the effect that was being looked
for. Establishing equivalence would require a predeclared margin and a sample
large enough to fit inside it; neither is available here.

Mean and median disagree on cut-height error and ground contact: the adaptive
stack is ahead in roughly three quarters of the cells yet behind on the mean,
the signature of a few poor cells rather than a uniform deficit. Both statistics
therefore appear in Table~\ref{tab:eval:tracking}.

Three differences stand out from the null result above, and two of them do
resolve. \ppoIDFD{}
scores $0.2247$ on head alignment, including $-0.855$ on one mesh, the collapse
under unconstrained fine-tuning measured over the full path; the adaptive stack
is the only one that holds alignment, at $0.9763$; and it pays for its ground
contact in impacts ($+33\%$, interval $[+0.013, +0.043]$) and tool acceleration
($+32\%$, interval $[+2.8, +21.3]\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$). Those last two intervals
exclude zero, and they are the only closed-loop contrasts in this chapter that
do, both of them against the adaptive stack. They are also the weakest evidence in
the table: impacts and tool acceleration aggregate per mesh rather than per
cell, so the bootstrap resamples three units and the interval should be read as
a sign indication rather than a calibrated bound.

worst-family statistics the lateral gate is failed by every stack, and by

stack alone.

The worst-family aggregation compresses eleven trajectories into one number, so

not separate these three stacks. Lateral is exceeded by each of them on a single

against $0.2247$ for \ppoIDFD{}, and it is on those that the comparative
statements of this chapter rest.

Three questions follow from this table, and the next three sections take them in
turn. Why the two representations produce different controllers at all, and what
each spends to track, is a question about the actuation chain and is answered in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay}. Whether the performance survives terrain, plant
and trajectory it has not seen is answered in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:robustness}. And why the adaptive stack does not convert
its model-level advantage into task performance -- an advantage established
in Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation} and absent from the table above -- occupies
Sections~\ref{sec:eval:identifiability} to~\ref{sec:eval:ceiling}.

\section{Actuator Representation and Dynamic Response}
\label{sec:eval:delay}

Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} established that the two actuator
representations do not produce the same controller. This section measures what
physically differs between them, and it is the section that answers RQ5 on
latency and command behaviour: how long a command takes to reach the cylinder,
how quickly each stack answers a change in what it is asked to do, and what
each spends in actuation to track at all.

Latency enters the system in two quite different places, and separating them
comes first. The hydraulics impose a transport delay that no controller can
remove; the control stack then adds its own, and only the second is an
architectural choice. Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-plant} measures the first on
open-loop excitation, where it can be isolated cleanly.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-stacks} asks whether the three stacks differ in how
quickly they drive the plant, and resolves the difference into its hydraulic and
mechanical halves. Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response} asks the question that
matters for the task -- how long each takes to answer a change in the commanded
line -- and finds the answer dominated by the control side rather than the
hydraulic one. Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort} then prices the tracking of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} in commanded actuation and in command
reversals, which is what wears the machine.

\subsection{Dead Time in the Plant}
\label{sec:eval:delay-plant}

Delay is measured here rather than assumed, and it is worth separating from an
earlier negative result. Attempts to identify the valve's dynamic \emph{model}
from the available datasets did not succeed, but that is a parameter-fitting
outcome and says nothing about whether the command-to-response \emph{latency} is
observable. It is, and the open-loop collection of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:excitation} is close to an ideal instrument for it: the
excitation is a train of zero-mean square waves with known edge times, which is
what one would design for a step-response measurement.

The estimator locates each command edge and records the first sample at which
the response leaves the pre-edge noise floor by four standard deviations. A
cross-correlation peak was rejected because it conflates dead time with rise
time. Only the pressure channel is drawn as a trace in
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:delay-response}: cylinder and joint velocity are
differentiated quantities whose edge-averages leave a transient at the command
instant that reads as a response preceding its own cause.

The dominant term is the command-to-pressure lag of $117$\,ms, stable across
three of the four amplitude tiers. The kinematic control is exact: the
command-to-extension and command-to-joint onsets agree to within $0.0$ control
steps at every amplitude, so the linkage contributes nothing and the estimator
is not manufacturing lag.

The medians do not compose -- the first two links sum to $16$ control steps
while the direct measurement gives $7$ to $11$ -- because the per-edge
distributions are wide and the median of a sum is not the sum of medians. Each
link is supported individually; the chain decomposition is not.

This matters for the architecture rather than merely describing the plant. A
dead time of $117$\,ms is $2.3$ model ticks at the $20$\,Hz estimator rate and
roughly a tenth of the \AAM{}'s one-second observation window. A controller
reading only the instantaneous state cannot compensate for it, because the
information required is not present in a single slice; this is the standard
argument for history-based estimation~\cite{ljung1999system}, and it holds here
on measured rather than assumed grounds.

\subsection{The Chain Compared Across the Three Stacks}
\label{sec:eval:delay-stacks}

The measurement above characterises one plant on open-loop excitation. The three
control stacks reach that plant through different actuator paths, and whether
those paths differ in latency is a question about the architectures rather than
about the hydraulics. It is answered by recording the same chain during a
closed-loop drive on each stack.

One methodological difference must be stated before the numbers, because it
prevents a false comparison. A closed-loop command varies continuously and has
no step edges, so onset detection cannot be used and the estimator is instead a
cross-correlation lag, which conflates dead time with rise time. The values in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:chain-stacks} are therefore valid for comparing stacks,
where the bias is identical across the three, and are \emph{not} comparable with
the open-loop dead times of Table~\ref{tab:eval:delay}. The two tables do not
measure the same quantity.

not in how motion propagates through the linkage.

\subsection{Step Response to a Commanded Change}
\label{sec:eval:step-response}

The dead time measured so far belongs to the hydraulics and is the same for
every stack. What differs is how each control stack copes with it, and that cannot be
recovered from an ordinary drive: reference and achieved path are coherent only
below about $0.3$\,Hz, where phase reflects the controller's own lag dynamics
rather than transport, so no delay extracted there is a dead time
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:validity}). The delay is therefore injected rather than
inferred. A step of $100$\,mm is
added to the \emph{commanded} line partway through the scored window, and each
stack is scored on the same mesh, seed and speed. The step is applied at the
single point where the tracking line is computed, so it reaches the policy's own
preview of the path as well as the error term: a step visible to the metric but
not to the controller would measure only the metric's discontinuity.

The result is that \textbf{the hydraulics are not what makes these stacks slow}.
Every stack begins to answer the step three to five times later than the
$117$\,ms the plant needs, and none settles inside two seconds. Part of that is
a hard bound rather than a control deficiency: at a command rate limit of
$0.004$\,m per control step the fastest possible completion of a $100$\,mm step
is $417$\,ms, which already exceeds the hydraulic dead time by a factor of
three. The remainder is control-side, and settling times of $2.2$ to $3.2$\,s
are five to eight times that bound.

Between the stacks the ordering is modest and not uniform. \ppoIDFD{} settles
soonest at $2.18$\,s, \ppoAAMIDFD{} is slowest to begin at $558$\,ms and slowest
to settle at $3.23$\,s, and \ppoROM{} overshoots least. The adaptive stack is
therefore not faster to answer a change in the command, which is worth stating
plainly alongside the finding of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-stacks} that it reaches cylinder motion in less than
half the time of the others: a shorter path from command to actuator does not
translate into a quicker response at the tool.

The dead-time column compares when a response becomes \emph{detectable} rather
than pure transport, because it is measured against each stack's own pre-step
band and the stacks oscillate differently during normal tracking; the rise and
settling columns carry the more trustworthy comparison.

\subsection{The Latency Budget of the Closed Loop}
\label{sec:eval:delay-synthesis}

The three results are easy to misread in isolation, and together they say
something the chapter needs later. The plant costs $117$\,ms from valve command
to differential pressure, and that figure is amplitude-invariant with an exact
kinematic control, so it is a property of the hydraulics rather than of any
controller. The stacks differ in how quickly they traverse that chain, and the
adaptive stack traverses it fastest, reaching cylinder motion in $133$\,ms
against $283$\,ms for both others. Yet when the commanded line moves, the
adaptive stack is the \emph{slowest} of the three to respond and to settle.

A short path from command to actuator therefore does not produce a quick
response at the tool, and the reason is that the dominant term is neither of the
two. A $100$\,mm step cannot be completed faster than $417$\,ms at the command
rate limit, which already exceeds the hydraulic dead time threefold, and the
stacks settle in $2.2$ to $3.2$\,s, five to eight times that bound. The
actuation chain is not the bottleneck; the commanded rate and the policy's own
dynamics are.

This bears directly on the adaptation question. An estimator that updates every
$50$\,ms and a plant that answers in $117$\,ms are both fast relative to a loop
that needs seconds to move the tool to a new line. Whatever the latent
contributes, it contributes it to a controller whose response is set elsewhere,
which is one candidate explanation for the gap between the model-level effect
reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval:id-sufficiency} and the closely matched
closed-loop arms of Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}. It is taken up as
an explanation in Section~\ref{sec:eval:ceiling}, once the model-level effect
itself has been established.

\subsection{Control Effort and Command Chatter}
\label{sec:eval:effort}

error are not equivalent if one commands twice the actuation to get there. Three
measures are taken from the same drives, chosen so that none restates another:
the root-mean-square valve command, its rate of change, and the number of times
per second the command reverses the direction of motion. The last is
deliberately scale-free, because a stack can command little in total and still
chatter, and chatter is what wears the machine.

\section{Generalisation and Robustness}
\label{sec:eval:robustness}

Before the sweep itself, it is worth stating what the evaluation protocol of
this chapter has already established about generalisation, because it is spread
across several sections and is easy to miss when each is read on its own. Every
closed-loop number reported here is measured on material the policies were not
trained on. The terrain meshes are held out, the eleven trajectory families are
scored individually rather than pooled, and the plant is drawn from the sixteen
reserved factor configurations whose keys are disjoint from the training and
validation splits by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}), so a scored run is a plant the stack has
never seen rather than merely an episode it has not seen -- with the
qualification, established in Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}, that on
the inverse/forward plant the unseen part of that draw is its mechanical half.
On that material
\ppoIDFD{} holds the lateral criterion in $32$ of $33$ (mesh, family) cells at
a median of $\unit[0.0281]{m}$ (Table~\ref{tab:eval:family-lateral}). The
learned actuator underneath it generalises across simulators on the same
evidence: the frozen inverse model was identified on Simscape alone and scored
on $64$ IsaacLab runs lying entirely outside its training distribution, where
its accuracy is flat to a factor of $1.09$ between the best and worst domain
(Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}). Generalisation across simulator, plant
draw, terrain and trajectory is therefore demonstrated, and it is demonstrated
without any online adaptation.

The question in this section is the remaining one: how far the plant may be
moved before that generalisation stops holding, which a pass or fail on a fixed
factor set cannot answer. The magnitude of the deployment-factor randomisation
was therefore swept. Each environment keeps the draw it would have received and
that draw is stretched away from the nominal plant along the same line, so the
requested stress is $0$ at the nominal machine, $1$ at the manifest as drawn and
$2$ at twice every deviation. The intention was that only the magnitude should
change and never which plants are visited, so that the curve would not be
confounded with a change of population.

That is what the sweep requested. Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress} reports
what the plant received, and the two differ in a way that governs how the rest
of this section may be read, so the audit is given before the result.

  }

\textbf{No stack's tracking moves with the requested stress.} Mean lateral error
over the sweep goes from $0.1286$ to $0.1184$\,m for \ppoROM{}, from $0.0277$ to
$0.0287$\,m for \ppoIDFD{} and from $0.0546$ to $0.0673$\,m for the adaptive
stack, and ground contact stays within $2.2\%$ of nominal for \ppoROM{} and
$2.7\%$ for the adaptive stack, while \ppoIDFD{} moves furthest at $5.5\%$. No
point on the curve is a breakdown.

That is a flat curve, and a flat curve admits two readings: the controllers
absorb the stress, or the stress did not arrive. The audit below establishes
that the second reading cannot be excluded, so the first is not asserted here.

The one excursion is instructive precisely because it is not a trend.
\ppoIDFD{} records $0.178$\,m at scale $1$ against roughly $0.03$\,m everywhere
else, including at the \emph{more} extreme scales $1.5$ and $2$. Resolving it by
family shows a single trajectory responsible: the double S-curve reaches
$1.352$\,m, against $0.058$ and $0.059$\,m at the neighbouring scales. One
combination of plant and path destabilises that stack, and the combination does
not survive being exaggerated. It is a genuine failure on a real manifest draw
and it is not a breakdown point, and the distinction matters: a curve summarised
only by its worst value would report a breakdown at scale $1$ that the
neighbouring points contradict.

The sweep uses one mesh and eleven families per point, so it resolves a trend
rather than a small difference between stacks, and its absolute values are not
comparable with the three-mesh tables earlier in this chapter.

\subsection{How Much of the Requested Stress Reached the Plant}
\label{sec:eval:delivered-stress}

Two properties of the perturbation path decide what a sweep point delivers, and
neither was accounted for when the sweep was designed. They are stated in the
order of their severity.

\paragraph{Which factors reach which plant.}
The six factors do not all act, and what acts differs by stack.
Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-reach} sets it out. The command reduced-order model
takes the four hydraulic factors as an explicit input, so they act on \ppoROM{}.
The inverse/forward plant does not: its step consumes the valve command, the
cylinder state, the differential pressure and the load, and takes no factor
argument at all. On \ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMIDFD{} the hydraulic factors therefore
reach the privileged latent encoder and the logs, and nothing else. What varies
across a held-out factor draw on those two stacks is tool mass and
centre-of-mass height, applied through PhysX without a clamp.

\paragraph{How far the factors that do act may travel.}
The stretch applied to a factor draw is not validated against the audited factor
ranges, because a scaled row is constructed directly rather than parsed. The
plant enforces those ranges instead: the reduced-order hydraulic model pins
every hydraulic factor back into the interval it was identified on --
compliance to $[-0.25, 0.10]$, flow to $[-0.25, 0.25]$, supply pressure to
$[-0.15, 0.15]$ and seal condition to $[0, 1]$ -- and then bounds the realised
actuator response gain to $[0.35, 1.50]$.

Table~\ref{tab:eval:delivered-stress} evaluates that clamping on the sixteen
held-out factor rows the sweep visits. Two distinct quantities appear there and
are worth separating, because conflating them overstates the finding. The
\emph{delivered fraction} is the Euclidean norm of the applied hydraulic
deviation from nominal divided by the norm of the requested one: it is a ratio
in factor space and says how much of the requested stress survived clamping, not
what it does to the machine. At the requested scale $1.5$, thirteen of the
sixteen rows have at least one factor pinned; at scale $2$, all sixteen do, and
$77.8\,\%$ of the requested deviation arrives. The label ``twice every
deviation'' is therefore accurate for the mechanical factors and wrong for the
hydraulic ones, which sit at the edge of the training envelope while the
mechanical factors alone continue to grow.

The \emph{response gain} is the separate quantity, and it is the one that
concerns authority. It multiplies both the velocity and the extension increment
the reduced-order model emits, so it scales the realised cylinder motion
directly. Two bounds apply to it. The explicit limiter clamps it to
$[0.35, 1.50]$, which alone permits a reduction of at most $65\,\%$. Within the
clamped factor ranges the worst case the six factors can actually compose is
$0.376$, so \textbf{the factor path cannot scale the reduced-order model's
realised actuator motion below $0.376$ of nominal, a reduction of at most
$62\,\%$}. That is a statement about this model's realised motion under this
perturbation path, and not about hydraulic authority in general; on the
inverse/forward plant the same factors scale nothing at all.

Three consequences follow, and they replace the conclusion this experiment was
originally read as supporting.

The first is that for two of the three stacks the sweep did not vary the
hydraulics at all. A flat curve for \ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMIDFD{} across a
hydraulic randomisation is the expected reading of an inert input, not evidence
that those stacks tolerate hydraulic variation. Their curves record tolerance to
a doubling of tool mass and centre-of-mass offset, which is a narrower claim and
is the one made here.

The second is that the sweep axis is not an axis of increasing difficulty. Above
scale $1$ it changes the \emph{kind} of plant rather than the amount of
deviation, since the hydraulic factors stop moving while the mechanical ones
continue. The single excursion described above is consistent with exactly that:
\ppoIDFD{}'s failure occurs at scale $1$, the last point at which the full
manifest draw is delivered intact, and disappears at $1.5$ and $2$, where the
hydraulic part of that same draw is pinned back inside the envelope. A
non-monotonic response is what a curve of this construction should be expected
to produce, and it is not evidence that the plant became milder.

The third is a bound on the instrument itself, on the one stack where the
hydraulic factors do act. A knockout to zero authority is unreachable by this
route: the realised motion cannot be scaled below $0.376$, whatever is
requested. A robustness boundary is a statement about the point at which
control authority becomes insufficient, and this experiment cannot reach that
point by construction. The flat curve in
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:robustness} is therefore a property of a bounded
perturbation path as much as of the controllers, and the claim that these stacks
are insensitive to plant variation is not supported by it.

\subsection{What a Robustness Boundary Would Require}
\label{sec:eval:boundary-design}

The correct experiment is not this sweep extended to scale $3$, which
Table~\ref{tab:eval:delivered-stress} shows would deliver less of what it
requests, not more. It is a search for the physical point at which command
authority becomes insufficient, and it needs three things this sweep lacks.

It needs, first, a stress axis that \emph{reaches every stack and means the same
thing on each}. The deployment factors are not one, for the two reasons above.
The axis specified here instead scales the realised cylinder motion the actuator
produced, immediately before that motion updates the physics target:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta x^{\text{applied}}_k = \alpha\,\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}_k ,
  \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1],
  \label{eq:eval:authority}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}$ comes from the command reduced-order model on
\ppoROM{} and from the inverse/forward pair on the other two. It bypasses the
clamp, it is applied at the same point in all three stacks, and it is named for
what it is: \emph{actuator-authority degradation} at the deployment interface,
not seal, not flow, and not any hydraulic parameter. Its meaning is
unambiguous -- the fraction of commanded actuator motion that is actually
realised -- and unlike a factor distance it has an obvious endpoint, since at
$\alpha \rightarrow 0$ the moving reference must become untrackable.

That endpoint is what makes the \emph{positive control} exact. Running
$\alpha = 1$ against $\alpha = 0.01$ and recording both $\Delta
x^{\text{actuator}}$ and $\Delta x^{\text{applied}}$ allows the ratio to be
checked numerically rather than inferred from a trajectory plot: it must equal
$\alpha$. Only then is the second question asked, whether the cutter loses the
line. A stack that still tracks at one per cent of its actuator authority is a
finding about the harness, and the two failures are distinguishable because the
ratio separates them.

It needs \emph{one factor at a time} before any combination. A six-factor
stretch permits one factor to compensate another, so the direction that makes
the task harder has to be measured rather than assumed.

It needs its \emph{thresholds declared in advance}. Two are used: degradation
onset, the first stress at which median lateral error reaches one and a half
times its own nominal value or ground contact falls by five percentage points;
and functional breakdown, ground contact below $0.700$ or a safety termination
rate above $0.080$.

And it needs a \emph{selection rule that cannot favour the adaptive stack}. The
degradation curve is measured on \ppoIDFD{} alone, the non-adaptive baseline;
the stress levels are frozen from that curve; and only then are all three stacks
scored at those levels. \ppoAAMIDFD{} is never inspected while the levels are
chosen, so the condition cannot have been selected to flatter it.

That experiment has since been run on the command reduced-order model, on
IsaacLab and GPU hardware. It is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:authority-ladder}. It was not run on the other two
stacks, so the cross-stack freeze described in the preceding paragraph was not
exercised and no comparison between stacks is drawn from it.

What the \emph{original} sweep establishes is narrower and is stated in place
of the robustness claim it replaces. On \ppoROM{}, within a perturbation path
that cannot remove more than $62\,\%$ of actuator authority and over a sweep
whose upper half is progressively clamped, no degradation of task performance
was observed. On \ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMIDFD{} the sweep varied tool mass and
centre-of-mass height only, and neither degrades under a doubling of both.
That sweep locates no boundary on any stack, and \emph{the absence of an
observed boundary is not evidence that one is distant}.

\subsection{The Factors, and What They Represent}
\label{sec:eval:factor-meaning}

Before the replacement curve, the perturbation vector itself is worth setting
out in physical terms, because two of the six factors carry most of the
interpretive weight in this chapter and their names do not make their meaning
obvious. Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-meaning} gives the six, the quantity each
scales, the audited interval it is drawn from, and the effect each produces in
isolation on the reduced-order model.

The last column deserves care in reading. It reports the relative change in
cylinder extension when that factor alone is swept across its full audited
range, holding the other five at nominal, measured open-loop on the
reduced-order model. It ranks \emph{reach} within the identified envelope, and
it is not a statement about which factor a deployed controller is most
sensitive to, because the closed loop has absorbers the open-loop probe does
not (Section~\ref{sec:eval:authority-ladder}). Seal condition leads at
$25.9\,\%$ and supply pressure trails at $6.3\,\%$, a factor of four between
the strongest and weakest hydraulic channel.

deployed episode vary them. Any closed-loop statement in this chapter is
therefore about the four channels in the upper block of
Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-meaning} and about the two mechanical ones, and not
about hydraulic parameters in general.

\paragraph{The lateral criterion does not survive this axis.}
One of the two predeclared onset criteria had to be set aside, and the reason
is physical rather than convenient. Removing actuator authority makes the arm
\emph{stiller}: it commands less motion, so it deviates less, and every
deviation metric improves as the stress increases. The lateral column of
Table~\ref{tab:eval:authority-ladder} is lowest at $\alpha = 0.2$, where the
cutter is least able to follow the line. A criterion that fires on growing
deviation cannot detect a failure that manifests as absence of motion. Ground
contact, which falls when the cutter stops reaching the ground, does detect it,
and it is used as the primary metric for that reason. This is a post-hoc
narrowing of a predeclared pair and is reported as one; the onset and breakdown
levels below are contact-only.

\paragraph{The curve is non-monotonic, and that is the result.}
Contact does not fall as authority is removed. It rises, from $0.7913$ at
nominal to $0.9535$ at $\alpha = 0.2$, and only then declines, crossing the
breakdown floor at $\alpha \leq 0.01$. Contact-only degradation onset is
$\alpha = 0.005$. Read literally, \ppoROM{} tolerates the removal of roughly
$97\,\%$ of its actuator authority without measured degradation, and performs
\emph{better} at a fifth of nominal authority than at nominal.

The second half of that sentence is the informative half, and it is not a
robustness result. A controller that improves when its actuator is attenuated
is over-commanding at nominal: the policy is requesting more cylinder motion
than the task needs, and the attenuation is acting as a gain correction the
tuning did not apply. This is consistent with the excess control effort
reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort} and it is a finding about the
deployed policy rather than about the plant's envelope.

\paragraph{Two absorbers sit between the factors and the machine.}
The reason a plant perturbation is hard to see at the task level on this stack
is measurable, and it is not that the plant is insensitive. Two mechanisms
absorb it. The direction guard, at its deployment setting, reduces the
extension error a reference hydraulic stress produces from $\unit[93.16]{mm}$
with the guard off to $\unit[19.07]{mm}$ with it on, removing $79.5\,\%$ of the
plant change; and it reorders the factor ranking rather than merely attenuating
it, so a ranking measured with the guard off does not describe the deployed
system. Separately, with the learned residual heads disabled the reduced-order
model does not move at all --- zero travel and zero extension error at every
stress --- because training pinned effective mass and viscous friction at their
ceilings and effective area at its floor. The realised displacement of this
plant originates entirely in the learned heads.

That second observation carries a constraint on how the open-loop rankings may
be described. The ordering in Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-meaning} is a property
of a learned model whose physical parameters are at their bounds. It is not an
identified hydraulic sensitivity of the FME~500 and is not presented as one.

\paragraph{What this says about the deployment plant.}
Three statements are supported, and a fourth is explicitly not.

First, the axis reaches the plant. The instrumentation check records
$|\Delta x^{\text{applied}}| / |\Delta x^{\text{raw}}|$ equal to $1.0000$ at
$\alpha = 1$ and $0.0100$ at $\alpha = 0.01$, to four decimals, so the flatness
of the upper curve is not the delivery failure that invalidated the earlier
sweep. The endpoint behaves as predicted: at $\alpha \rightarrow 0$ contact
collapses to $0.6148$.

Second, the deployment plant is not authority-limited over any range a
plausible hydraulic fault would produce. A fault severe enough to remove
$97\,\%$ of realised actuator motion is not a degraded seal or a weak pump; it
is a disconnected actuator. Within the range the factor path can reach --- a
reduction of at most $62\,\%$, established above --- the ladder shows no
degradation whatever.

Third, the earlier flat curve is now explained rather than merely qualified.
Its flatness is consistent with a plant whose task-level response to authority
loss is genuinely flat until authority is nearly gone, combined with the two
absorbers above and the clamping of the hydraulic half of the sweep.

What is \emph{not} supported is a plant-robustness envelope. The plant was held
at nominal for every point of this ladder: the factor manifest was not supplied
to the run, so \verb|deployment_factor_enabled| was false throughout and the
sixteen held-out draws were never visited. This curve varies actuator authority
on a fixed plant. It is an actuator-authority axis and is captioned as one. The
envelope over plant variation --- the same ladder under the held-out combined
draws, and on the other two stacks --- remains unmeasured, and the boundary is
still not located.

\section{Identifiability of Plant Variation}
\label{sec:eval:identifiability}

Sections~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} to~\ref{sec:eval:robustness} answer RQ5 and
leave RQ6 standing. Both representations support closed-loop control on
held-out terrain, plants and trajectories, and neither degrades when the plant
is moved as far as this work can move it -- all without any online adaptation.
That is the setting in which adaptation has to earn its place, and the first
question is not whether an estimator is accurate but whether there is anything
in the data for it to recover. This section measures that, and then asks what
kind of quantity the recovered latent actually is.

\subsection{Excitation Design and the Identifiability Envelope}
\label{sec:eval:excitation}

What the data makes identifiable determines what any estimator can recover, so
it is measured before any estimator is judged, and the headline is that
excitation gathered under a task controller does not separate plants at all.

The reason is structural. A task controller computes its command from the
measured state, so command and response are close to redundant and little is
left for a plant descriptor to explain; the input must be rich enough for the
regressor to stay well conditioned~\cite{ljung1999system}, which makes
excitation design a first-class decision~\cite{schoukens2019nonlinear}. The
collection used throughout this chapter therefore removes the controller
entirely and applies open-loop
zero-mean square-wave excitation directly, at the cylinder-command interface
because the command reduced-order model accepts cylinder extensions and not
valve signals by design (Section~\ref{sec:models:rom}). This collection, \texttt{rom\_openloop\_v1}, comprises $118$ plant draws of
$11\,040$ control steps each, split $82/20/16$ by run into
training, validation and reserved test sets, and its Hankel matrix reaches full
rank $40$ of $40$ at a condition number of $17.8$.

The excitation sweeps four amplitude tiers. Table~\ref{tab:eval:identifiability}
reports what each tier makes identifiable, decoding the six true plant factors
from the per-tier mean latent estimate and scoring on held-out runs. The
seal-condition factor is the positive control throughout, since it is the one
factor the estimator is known to represent (Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content});
if it collapses, the decode is broken and no other entry in the row carries
information.

The obvious explanation for the collapse is actuator saturation, and it is
wrong. Saturation reaches only $9.98\%$ of steps even at the largest amplitude,
which cannot account for a decode falling from $R^2 = 0.703$ to zero. The
measurement that does account for it is operating-point drift. Every run
executes an identical excitation schedule, so at a matched tick the only thing
that differs between runs is the plant, and the across-run spread of cylinder
extension is a direct reading of how far the plants have desynchronised in
position. That spread grows from $1.5$\,mm at the start of the episode to
$54.2$\,mm at the end, reaching half of the stroke the probe itself produces.
Once runs are that far apart, a one-second observation window cannot separate a
response that differs because the seal is leaking from one that differs because
the cylinder is sitting five centimetres lower. The confound is an accumulated
history effect, and it is not one the estimator can be blamed for.

Two consequences follow, and both constrain later sections. First, all
adaptation experiments in this chapter are run below amplitude $0.35$, inside
the band where plant identity is present at all; an experiment run above it
would be testing the estimator on data that contains no answer. Second, the
excitation plan ramps amplitude monotonically across the episode, one tier per
quarter. That schedule is deliberate: it sweeps the full amplitude range inside
every run, so each plant draw contributes to all four tiers and the tiers are
compared on identical plants rather than across separate collections. The
consequence for interpretation follows from the design and is stated so that the
collection is read correctly: amplitude and episode time are the same axis here,
so this collection supports statements about amplitude and not about elapsed
time within an episode. A pilot analysis that read decode quality as decaying
over the episode was reading the amplitude sweep, and is recorded as such.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Data Against Excitation Data}
\label{sec:eval:closedloop-data}

The envelope above is a statement about data rather than about estimators, and
the sharpest demonstration of that is an architecture that changes sign when the
data changes. The latent-conditioned state predictor of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:id-sufficiency} lowers free-running error by a median of
$22.3\,\%$ on the open-loop collection. On the earlier collection gathered under
a task controller, the same architecture, the same objective and the same
selection criterion put the latent \emph{behind} its no-latent baseline: the
no-latent predictor scored $0.17278$, the end-to-end latent $0.17596$ and the
privileged latent $0.18515$, so plant conditioning appeared worthless or worse.

Nothing about the model changed between those two results. What changed was
whether the data contained an answer, exactly as the envelope above predicts:
under a task controller the command is computed from the state, so command and
response are close to redundant and the plant has little left to explain. Two
consequences follow for this chapter. Reporting only the closed-loop collection
would have supported a confident negative conclusion about the architecture that
the open-loop data contradicts, which is why every model-level experiment here
uses \texttt{rom\_openloop\_v1}. And an identifiability check of this kind is
cheap, requires no estimator, and belongs before rather than after the decision
to build one.

\subsection{Information Content of the Adaptive Latent}
\label{sec:eval:latent-content}

That the latent helps does not establish \emph{why}, and the two available
explanations carry different consequences for the thesis. Under the first, the
estimator recognises which plant it is operating on and the policy is
conditioned on a plant identity. Under the second, the estimator tracks a
time-varying operating condition and its value lies in the estimate moving.
H2 separates them with a substitution experiment that changes nothing but the
contents of the latent slot.

One trained model is scored three ways on the same data. In the \emph{online}
condition the latent is read from the observable history at every model tick,
as in deployment. In the \emph{constant} condition it is replaced by that run's
own mean estimate, which preserves the correct plant identity exactly and
destroys all variation. In the \emph{shuffled} condition it is replaced by
another run's mean, preserving the distribution of latent values while making
the identity wrong. The gap between online and constant measures what the
variation is worth; the gap between constant and shuffled measures what the
identity is worth.

second effect is consistently signed in all five seeds and so is real rather
than noise, but it is roughly thirty times smaller than the first. Essentially
all of the latent's value lies in the estimate moving, and almost none of it in
the estimate being correct about which plant this is. H2 is supported.

This licenses one claim and forbids another. The claim it forbids is that the
module identifies the plant it is operating on: at $0.43\%$, that reading is
very nearly false, and the thesis does not make it. The claim it supports is
that the module tracks a time-varying operating condition, and that the
tracking is what earns the performance.

This is the ordinary form of the claim rather than a weak one. The module
estimates from a fixed one-second causal window and carries no state between
ticks, so it re-estimates rather than accumulates: recursive estimation with a
forgetting factor, the standard construction when the estimated parameters are
themselves time-varying~\cite{astrom1995adaptive,ljung1999system}. A converging
estimator would lock onto a past operating condition and stop
responding~\cite{kumar2021rma}.

Two boundaries are worth stating before the closed-loop chapters. The first is
that this analysis concerns the jointly fine-tuned estimator trained on
open-loop excitation, which is a different model on different data from the
deployed estimator characterised in Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}; the
two disagree about stability, and the disagreement is informative rather than
contradictory. The deployed estimator, trained under a task controller, is
static within a run, whereas this one varies within an episode by roughly nine
times the spread between plants. Excitation decides which of the two behaviours
the estimator exhibits. The second boundary is that everything here is measured
on a dynamics model rather than in the control loop; H4 repeats the
substitution experiment on the closed-loop stack in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}, and only that result can carry the adaptation
claim into the task.

\section{Online Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation}

Plant identity is present in the data when the excitation is right, and what the
latent carries is a moving operating condition rather than a fixed label. This
section asks what that buys. It proceeds outward from the model to the machine:
first whether the latent improves a model that consumes it and whether the
estimate is as good as the truth, then whether the estimate follows the plant
when the plant moves, then whether either matters to the closed loop under a
plant change and under an oracle that removes estimator error altogether.

\subsection{The Model-Level Gain and the Frozen Inverse Model}
\label{sec:eval:id-sufficiency}

The inverse model is trained on randomised Simscape data and deployed against
the IsaacLab reduced-order plant, and the architecture of
Chapter~\ref{chap:control} depends on it staying frozen. H1 asks whether that
is defensible. The direct measurement of inverse-model transfer error is
reported in Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}; what is tested here is the
weaker but more consequential proposition that whatever residual gap remains is
learnable \emph{without touching the inverse model at all}.

The test removes the inverse model from the loop entirely. A state predictor
consumes the current state, the applied command and an estimated latent inferred
end to end from $[\jointpos, \jointvel, \dpress, \valvecmd]$, and predicts the
next state. The baseline is the same predictor with no latent input, retrained
on the identical data, split and objective, at five matched seeds.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:sufficiency} reports the pairs.

is accurate in any absolute sense. It shows that the residual mismatch between
the Simscape training distribution and the deployment plant is recoverable by a
latent-conditioned state model, which is what licenses the simpler architecture
in which the inverse model is never fine-tuned online.

\subsection{Estimated Against Privileged Latents}
\label{sec:eval:predictive-utility}

Section~\ref{sec:eval:latent-content} established that the latent's value is
temporal and Section~\ref{sec:eval:id-sufficiency} that it lowers free-running
error. Neither asked the question a controller cares about: is the \emph{estimated}
latent as useful as the true one, and is it better than no plant information at
all? That comparison is made here, on the frozen system-dynamics model, by
supplying four different latents to one network and changing nothing else.

The four arms are the privileged latent read from the true factors, the causal
AAM estimate, an in-distribution mean carrying no plant information, and a
shuffled latent with the right distribution and the wrong plant. All four are
scored on the same $36$ held-out runs, on free-running rollouts at one, ten and
twenty ticks, with the model integrating its own predictions.

\subsection{Response to a Change of Plant}
\label{sec:eval:plant-switch}

An estimator that identifies a fixed plant is not yet an adaptive one. What
remains is whether the estimate follows the plant when the plant moves. No
recorded run answers this, because each run holds one factor vector for its
whole length. The trajectory is therefore \emph{constructed}: two held-out runs
are spliced at a matched tick, which is admissible here because every run
executes the identical eleven-segment schedule, so at a matched tick the
commanded motion is the same and only the plant differs. The result is a
constructed sequence rather than a recorded one and is reported as such.

Eight pairs are used, chosen as the most separated in latent space among the
sixteen reserved runs, and each is spliced at its midpoint. In all eight the
estimate ends closer to the new plant than to the old one, and in all eight it
moves away from the old plant after the splice. The median time to cross, that
is for the estimate to become closer to the new plant than the old, is
$\unit[0.18]{s}$, and the median time to cover ninety per cent of the distance
between the two settled values is $\unit[0.70]{s}$. Both sit inside the
$\unit[1.0]{s}$ observation window, which is the shortest interval in which a
change can become fully visible to a causal estimator reading that window.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/ch7_plant_switch}
  \caption{Latent response to a constructed plant switch, for the two
  most separated held-out pairs. The distance from the estimate to the old
  plant's latent rises and the distance to the new plant's falls, with the
  crossing occurring inside the estimator's own $\unit[1.0]{s}$ observation

The estimator therefore re-identifies rather than merely classifying: it does
not carry a plant label fixed at the start of an episode but revises it from
history when the evidence changes.

\subsection{The Benefit Does Not Scale With Plant Deviation}
\label{sec:eval:no-scaling}

The natural expectation is that plant knowledge is worth more the further the
plant sits from nominal, and it is the expectation under which an adaptive
architecture is usually justified. It is not supported here. Ranking the $36$
held-out runs by the magnitude of their factor deviation and measuring the
latent's benefit as the paired gap between the mean-latent and estimated-latent
arms, the milder half of the population returns a mean benefit of
$2.53\times10^{5}$ and the more severe half $1.85\times10^{5}$, a ratio of
$0.73$. The correlation between deviation magnitude and benefit is $-0.143$.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.56\textwidth]{images/ch7_benefit_scaling}
  \caption{Latent benefit against plant deviation over the $36$ held-out runs,
  measured as the paired gap between the mean-latent and estimated-latent arms
  at the $\unit[1.0]{s}$ horizon. The dashed line is a least-squares fit. The
  benefit does not grow with deviation, which forecloses the reading that a
  closed-loop effect exists but only at large mismatch.}
  \label{fig:eval:benefit-scaling}
\end{figure}

The gain is therefore uniform across the plant family rather than concentrated
where the plant is hardest. This is measured at model level, where the closed
loop cannot absorb it, so it is a property of the latent rather than of the
controller. It also forecloses one reading of the closed-loop result of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:robustness}: the absence of a benefit under large plant
deviation is not a case of the controller hiding an effect that grows with
mismatch, because no such growth exists to hide.

\subsection{Response to a Mid-Episode Plant Change}
\label{sec:eval:ablations}

This experiment measures the disturbance envelope of the deployed stack: how
large a change in the plant it absorbs without loss of performance. The result
is a specification, and it states when a fixed controller suffices.

\paragraph{Design.}

The plant's valve effectiveness is scaled by $0.6$ at a known instant inside the
scored window. All arms share the policy, the mesh, the seed and the schedule,
and differ only in what occupies the latent slot.

The \emph{online} arm is the deployed system and the \emph{frozen} arm latches a
pre-change value, so it is identical up to the latch and then stops tracking;
beating it is the adaptation result. Three controls bound the reading:
\emph{shuffled} carries the right latent distribution and the wrong plant, which
rules out any benefit from extra input dimensions; \emph{mean} is in
distribution and carries no plant information; and \emph{sham} repeats the
online arm with the schedule firing and the plant left alone. \ppoIDFD{} is
scored under the same schedule as a robust reference.

A zeroed latent is deliberately not used as the no-information arm. Measured on
a real trace both channels sit at means of $-1.19$ and $-2.34$ with no positive
samples, so zero lies two to three standard deviations outside the distribution;
it would be an out-of-distribution arm wearing the label of an ablation. The
\emph{mean} arm does that job instead.

\paragraph{Effect of the perturbation.}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.86\textwidth]{images/ch7_e3_sham}
  \caption{Both runs use the same policy, mesh and schedule; in one the valve

  \label{fig:eval:sham}

Three measurements settle it, and they must be read in order.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The perturbation is real.} The recorded plant parameter falls
    from $0.893$ to $0.536$, a ratio of $0.600$ exactly as configured, at
    $9.0$\,s inside a window ending at $23.4$\,s.
  \item \textbf{The task does not notice it.} Over the post-change window the
    perturbed run records $95.3$\,mm of lateral error against $98.1$\,mm
    unperturbed: a difference of $-2.9\%$, in the wrong direction, and well
    inside the spread between cells.
  \item \textbf{The estimator does not detect it.} $\latenthat{}$ shifts by
    $0.496$ across the step when the plant changes, and by $0.473$ when it does
    not.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{A $40\,\%$ reduction in valve effectiveness moves neither the task nor
the estimate.} Tracking, cut height and ground contact all hold through a change
of that size. The tempting conclusion -- that no adaptation is required within
this envelope -- is not the one the experiment supports, because two properties
of the design bound what it could have shown.

The first is the size of the perturbation. A perturbation that leaves the task
within $2.9\,\%$ of its unperturbed value, in the wrong direction, has not
created an error for adaptation to recover; measurement (2) above is a statement
about the disturbance and not about the controller. A development measurement recorded in the evaluation harness puts a
transport-delay step from $2$ to $8$ control steps at $+22\,\%$ in lateral error
where this valve step costs a fraction of a per cent, so perturbations with a
measurable effect on this stack do exist. Injected delay is not the right one:
the $\unit[117]{ms}$ plant dead time is present throughout training, so a policy
that already compensates for it is being tested on a novel disturbance rather
than on adaptation (Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-plant}). The point stands
regardless of which knob is chosen -- the perturbation has to be sized against a
measured degradation curve before an adaptation arm can recover anything.

The second is the choice of factor. Valve effectiveness acts on flow, and the
deployed estimator barely represents flow: linear decoding from the latent gives
$R^2 = 0.114$ for flow against $0.956$ for seal condition
(Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}). This experiment therefore asks an
estimator that encodes seal condition to detect and compensate a sudden change
in flow, which is not the strongest test of the adaptation path that the
available factors permit.

The obvious correction -- perturb seal instead -- is not available on this
stack, and why not is itself a result. Seal enters the plant only as an input to
the command reduced-order model, and \ppoAAMIDFD{} runs the inverse/forward
pair, which takes no factor input at all
(Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-reach}). \emph{The factor the estimator identifies
best cannot be perturbed where the controller runs, and the surface that can be
perturbed there carries the factor it identifies worst.} That is a property of
the architecture as built, not of the estimator, and it bounds what any
mid-episode experiment on this stack could have shown.

Two experiments follow from it, and they are specified in place of the single
one this section reports. The first is \emph{native}: tool mass steps
mid-episode, a property that genuinely exists in the deployed simulation and
reaches every stack unclamped through the physics engine, with no emulation
anywhere. The estimator may well not benefit, since mass is the factor it
decodes worst, and that is a legitimate outcome rather than a failed
experiment. The second is a \emph{positive control aligned with the estimator}:
a seal-associated \emph{gain proxy}, reproducing the amplitude half of the
reduced-order model's seal law as a scaling of realised actuator motion,
$\alpha_{\mathrm{eq}}(w) = (1 + 0.625\,w)^{-2}$. It is deliberately not called
seal degradation: it omits the leakage term and the longer valve time constant
that accompany seal condition in the training domain, and reproduces only the
amplitude effect. It answers the narrower question of whether the adaptation
path can exploit a change along the direction the estimator demonstrably
learned. Both draw their magnitude from the degradation curve of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design}, measured on the non-adaptive stack, so
that the perturbation is sized to leave recovery headroom rather than chosen
against the adaptive stack's own response. Neither is run here.

What the experiment does establish stands: the perturbation was applied
($0.893 \rightarrow 0.536$, exactly as configured), the sham arm shows the
post-change rise in error occurs equally without any plant change, and no arm
separates. That is a null with an identified cause, and the cause is the
perturbation rather than the controller or the estimator.

One methodological point generalises beyond this experiment. Whole-episode means
cannot express recovery: an arm that recovers in two seconds and one that never
recovers differ by a few per cent once averaged over a $23$-second window, which
is what the arms above report. The replacement experiments are scored on the
transient instead -- integrated absolute error over $[t_c, t_c+1]$,
$[t_c+1, t_c+3]$ and $[t_c+3, t_c+5]$, peak error within three seconds, and the
time to re-enter and hold a band around the pre-change level, with
\emph{not recovered} reported as an outcome rather than a missing value.

\paragraph{The envelope boundary.}

A perturbation reveals the edge of the envelope only if it degrades performance.
Transport delay does not qualify: the $117$\,ms dead time is present throughout
training, so the policies already accommodate it. A step in the \emph{commanded}
line produces a measurable response on every stack
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response}) and is the instrument for that
measurement.

\subsection{An Oracle Reference for Plant-Identity Conditioning}
\label{sec:eval:oracle}

One question decides whether building an estimator will pay: would perfect
knowledge of the plant help this closed loop? It is answerable directly, by
supplying the true factors through the privileged encoder, and the answer is
obtained without training an estimator. The result is an oracle reference
\emph{within this encoder, adapter and policy}, not a universal bound on what
plant knowledge could ever be worth: a different representation or a different
downstream consumer could use the same information differently.

One property of these arms should be read before their scores. They differ only
in what occupies the two latent channels of the actor observation, and they
differ measurably, which establishes that the deployed policy consumes the
latent rather than ignoring it. That is worth stating because it is the
precondition for everything else in this section: a null from an actor
indifferent to $\latenthat$ would say nothing about plant knowledge, whereas a
null from an actor demonstrably sensitive to it is a statement about the loop.
It is also why the deployed estimate can finish \emph{worse} than an
uninformative constant, since only an input that is used can do harm; that
ordering is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:eval:ceiling}.

The plant is drawn per episode from the held-out factor set and is not perturbed
mid-episode, so plant identity is a real and stable property of each run. Four
latents are compared on the adaptive stack: the deployed estimate, a
\emph{privileged} latent read from the true factors by the frozen encoder, an
in-distribution \emph{mean} carrying no plant information, and a
\emph{shuffled} latent with the right distribution and the wrong plant.
\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} are scored under the same factor domain.

against their own rows rather than against each other.

\paragraph{The oracle result.}

Knowing the plant exactly is worth $-3.4\%$ in lateral error and $-4.5\%$ in cut
height, and neither survives its sign test: the oracle beats the
no-information arm in $18$ and $19$ of $33$ cells respectively, which is
indistinguishable from chance. A latent read directly from the true factors
cannot outperform a latent carrying no plant information at all.

\textbf{Within this conditioning architecture, estimator accuracy is not the
limiting factor}: a latent read from the true factors produces only a small
improvement that the sample cannot resolve, so effort spent improving the
\AAM{} would not have been repaid here. Run before an estimator is trained, this
measurement redirects that effort. Whether it generalises to other
latent-conditioned designs is a hypothesis this experiment cannot test, because
it varies the latent inside one fixed encoder, adapter and policy.

\paragraph{Scope.}

Three limits belong with these numbers, and the first is the most consequential.
The privileged latent is read from all six true factors, but four of them --
flow, compliance, seal condition and supply pressure -- do not act on the
inverse/forward plant this stack runs on
(Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-reach}). What separates one held-out draw from
another here is tool mass and centre-of-mass height, and those are the two
factors the latent represents worst, at $R^2 = -0.008$ and $0.056$
(Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}). The oracle arm therefore supplies exact
knowledge of four quantities the plant ignores and a poor encoding of the two it
does not. That is a bound on what this measurement could have shown: it is
evidence that plant identity does not help \emph{through this latent, on this
plant}, and it is not the general statement that perfect plant knowledge is
worth nothing to this controller. Re-running it on a plant that consumes the
hydraulic factors, or with a latent that encodes the mechanical ones, are both
open.

Two further limits belong with these numbers. The oracle arm necessarily differs from
the others in the adapter as well as the latent, because an adapter must be
trained for the latent it consumes and one trained on estimated latents refuses
a privileged one; the $-3.4\%$ is therefore a comparison across two changes
rather than one. And $33$ cells resolve only large effects, so the flat ceiling
bounds the available benefit rather than proving it exactly zero. What the
experiment establishes is that the benefit is not large enough to find with the
sample this chapter uses, under conditions chosen to favour it.

\section{Why the Model-Level Gain Does Not Reach the Controller}
\label{sec:eval:ceiling}

The evidence assembled so far contains a discrepancy that has to be accounted
for rather than reported. At model level the latent is worth a median of
$22.3\,\%$ in free-running state error, its time-variation alone is worth
$13.6\,\%$, and the estimate is statistically indistinguishable from a
privileged latent read off the true factors. In the closed loop none of that
appears: the paired intervals against \ppoIDFD{} span zero
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}), a mid-episode plant change is absorbed
without any arm separating (Section~\ref{sec:eval:ablations}), and an oracle
latent beats an uninformative one in $18$ of $33$ cells, which is chance
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}).

Four explanations are available and they are not equally supported. This
section takes them in order of how directly they are measured: the ceiling
itself, the plant information the baseline already receives, the two roles the
architecture asks one latent to play, and the time constants of the loop that
consumes it.

\subsection{The Ceiling Bounds the Estimator Out of the Question}
\label{sec:eval:ceiling-first}

The first explanation is the one that removes an entire class of alternatives.
A better estimator cannot recover the model-level gain, because the estimator is
not what limits the closed loop: supplying the \emph{true} factors through the
privileged encoder is worth $-3.4\,\%$ in lateral error and $-4.5\,\%$ in cut
height, and neither survives its sign test
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}). Whatever separates the model-level result from
the closed-loop one therefore lies downstream of the latent's accuracy, in what
the controller does with plant information rather than in how well that
information is recovered.

One qualification bounds how far that reading travels, and it is the reason this
section reports four explanations rather than one. The oracle is flat in part
because most of what it knows is inert on this plant: four of the six factors do
not enter the inverse/forward dynamics at all, and the two that do are the two
the latent encodes worst (Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-reach}). A flat ceiling
measured under those conditions bounds the benefit available \emph{here} without
establishing that plant knowledge is generally worthless to a controller of this
kind. The three explanations that follow concern the downstream path and are
measured independently of that qualification.

\subsection{The Baseline Is Not Plant-Blind}

Part of the explanation precedes the estimator entirely. The robust stacks
receive the observable consequence of plant variation through the twelve
model-error channels of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo}, which carry the actuator's
standing residual under whichever plant is active. That residual is what the
policy corrects against, and it differs by plant. Conditioning on an estimated
latent therefore does not supply the first plant-dependent signal the controller
has seen; it adds an identity label on top of a consequence the policy was
already acting on. Where the consequence is already observable and the
controller already responds to it, naming the plant that produced it has less
left to contribute, which is consistent with the size of the oracle result in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:oracle}.

\subsection{The Two Consumers of the Latent}

The ranking of the four arms identifies a property of the architecture rather
than a deficiency of the estimator. The deployed estimate is the worst of the
four, by $20.7\%$ on lateral error and $39.3\%$ on cut height against the mean
latent: a genuine but imperfect estimate performs worse than a constant carrying
no information, and worse than one that is actively wrong about the plant. That
ordering is only explicable if what separates the arms is not their information
content, and the paragraph below establishes that it is not.

The mechanism follows from Section~\ref{sec:eval:latent-content}, and it is the
same property seen from the other side. The estimate varies within an episode by
about nine times the spread between plants. The mean and shuffled arms supply a
perfectly smooth signal; the deployed arm supplies one that moves continuously.
The arms therefore differ in two respects, information and variability, and on
this evidence the second dominates: the policy is better served by a constant it
can ignore than by a signal that moves under it.

That is not a contradiction of the model-level result but its complement. In
Section~\ref{sec:eval:latent-content} the same time-variation was worth
$13.6\%$ to a one-step state predictor, which consumes the latent afresh at
every tick and benefits from its responsiveness. The deployed actor is a
memoryless MLP and does not integrate anything itself; what accumulates is the
closed-loop trajectory, because a latent that moves under the actor changes its
action from tick to tick and the plant carries the consequences forward. The
same quantity is therefore plausibly informative to a one-step predictor and
disruptive to a feedback loop, and the two roles were never separated in the
architecture: one latent is routed to both. The arms differ in information and
in variability at once, so this reading is the mechanism the evidence favours
rather than one this experiment isolates. Section~\ref{sec:eval:bandwidth}
separates the two.

\subsection{Latent Bandwidth on the Policy Path}
\label{sec:eval:bandwidth}

The arms of Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle} change what the latent knows and how
fast it moves at the same time, so they cannot say which of the two costs the
task anything. This section holds the content fixed and varies only the speed. A
one-pole low-pass with time constant $\tau$ is applied to the latent on the
policy path alone; the inverse-model adapter keeps the full-rate estimate in
every arm. The checkpoint, the plant draw and the $33$ cells are those of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}, and no network is retrained.

Table~\ref{tab:eval:bandwidth} gives the contrast that resolves, and it is the
one closed-loop result in this chapter that does so in favour of a design
change. Slowing the latent from $\tau = \unit[0.032]{s}$ to $\unit[0.796]{s}$
improves cut-height tracking by $14.5\%$ on the median as well as $26.2\%$ on
the mean, ahead in $22$ of $33$ cells at $p = 0.015$, on a metric fixed before
the sweep was run. Lateral error moves the same way and does not resolve. The
speed at which the latent is allowed to move therefore changes closed-loop
tracking, at fixed content and with no network retrained, which is the
prediction the two-consumers reading makes.

Three limits belong with that result. Against the \emph{unfiltered} arm every
filtered arm improves the mean by $11$ to $46\,\%$ while the medians stay flat
and no Wilcoxon test reaches $p < 0.10$; panel~(c) shows why, since a minority
of cells carry the mean. The mechanism is also not confirmed: the temporal
variance of the latent as the actor reads it is $0.277$ unfiltered and between
$0.270$ and $0.288$ across every filtered arm, with no ordering in $\tau$, so
the latent's variation is mostly slower than the cutoffs applied and the
proposed cause remains unmeasured. And the sweep varies one input to one
checkpoint that was trained on the unfiltered signal, so the optimum may be a
property of that training rather than of the architecture.

What survives is narrow and is stated as such: on this checkpoint, the speed at
which the latent is allowed to move changes cut-height tracking, and the best
setting is neither the raw estimate nor a constant.

\subsection{The Loop Is Slower Than Anything the Latent Changes}
\label{sec:eval:timescales}

The last explanation is a matter of time constants, and it is the one that
generalises beyond this architecture. The measurements of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay} place four rates on the same axis. The estimator
re-reads its window and issues a new latent every $\unit[50]{ms}$. The
hydraulics answer a valve command in $\unit[117]{ms}$, amplitude-invariant and
with an exact kinematic control. The command rate limit alone puts a
$\unit[417]{ms}$ floor under a $\unit[100]{mm}$ step, three times the hydraulic
dead time. And the stacks themselves settle such a step in $2.2$ to
$\unit[3.2]{s}$, five to eight times that floor.

The latent therefore moves one to two orders of magnitude faster than the
quantity it is trying to influence. Whatever it tells the actor about the plant,
the actor's authority over the tool is exercised through a rate-limited command
and a loop whose response is measured in seconds, so a plant change arrives, is
observed through the model-error channels, and is corrected long before
identifying \emph{which} plant produced it could shorten the correction. It also predicts that a slow disturbance
will be rejected without identification, which is consistent with -- though not
established by -- the unmoved task under a $40\,\%$ loss of valve effectiveness
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:ablations}), a perturbation too small to separate the two
explanations.

The four explanations are consistent with one another and with the data, and
they differ in standing. The ceiling and the baseline's existing plant
information are measured directly. The two-consumers reading is the mechanism
the evidence favours, and the bandwidth sweep is its one direct test, which
resolves on cut height and not on lateral error. The timescale argument is an
account of why the ceiling is flat rather than an independent measurement, and
it is the part of this explanation that would transfer to another machine as a
prediction rather than as a result.
\section{Integrated Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

The two research questions of this chapter are answered separately below,
because the evidence behind them differs in kind: RQ5 rests on a controlled
single-variable pair and RQ6 on within-checkpoint substitutions and a
pre-estimator ceiling.

\subsection*{RQ5: What the Actuator Representation Changes}

\emph{How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking, command
behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?}

It changes the controller substantially, and the direction is not uniform across
metrics. On the matched pair, driving the plant through the frozen inverse and
forward models rather than the command reduced-order model lowers mean lateral
error from $\unit[0.1026]{m}$ to $\unit[0.0703]{m}$ and cut-height error from
$8.4$ to $\unit[6.3]{mm}$, and it holds the lateral criterion in $32$ of $33$
(mesh, family) cells against $30$ of $33$
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}). The command behaviour behind that is the more
informative result. \ppoROM{} spends the \emph{least} actuation of the three and
tracks worst, while reversing the direction of motion $40$ times a second
against $10.6$ for \ppoIDFD{}, so its actuation is largely spent fighting itself
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort}); that also accounts mechanically for its
tool-acceleration and impact figures, which would otherwise read as unrelated
observations. Representation therefore buys tracking by buying smoothness, not
by buying effort.

Latency separates the representations in the hydraulic half of the chain and not
the mechanical one, where all three stacks read the one-step resolution floor
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-stacks}). But the ordering there does not survive
into the task: a shorter path from command to cylinder does not produce a
quicker response at the tool, because the response to a commanded change is set
by the command rate limit and the policy's own dynamics rather than by the
actuation chain (Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response}). This is the most
transferable finding of the RQ5 strand, and it is the reason a faster actuator
path is not by itself a design goal on a machine of this class.

Two findings belong beside that comparison even though they are not attributable
to the representation. \ppoIDFD{} fails the head-alignment gate badly, at
$0.2247$ with $-0.855$ on one mesh, which is the collapse under unconstrained
fine-tuning seen against the acceptance contract; the adaptive stack is the only
one of the three to hold alignment, at $0.9763$, and it pays for its ground
contact in hard impacts and tool acceleration
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}). And the lateral gate is exceeded by all three
stacks on worst-family statistics, on a different family in each case, which
makes that threshold a statement about the hardest trajectory in the library
rather than a discriminator between architectures. No stack dominates the
scorecard.

Robustness does not separate the representations at all, and both are robust in
the strong sense. Every closed-loop figure in this chapter is measured on
held-out meshes, on eleven trajectory families scored individually, and on
plants drawn from sixteen reserved factor configurations disjoint from training
by construction, of which the mechanical half reaches every stack and the
hydraulic half only \ppoROM{} (Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}); underneath the policy the frozen inverse model was identified
on Simscape alone and scores flat to a factor of $1.09$ across domains on $64$
IsaacLab runs outside its training distribution
(Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}). Generalisation across simulator, plant draw, terrain and trajectory is
therefore established for both representations, and established without online
adaptation.

Robustness \emph{beyond} that held-out population is a weaker claim than this
work can make, and weaker than an earlier reading of the sweep suggested. No
stack's tracking moves across the randomisation sweep, but on \ppoIDFD{} and
\ppoAAMIDFD{} that sweep did not vary the hydraulics at all -- the
inverse/forward plant takes no factor input, so only tool mass and
centre-of-mass height act -- and on \ppoROM{}, where they do act, the upper half
of the sweep is progressively pinned to the training envelope and the path
cannot remove more than $62\,\%$ of actuator authority in any case
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). The correct reading is that no
boundary was located within a bounded instrument, not that the stacks are
insensitive to plant variation. Locating the boundary is specified in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design} and left to future work.

\subsection*{RQ6: Whether Online Adaptation Pays}

\emph{Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable from
recent observations, and does online adaptation by latent identification improve
closed-loop performance?}

The first half is answered yes, with a condition. Plant identity is recoverable
from observable history, but only from data that excites the plant: on open-loop
excitation a latent-conditioned predictor lowers free-running error by a median
of $22.3\,\%$ in $5$ of $5$ seeds, while on data gathered under a task
controller the identical architecture finishes behind its own no-latent baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:closedloop-data}). Even within the open-loop collection
the recoverable band is narrow: the seal-condition decode falls from
$R^2 = 0.703$ to zero between the second and third amplitude tier, and the cause
is operating-point drift rather than saturation
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:excitation}). Inside that band the estimator is not the
weak link -- it is statistically indistinguishable from a privileged latent at
every prediction horizon and re-identifies a constructed plant switch within its
own observation window (Sections~\ref{sec:eval:predictive-utility}
and~\ref{sec:eval:plant-switch}). What it recovers is a moving operating
condition rather than a plant label: freezing the estimate at its own correct
per-episode value costs $13.6\,\%$, and making that frozen value wrong costs a
further $0.43\,\%$, a factor of thirty-two
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:latent-content}).

The second half is answered no, under the conditions tested, and the null is
bounded rather than merely observed. Every paired closed-loop interval spans
zero, and an oracle latent read from the true factors is worth $-3.4\,\%$ in
lateral error at $18$ of $33$ cells, which is chance. The mid-episode plant
change carries less weight than the other two: a $40\,\%$ reduction in valve
effectiveness passes unnoticed by the task, by the estimator and by an
unperturbed sham arm, but it perturbs a factor the estimator barely encodes and
by an amount too small to create recoverable error, so it bounds the
perturbation rather than the adaptation path
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:ablations}). That last measurement is the load-bearing one, because it is
obtained \emph{before} an estimator is built and shows that estimator accuracy
was never the constraint (Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}).

Three determinants explain the null and were separated in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:ceiling}. The baseline is not plant-blind: the twelve
model-error channels already carry the observable consequence of plant
variation, so conditioning adds an identity label on top of a consequence the
policy is already correcting. The architecture routes one latent to two
consumers with opposite requirements -- a one-step predictor rewarded for
responsiveness and a memoryless actor in a feedback loop that the same
responsiveness disturbs -- and the bandwidth sweep, which holds content fixed
and varies only speed, improves cut-height tracking by $14.5\,\%$ on the median
at $p = 0.015$ with no network retrained. And the loop's own time constants
leave adaptation little to do: a latent updating every $\unit[50]{ms}$ acts on a
controller whose step response is floored at $\unit[417]{ms}$ by the command
rate limit and settles in seconds.

The design that follows is to hold or filter the latent on the policy path while
leaving it responsive on the model path. It is specified here and not tested
beyond the single sweep of Section~\ref{sec:eval:bandwidth}.

\subsection*{Outcome of the Declared Hypotheses}

H1 is supported: an estimated latent lowers free-running state error in $5$ of
$5$ matched seeds, at a median of $-22.3\,\%$ with a paired interval excluding
zero, so the residual gap between the Simscape training distribution and the
deployment plant is recoverable without touching the inverse model, which is
what the frozen-inverse architecture requires. H2 is supported: the estimate's
time-variation is worth $13.6\,\%$ and its plant identity $0.43\,\%$, both
consistently signed across seeds, so the latent is a time-varying operating
condition and not a plant label. H3 returns an interval rather than a verdict,
and the interval is informative on ground contact -- less than about $2.4$
percentage points either way -- while on the two tracking errors it is
comparable to or wider than the baseline mean itself, so the effect is bounded
only loosely and equivalence is not established. H4 is supported in the sense
that matters practically and refuted in the sense that was hoped for: the
closed-loop effect \emph{was} boundable before an estimator was built, and the
bound it returned was flat, since a privileged latent read from the true factors
does not beat an uninformative one at better than chance.

\subsection*{Relation to the Research Gap}

Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals} positioned this work against history-based
adaptation methods that infer a low-dimensional plant code and condition a policy
on it. This chapter locates two conditions those arguments leave implicit. A
plant latent earns its place when it feeds a consumer that benefits from its
responsiveness, and when the plant variation is large enough for identity to be
worth having. Both are measurable before an estimator is trained: the
identifiability envelope of Section~\ref{sec:eval:excitation} establishes the
first and the oracle ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle} the second.
Together they form a pre-deployment protocol, and that is the contribution of
this chapter.

A third condition follows from the results taken together, and
it reconciles the null reported here with the gains that motivate the method.
Rapid motor adaptation and its descendants are demonstrated on legged robots,
where the plant changes discontinuously at contact, the correction window is a
fraction of a gait cycle, and the reported gains are success rates on terrain
that makes the unconditioned baseline fall
\cite{lee2020learning,kumar2021rma}. In that setting feedback cannot substitute
for identification, because the consequence of an unknown plant arrives before
the loop can measure and correct it. The present system is the opposite case.
Its plant is fixed within an episode, its actuator is heavily damped and in
sustained contact, its task response is set by a command rate limit three times
the hydraulic dead time, and it settles over seconds
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-synthesis}). A loop with that much time observes
the plant's effect through the model-error channels of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:ppo} and corrects it, so the identity behind the effect
adds little.

The condition can therefore be stated as a criterion rather than as a property
of this machine: \emph{latent conditioning pays where plant variation acts
faster than the loop can observe and correct it}. That criterion covers both
the locomotion results and the null reported here, and it is the boundary the
adaptation literature reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals} generally
leaves unstated. It also predicts where this estimator would pay on the present
machine, since Section~\ref{sec:eval:predictive-utility} shows the latent
improving a model asked to predict before observing, which is the quantity a
predictive controller consumes and a reactive one does not.
\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:eval:validity}

\subsection*{The Plant as a Learned Model}

The deployment plant is the command reduced-order model driving PhysX, and
\ppoROM{}'s actuator \emph{is} that model. It is therefore a reference condition
rather than a third competing approximator, and its advantages should not be
read as an approximation result. The reduced-order model was calibrated against
PhysX using parked shapes rather than the moving-verge task, so the calibration
is weakest exactly where the cutter meets the ground, and the inverse and
forward models received no comparable calibration.

\subsection*{An Unmeasured Input}

The differential pressure the adaptive module takes as an input is produced by
the actuator model and integrated at every tick, not sensed
(Section~\ref{sec:sys:sensing}). Within this simulation that is legitimate,
because the model is the plant. On hardware it would require pressure
transducers the FME~500 does not carry, and this is the channel where the
module's measured advantage is concentrated. Any transfer of the model-level
result therefore depends on instrumentation that is currently absent. No
sensor-noise or quantisation model is applied anywhere in this work, so all
observations are exact.

This leaves one question about the observation contract genuinely open, and it
is carried here as a limitation rather than presented as settled. Every result
in this work uses the pressure-inclusive observation row, in which measured
differential pressure is one of the four signal groups the module consumes. A
stricter alternative contract was specified alongside it, excluding
differential pressure precisely because a deployed machine cannot supply it, and
the choice between the two was never resolved for deployment. The cost of the
stricter contract has since been measured. Retraining the estimator on the
pressure-excluded row, under a matched budget and an otherwise identical
dataset, split, objective and seed, raises the latent error by $3.93\,\%$ on
validation and $3.41\,\%$ on the reserved test split, worse in both of two
seeds. The deployable variant is therefore worse, as expected, but by a margin
that does not disqualify it: removing the one channel carrying most of the
hydraulic information costs a few per cent rather than the estimator's
usefulness. Three limits belong with that figure. It is measured at model level
and says nothing directly about task tracking; it rests on two seeds rather than
the five used elsewhere in this chapter; and it uses a twenty-epoch budget
rather than the schedule that produced the deployed checkpoint, so the absolute
errors sit above those reported in Section~\ref{sec:models:aam} while the paired
difference is the quantity of interest.

\subsection*{Resolution of the Evidence}

The closed-loop comparisons use $33$ (mesh, family) cells and bound differences
between architectures at a few per cent; they do not establish equivalence. The
evaluation is deterministic, so the replication unit is the cell and not the
seed, and a sign count over evaluation seeds would report one observation
several times.

Two experiments bound their disturbance rather than the architecture, and one of
them bounds it more tightly than its design intended. The randomisation sweep
found no breakdown, but the plant does not receive what the sweep requests. On
the inverse/forward plant the four hydraulic factors are not consumed at all, so
for two of the three stacks the sweep varied tool mass and centre-of-mass height
only; on the command reduced-order plant they are consumed but clamped to the
identified range above scale $1$, and no more than $62\,\%$ of actuator
authority can be removed through that path
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). This affects the oracle experiment as
well as the sweep: the privileged latent supplies four factors the deployed
plant ignores alongside the two it does not, which bounds what that measurement
could have shown (Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle}). The mid-episode plant
change is a real perturbation but a small one, applied to a factor the
estimator represents poorly. The replacement experiments are specified against a
stress axis that reaches every stack -- Equation~\eqref{eq:eval:authority} --
rather than against the factor set, and their results are not part of this
thesis. Neither licenses a robustness claim, and
neither licenses the converse claim that the plant cannot be degraded: what they
establish is that the perturbations available in this work are mild and, in the
sweep's case, partly bounded by the plant model itself. The sweep also stretches
recorded factor draws rather than sampling new ones, so it says nothing about
combinations the manifest never visited.

\subsection*{Conditions Not Identical Across Stacks}

The three stacks are matched at evaluation but not in training. \ppoROM{} and
\ppoIDFD{} come from runs whose manifests agree on every recorded field; the
adaptive stack is a different run, chosen for the reason in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:horizon}, and it differs from that pair in three recorded
respects beyond the latent itself: a different seed ($41$, with terrain seed
$41$), an actor learning rate two orders of magnitude lower
($3\times10^{-6}$) and no frozen-actor warm-up at all ($0$ against $150$
iterations). Those settings were not arbitrary. Each stack was trained under the
best configuration found for it during development, and the adaptive stack's
lower learning rate reflects a stability judgement specific to an actor that
consumes a latent moving within the episode; that rationale is the author's
engineering judgement, since the manifests store the settings and not the
reasoning behind them. One field is missing where it would be most useful: the
\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} manifests do not record the truck-speed schedule at
all, while the adaptive run records $2$ to $\unit[7]{km/h}$ sampled per episode,
so it is the reference pair rather than the adaptive run that is undocumented in
this respect. Its absence does not differentiate the matched pair, whose other
fields agree, and the evaluation speed is pinned identically for all three. The oracle arm of Section~\ref{sec:eval:oracle} additionally
uses a different adapter checkpoint, because an adapter must be trained for the
latent it consumes, so that contrast spans two changes rather than one.

\subsection*{Quantities Not Recoverable from Operational Data}

Closed-loop delay compensation is not measurable from a normal drive: reference
and achieved path are coherent only below $0.3$\,Hz, where phase reflects
controller dynamics rather than transport, so Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response}
injects a step instead. The valve's dynamic model is likewise not identifiable in
either dataset, although its transport latency is.

\subsection*{Coverage}

The factor design is finite and sparse: $118$ runs with $16$ reserved
configurations, six factors, and an operating envelope that does not cover the
full machine. Excitation amplitude is swept monotonically within each run of the
open-loop collection by design, so that collection supports statements about
amplitude rather than about elapsed time within an episode. All
results are simulation-to-simulation; the Simscape-to-IsaacLab abstraction gap
is characterised in Chapter~\ref{chap:models} but the simulation-to-reality gap
is not addressed anywhere in this work.
