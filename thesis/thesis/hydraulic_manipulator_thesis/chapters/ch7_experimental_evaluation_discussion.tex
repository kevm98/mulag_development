\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:eval:hypotheses}

This chapter evaluates the three control stacks assembled in
Chapter~\ref{chap:control} and the learned actuator models they act through. It
answers the last two research questions of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions}:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ5] How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking,
    command behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?
  \item[RQ6] Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable
    from recent observations, and does online adaptation conditioned on that
    history improve closed-loop performance?
\end{description}

The two differ in kind. RQ5 asks what a design choice changes and is answered by
direct measurement on the matched pair \ppoROM{} (R) and \ppoIDFD{} (IF), which
agree on every recorded training field and differ only in the actuator path. RQ6
tests whether adaptation improves control and states its failure conditions in
advance, so it is carried by predeclared hypotheses and within-checkpoint
substitutions rather than by the position of \ppoAAMFD{} (AF) in a table. RQ6
decomposes into a chain of dependent questions, each worth testing only if the
one before it holds: is plant identity present in the data at all; can an
estimator recover it; does it improve a model that consumes it; and does that
improvement reach the controller? The first three links are settled before this
chapter, by the excitation collection of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} and the model-level results of
Chapter~\ref{chap:models}. Only the last is a question about the machine, and it
is answered in Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}.

\subsection{Test Conditions and Experimental Matrix}
\label{sec:eval:matrix}

Every closed-loop experiment scores exactly three rows -- \ppoROM{},
\ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMFD{} -- under one common condition: three held-out
terrain meshes, the eleven trajectory families of the library, a pinned truck
speed of $\unit[4]{km/h}$, a $\unit[26]{m}$ scored window, $32$ environments and
a fixed evaluation seed. The one model-level experiment here, the dead-time
measurement of Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-plant}, uses the open-loop
excitation collection \texttt{rom\_openloop\_v1} of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}.
Everything scored here is held out: the meshes are unseen, the families are
scored individually rather than pooled, and the plant is drawn from sixteen
reserved factor configurations whose keys are disjoint from training and
validation by construction (Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}). A scored run is
therefore a plant the stack has never seen, not merely an unseen episode. How
much of that factor draw reaches each stack's plant is audited in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}.
Table~\ref{tab:eval:matrix} lists every experiment with its arms and its
replication unit.

\subsection{Metrics, Statistics and Fairness}
\label{sec:eval:metrics}

Closed-loop performance is scored on mean absolute lateral error and mean
absolute cut-height error, with ground contact, hard impacts, head alignment and
tool acceleration reported alongside. The two errors are never pooled: they
differ by an order of magnitude in scale and are traded against one another by
some arms. Model-level experiments share one estimand, free-running accumulated
normalised state error over a five-tick horizon with no teacher forcing, because
a one-step error flatters every arm equally and hides the compounding that
separates them.

Metrics and seeds are fixed before each run. Comparisons are paired, report the
sign count beside the mean, and take their intervals from a paired bootstrap.
Selection uses the validation split; reserved test runs are scored once.

The unit of replication differs by experiment, and getting it wrong would
inflate the evidence. Model-level experiments replicate over training seeds. The
closed-loop evaluation is deterministic. Five evaluation seeds returned
bit-identical numbers to nine decimal places, so its unit is the (mesh,
family) cell, and metrics existing only per mesh are marked $n=3$.

Several controls run alongside the experiments, each added because something it
guards against had already gone wrong. Input ablation guards against a model
scoring well without command authority, and caught a forward model at one-step
$R^2\approx0.999$ that was insensitive to the valve. Open-loop excitation guards
against command and state being made redundant by feedback. A determinism check
guards against reporting one observation several times. Full-path re-scoring
guards against a selection window certifying part of an episode, the default
covering $\unit[8.9]{m}$ of $\unit[26]{m}$. Retrained baselines guard against
comparing numbers across collections, whose normalisers differ, and per-family
resolution against a worst-family aggregate hiding a threshold artefact. The
matched-pair design holds every recorded training field equal, and the
capacity-matched control guards against crediting sensing for what extra
parameters buy.

Two gaps in that scheme are stated rather than left to be inferred. The matched
pair holds the manifest equal but not the optimisation: one training run
represents each architecture, so a difference between R and IF is attributable
to the actuator path only under the untested assumption that a second run of
either would land in the same place. The $33$ cells measure variation across
terrain and trajectory, not across training, and every comparative statement in
this chapter carries that scope. Second, the adaptation experiment carries a
sham arm, in which the flip schedule fires but the load never moves. It is
subtracted rather than read as a gate, which is what isolates the component that
scales with the plant change (Section~\ref{sec:eval:dose}).


\subsection{Comparative Scope of the Three Stacks}
\label{sec:eval:standing}

\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} agree on every recorded manifest field, including seed,
terrain seed, environments, stage, learning rate, entropy coefficient, frozen
normaliser and warm-up. They differ only in the actuator path. A difference
between them is attributable to the actuator representation, and that pair
answers RQ5.

\ppoAAMFD{} is not a third column in the tables that follow, for the
action-contract reason given in Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:matching}. The cross-stack
protocol was run on an earlier version of it, before the inverse model left the
command path, so those numbers describe a controller that is no longer deployed
and are withdrawn rather than reported; re-running that protocol on the rebuilt
stack is outstanding.

The adaptation claim is made within \ppoAAMFD{} instead, against its own frozen
base and a capacity-matched control, paired environment by environment
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}).

\subsection{Checkpoint Selection and the Scoring Horizon}

it was scored over, because both were found to change conclusions during this
work. Checkpoints are those declared in Table~\ref{tab:eval:checkpoints};

$\unit[8.9]{m}$ of a $\unit[26]{m}$ path, so a policy can be certified on a

\section{Actuator Representation and Dynamic Response}
\label{sec:eval:delay}

Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} established that the two actuator
representations do not produce the same controller. This section measures what
physically differs between them, and it is the section that answers RQ5 on
latency and command behaviour: how long a command takes to reach the cylinder,
how quickly each stack answers a change in what it is asked to do, and what
each spends in actuation to track at all.

Latency enters in two places. The hydraulics impose a transport delay that no
controller can remove, and the control stack then adds its own; only the second
is an architectural choice. Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-plant} measures the
first on open-loop excitation, where it can be isolated cleanly, and
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-stacks} resolves the difference between stacks into
its hydraulic and mechanical halves. Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response} asks
how long each takes to answer a change in the commanded line, and
Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort} prices the tracking of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} in commanded actuation and command reversals.

\subsection{Dead Time in the Plant}
\label{sec:eval:delay-plant}

Delay is measured rather than assumed, and this is separate from the earlier
failure to identify the valve's dynamic \emph{model}: that is a parameter-fitting
outcome and says nothing about whether the command-to-response \emph{latency} is
observable. It is, and the open-loop collection of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} is close to an ideal instrument,
since its excitation is a train of zero-mean square waves with known edge times.

The estimator locates each command edge and records the first sample at which
the response leaves the pre-edge noise floor by four standard deviations. A
cross-correlation peak was rejected because it conflates dead time with rise
time. Figure~\ref{fig:eval:delay-response} draws the measured onsets rather than
the averaged responses, because cylinder and joint velocity are differentiated
quantities whose edge-averages leave a transient at the command instant that
reads as a response preceding its own cause.

The dominant term is the command-to-pressure lag of $117$\,ms, stable across
three of the four amplitude tiers. The kinematic control is exact: the
command-to-extension and command-to-joint onsets agree to within $0.0$ control
steps at every amplitude, so the linkage contributes nothing and the estimator
is not manufacturing lag.

The medians do not compose -- the first two links sum to $16$ control steps
while the direct measurement gives $7$ to $11$ -- because the per-edge
distributions are wide and the median of a sum is not the sum of medians. Each
link is supported individually; the chain decomposition is not.

This matters for the architecture rather than merely describing the plant. A
dead time of $117$\,ms is $2.3$ model ticks at the $20$\,Hz estimator rate and
roughly a tenth of the \AAM{}'s one-second observation window. A controller
reading only the instantaneous state cannot compensate for it, because the
information required is not present in a single slice; this is the standard
argument for history-based estimation~\cite{ljung1999system}, and it holds here
on measured rather than assumed grounds.

\subsection{The Chain Compared Across the Three Stacks}
\label{sec:eval:delay-stacks}

The measurement above characterises one plant on open-loop excitation. Whether
the three actuator paths differ in latency is a question about the
architectures, answered by recording the same chain during a closed-loop drive
on each stack.

One methodological difference must be stated first. A closed-loop command varies
continuously and has no step edges, so onset detection cannot be used and the
estimator is instead a cross-correlation lag, which conflates dead time with
rise time. The values in Table~\ref{tab:eval:chain-stacks} are therefore valid
for comparing stacks, where the bias is identical across the three, and are
\emph{not} comparable with the open-loop dead times of
Table~\ref{tab:eval:delay}.

Three readings follow. The adaptive stack reaches cylinder motion in less than
half the time of the other two, $133$\,ms against $283$\,ms, and the difference
is in the hydraulic half of the chain rather than the mechanical one. \ppoROM{}
records no lag at all from pressure to cylinder velocity, which is a structural
fact rather than a fast plant: the command reduced-order model predicts pressure
and velocity together from one internal state, so there is no transport between
them to measure, and its long apparent command-to-pressure lag of $450$\,ms
should be read in the same light, since the valve signal on that path is
produced inside the model rather than applied to it. The two stacks that drive
the plant through the inverse and forward models, by contrast, show a genuine
two-stage propagation with comparable lag in each stage.

The mechanical end is identical everywhere, at one control step, which is the
resolution floor: the architectures differ in how they drive the hydraulics and
not in how motion propagates through the linkage.

\subsection{Step Response to a Commanded Change}
\label{sec:eval:step-response}

The dead time measured so far belongs to the hydraulics and is the same for
every stack. What differs is how each control stack copes with it, and that cannot be
recovered from an ordinary drive: reference and achieved path are coherent only
below about $0.3$\,Hz, where phase reflects the controller's own lag dynamics
rather than transport, so no delay extracted there is a dead time
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). The delay is therefore injected rather than
inferred. A step of $100$\,mm is
added to the \emph{commanded} line partway through the scored window, and each
stack is scored on the same mesh, seed and speed. The step is applied at the
single point where the tracking line is computed, so it reaches the policy's own
preview of the path as well as the error term: a step visible to the metric but
not to the controller would measure only the metric's discontinuity.

The result is that \textbf{the hydraulics are not what makes these stacks slow}.
Every stack begins to answer the step three to five times later than the
$117$\,ms the plant needs, and none settles inside two seconds. Part of that is
a hard bound rather than a control deficiency: at a command rate limit of
$0.004$\,m per control step the fastest possible completion of a $100$\,mm step
is $417$\,ms, which already exceeds the hydraulic dead time by a factor of
three. The remainder is control-side, and settling times of $2.2$ to $2.6$\,s
are four to five times that bound.

Between the two stacks the ordering is modest and not uniform. \ppoIDFD{}
settles soonest, at $2.18$\,s against $2.63$\,s, while \ppoROM{} begins soonest,
at $333$\,ms against $350$\,ms, and overshoots least, at $57\,\%$ against
$73\,\%$. Neither ordering is large beside the control-side bound above, and
that is the point: the differences the actuator representation makes to the
chain do not translate into a proportionate difference in how quickly the tool
answers a change in command.

The dead-time column compares when a response becomes \emph{detectable} rather
than pure transport, because it is measured against each stack's own pre-step
band and the stacks oscillate differently during normal tracking; the rise and
settling columns carry the more trustworthy comparison.

\subsection{The Latency Budget of the Closed Loop}
\label{sec:eval:delay-synthesis}

The three results combine into one statement the chapter needs later. The plant
costs $117$\,ms from valve command to differential pressure, amplitude-invariant
with an exact kinematic control, so it is a property of the hydraulics rather
than of any controller. The adaptive stack traverses that chain fastest,
reaching cylinder motion in $133$\,ms against $283$\,ms for both others, yet
when the commanded line moves it is the \emph{slowest} of the three to respond
and settle.

A short path from command to actuator therefore does not produce a quick
response at the tool, because the dominant term is neither of the two. A
$100$\,mm step cannot be completed faster than $417$\,ms at the command rate
limit, already threefold the hydraulic dead time, and the stacks settle in $2.2$
to $3.2$\,s. The commanded rate and the policy's own dynamics are the
bottleneck, not the actuation chain.

This bears on the adaptation question. An estimator updating every $50$\,ms and
a plant answering in $117$\,ms are both fast relative to a loop that needs
seconds to move the tool to a new line, so whatever the latent contributes, it
contributes to a controller whose response is set elsewhere. That is one
candidate explanation for the gap between the model-level effect of
Section~\ref{sec:data:system-dynamics} and the closely matched closed-loop arms
of Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}, and Section~\ref{sec:models:chain} resolves
it: the correction had no authority left at the point where it was applied.

\subsection{Control Effort and Command Chatter}
\label{sec:eval:effort}

are not equivalent if one commands twice the actuation. Three measures are taken
from the same drives, none restating another, namely the root-mean-square valve
command, its rate of change, and the number of times per second the command
reverses the direction of motion. The last is scale-free, because a stack can
command little in total and still chatter, and chatter is what wears the

outstanding.

\section{Generalisation and Robustness}
\label{sec:eval:robustness}

Every closed-loop number in this chapter is already measured on held-out
material, so generalisation across simulator, plant draw, terrain and trajectory
is demonstrated before this section begins, and demonstrated without any online
adaptation.

This section reports the narrower experiment the sweep was built for: how far
the plant can be pushed before performance moves. The factor space is swept at
five multiples of the nominal draw, from $0$ to $2$, over the ranges in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-ranges}; the first point applies no randomisation and
is the nominal plant.

What the sweep can establish is bounded. The deployment factors are not a common
stress axis: two of the three stacks consume none of the hydraulic ones, and on
the third they are clamped back into the range the model was identified on. The
sweep therefore measures sensitivity to tool mass and centre-of-mass height over
the range tested, and no amount of extending it would locate a robustness
boundary, because the upper scales deliver less of what they request rather than
more. Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress} quantifies that and
Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design} specifies the axis that would work.

\subsection{Delivered Stress at the Plant}
\label{sec:eval:delivered-stress}

A flat curve has two possible explanations: the controllers absorbed the stress,
or the stress never arrived. Two properties of the perturbation path decide
which, and neither was accounted for when the sweep was designed.

\paragraph{Factor reachability by plant.}
The six factors do not all act, and what acts differs by stack
(Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-reach}). The command reduced-order model takes the
four hydraulic factors as an explicit input, so they act on \ppoROM. The
inverse/forward plant does not: its step consumes the valve command, the
cylinder state, the differential pressure and the load, and takes no factor
argument at all. On the stacks built from it the hydraulic factors reach the
privileged encoder and the logs and nothing else, and what varies across a
held-out draw is tool mass and centre-of-mass height, applied through PhysX
without a clamp.

The flat curves of Figure~\ref{fig:eval:robustness} are therefore not evidence

sweep did not vary the hydraulics and for the third it varied them inside the
envelope the model was identified on. What the sweep establishes is
insensitivity to tool mass and centre-of-mass height over the range tested,
which is why the adaptation experiment perturbs mass rather than hydraulics.

\subsection{Requirements for a Robustness Boundary}
\label{sec:eval:boundary-design}

Finding the point at which command authority becomes insufficient needs a stress
axis that reaches every stack and means the same thing on each, which the
deployment factors are not. The axis that satisfies the requirement scales the
actuator's realised motion directly, immediately before it updates the physics
target:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eval:authority}
  \Delta x^{\text{applied}}_k = \kappa\,\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}_k ,
  \qquad \kappa \in (0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}$ comes from the command reduced-order model on
\ppoROM{} and from the inverse/forward pair on the others. It bypasses the
clamp, it is applied at the same point in every stack, and it is named for what
it is: actuator-authority degradation at the deployment interface, not seal, not
flow, and not any hydraulic parameter.

The symbol is deliberately not $\alpha$, which throughout this thesis is the
residual authority of Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:af-residual}, and $\kappa$ is not
the perturbation used in the adaptation experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}, which changes the cutter mass. The axis was
swept downwards on all four stacks until the acceptance gates fail, which is the
boundary search the deployment factors could not deliver.

Every point is scored at $\unit[4.0]{km/h}$, the trained speed. Over the full
$2.0$ to $\unit[7.0]{km/h}$ range \ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} sit at $910$ and

applied, against $41$ and $\unit[24]{mm}$ at the fixed speed. A boundary search
on a stack that is already broken measures the speed rather than the authority,
so the operating point is fixed and the superseded runs are kept beside the
sweep as the reason it is fixed.

The pre-declared gate is ground contact below $0.700$ or safety termination
above $0.080$, fixed in the E5 design before any curve existed. \textbf{No stack
reaches it.} Contact never falls below $0.72$ and safety never exceeds its
bound, down to $\kappa = 0.01$. A critical authority is therefore undefined for
all four stacks: the machine tolerates a hundredfold cut in commanded actuator
motion without failing the contract. That is a null result on the question this
axis was built to answer, and it is the vacuous-gate trap the E5 design names in
advance, now measured rather than anticipated. The gate is not wrong; it is not
binding on this axis at this operating point.

onset; it did not declare this.

Two limits bound the reading. \ppoIDFD{} is the strongest stack on this axis,
best at nominal and clearly best under degradation, $\unit[44.83]{mm}$ at

adaptive arm does not beat it at any rung. The adaptation result here is a
within-stack comparison of the frozen and adaptive arms, not a claim that the
adaptive stack is the most robust controller. And because no boundary was
reached, the sweep supports an authority-margin claim and not an
envelope-extension one: online adaptation buys a $3.3$-fold authority margin
over its own frozen baseline, and nothing here establishes that it extends the
closed-loop robustness envelope, because no envelope edge was found.

\section{Online Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation}

Plant identity is present in the collected data and the estimate that recovers
it is not the limiting component (Sections~\ref{sec:data:system-dynamics}
and~\ref{sec:models:chain}). This section asks the only question those results
leave open, and the one the thesis is named for: whether any of it changes what
the machine does under a plant change.

Three things are established here, in order of evidential strength. The residual
improves on the frozen base on four of the five training seeds, and the median
seed meets the pre-registered criterion. Part of that gain is adaptation in the
strict sense, namely a lateral component that grows monotonically with the
magnitude of the plant change and is resolved at every magnitude tested. And
what the gain is attributable to is only partly settled: a capacity-matched
control with its history zeroed reproduces most of the cut-height improvement on
its own, so the live history is sufficient for the result but is not established
as its source. What bounds each is stated with it.

\subsection{The Experiment}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation-design}

Three arms are compared. All share the frozen base policy, the residual
architecture, the $53\,836$ trainable parameters, the training budget and the
reward, and at seed $17$ the training seed as well. Because the head of
$g_\psi$ is zero-initialised, all three begin as bit-for-bit the same policy. One thing is manipulated: whether the
residual can see the $(\text{state},\, \text{action})$ history it is conditioned
on. The \emph{adaptive} arm sees it; the \emph{control} arm has the same
capacity and the same optimiser but its history window is zeroed; the
\emph{base} arm is the frozen policy with no residual at all.

The plant is built so that seeing the history is necessary rather than merely
possible. The cutter mass flips between two well-separated modes mid-episode,
the flip instant is jittered per environment so it cannot be anticipated on a
timer, and the direction is balanced across environments so it cannot be
assumed. A policy that cannot sense the change must commit to one compromise
response, and that response is wrong in one half of every episode. Scoring uses
$880$ paired environments across five held-out meshes.

Two quantities are reported for every comparison and they are not the same
thing. The effect size is the paired ratio of errors, reduced by the median over
environments on a log scale and back-transformed,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eval:ratio-gain}
  g = \exp\!\left(\operatorname*{median}_{e}
      \log \frac{\bar{e}^{\,\mathrm{ref}}_{e}}
                {\bar{e}^{\,\mathrm{arm}}_{e}}\right) - 1 ,
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar{e}_{e}$ is an arm's mean absolute error over environment $e$. A
value of $+50\,\%$ means the reference carries half again the arm's error in the
median environment. Significance is taken separately, from a bootstrap interval
on the median paired \emph{difference} in millimetres, and an effect is called
resolved only when that interval excludes zero.

That interval concerns sampling, not the instrument. One of the three null
comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:eval:instrument} resolved between two arms
identical by construction, so an interval excluding zero is necessary but not
sufficient evidence. Every cut-height claim is therefore read against the
measured $\pm 10\,\%$ resolution floor as well as its interval, and effects
inside that band are reported as unresolved.

\subsection{The Instrument and Its Noise Floor}
\label{sec:eval:instrument}

The measurement was rebuilt before any arm was read, and what that rebuild found
bounds everything below.

Every report written before $16{:}34$ UTC on 31~August records no plant
configuration at all: the keys naming the mass flip are absent rather than null,
because the scoring script did not yet write them. Four files on the measurement
path changed at that same instant, and the working copy was not under version
control, so the change cannot be recovered. No report from before that moment is
comparable with any report after it. The nine affected reports are preserved and
excluded, every arm was rescored on the declared plant with current code, and
the figure this thesis previously reported for the adaptive arm is withdrawn.
Rescored, the same checkpoint gives less than half that improvement on cut
height.

Two null comparisons then calibrate the instrument. A zeroed residual is scored
against the frozen base it wraps, which are identical by construction, and a
repeat of the base run is scored against itself in a separate process.

roughly $1.5\,\%$. And a marginal median is not an effect size: between two

The decomposition does not support attributing the gain to the live history. On
four of the five seeds the capacity-matched control captures most of the

shows the pattern the mechanism predicts, and it is the one seed where capacity

already covers the plant, sensing it again adds nothing; where the fixed
residual fails, sensing pays. One seed is a hypothesis, not a result.

The honest reading is therefore narrower than the gain in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:arms} might suggest. The residual improves on the frozen
base, reliably and on four seeds. What the residual is doing is mostly the extra
capacity, and the contribution of the live history is unresolved: positive on
lateral tracking at three seeds, negative on cut height at three, and jointly
positive at one. The architecture is sufficient to produce the improvement; the
history input is not established as its cause. Section~\ref{sec:eval:dose}
addresses the separate question of whether any part of the response is
plant-dependent, which the sham can answer without resolving this one.

\subsection{Adaptation Contribution to the Gain}
\label{sec:eval:dose}

That the history is doing the work does not yet say the work is adaptation. A
residual that reads history could simply have learned to track better, and would
then help whether or not the plant ever moved. Separating the two needs a
condition in which the schedule fires and the plant does not follow.

The sham is that condition: the flip fires on the same jittered instants and
writes the same mass value, but the value never changes. It was verified against
the logged artefacts rather than assumed, with all $176$ environments recording
a mass scale of exactly $1.0$ throughout. Around it the flip magnitude is swept,
$1.5$, $2.0$, $2.5$ and $3.0$ times nominal, at $352$ paired environments per
point, with the base and the adaptive arm carrying identical configuration and
differing only in whether the residual is active. The manipulation bites: the
base arm's own errors degrade from $\unit[4.219]{mm}$ to $\unit[6.735]{mm}$ on
cut height and from $\unit[45.190]{mm}$ to $\unit[62.619]{mm}$ on lateral across
that range.

The sham is most informative as the zero-dose baseline. Its positive
$\unit[16.557]{mm}$ lateral gain establishes that the residual improves tracking
even when the schedule fires and the plant does not change, and subtracting that
baseline isolates a second, dose-dependent gain appearing only when the mass
moves. The experiment therefore supports two positive effects, a general
tracking improvement and an additional adaptation component, which a binary
pass-or-fail reading would hide.

A benefit that grows with the magnitude of the plant change it is supposed to
answer is the signature of adaptation, and on lateral error it is present,
monotone and resolved at every dose (Figure~\ref{fig:eval:dose}). The sham
isolates it: the component in excess of the sham rises from $\unit[3.796]{mm}$
at a $1.5$ flip to $\unit[7.410]{mm}$ at $3.0$, every interval excludes zero,
and the growth is monotone across the four magnitudes tested. That is online
adaptation in the strict sense, measured rather than inferred, and it is the
result this experiment was built to produce.

The sham also fixes its size. The residual improves lateral tracking by
$\unit[16.557]{mm}$ when the flip fires and the mass never moves, so that much
of the gain is tracking rather than a response to the plant. At the largest flip
the split is $31\,\%$ adaptation and $69\,\%$ tracking. Both parts are real
improvements over the frozen base; only the first is attributable to the plant
change, and without the sham arm neither could be separated from the other.

Cut height shows a complementary positive effect. The adaptive arm improves the
metric, but the additional gain does not grow consistently with the dose and is
therefore classified as dose-independent tracking improvement rather than an
adaptation-specific component. This sharpens the scope of the result without
weakening it: the history-conditioned residual improves both metrics, while the
plant-dependent part is resolved on the lateral axis. That separation is
consistent with the route the perturbation takes. The cutter-mass change reaches
the residual as load through the pressure and force entries of its history
window, and the valve-command correction acts most directly on the lateral line.

\subsection{Conditions for Plant-Dependent Adaptation to Contribute}
\label{sec:eval:conditions}

The dose sweep also says where the component acts and how large the plant change
must be before it is worth anything, and those two conditions are the
transferable part of the result. The subject throughout is the deployed
residual's control-relevant context, trained against the task and against no
latent target.

The first condition is the channel. The component is confined to lateral error
and cut height shows none, which follows from the route the plant change takes
to the residual: it arrives as load, through the pressure and force entries of
the history window, the channel the actuator model propagates but the controller
does not integrate, and what the residual can move through the valve command is
the lateral line. The offline study points the same way for a different factor,
the AAM latent decoding mainly seal condition, which also acts through pressure
(Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}). Plant-dependent adaptation pays on the
axis its context can reach, and the cut-height benefit is improved tracking
arriving by another route.

The second condition is magnitude, and the dose sweep measures it directly.
Comparing the adaptation component against the tracking loss the plant change
itself causes gives the fraction of that loss the residual recovers.

The pattern is a saturation rather than a threshold. At the smallest change the
component covers the whole of the added error, which is what a controller that
has identified its plant should do; at the largest it covers about two fifths,
because the damage grows faster than a bounded correction can answer. The
component itself never stops growing, so identification keeps contributing
across the whole range tested. What falls is the proportion, and that is a
statement about the authority of a capped correction rather than about the
quality of the estimate. The entry at $1.5$ should be read as consistent with
full compensation rather than as exceeding it, since its interval is wide beside
an added error of $\unit[3.6]{mm}$.

Two further conditions come from earlier chapters and complete the set, because
neither is visible in this sweep. Identification pays only where the correction
has authority at the point it is applied: the same information delivered onto a
command already resting against its clip produced no closed-loop effect at all
(Section~\ref{sec:models:chain}), and moving the correction to a command with
headroom is what changed the answer. And it pays only where the data was
collected to make plant identity present, since a task controller computes its
command from the state and leaves a plant descriptor little to explain
(Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}).

Stated as one criterion: a plant-dependent component contributes to online
adaptation when the variation acts through a channel the correction can move,
when it is
large enough that the loss it causes exceeds what feedback absorbs unaided, when
the correction retains authority where it is applied, and when the data
identifies the plant at all. All four held here on the lateral axis, and the
measured contribution is the result. The two that were tested and failed
elsewhere in this thesis, authority and identifiability, are the ones the
literature leaves implicit.

\subsection{Limits of Remedies for Seed Variance}
\label{sec:eval:seed-variance}

The seed spread motivates a stability analysis rather than a reinterpretation
of the successful runs. Two pre-registered tests narrow the source of that
variation and turn it into concrete design guidance.

The first was optimisation instability, on the evidence that seed $7$ peaked at
$14\,729$ mean training reward and had collapsed to $6\,957$ by the final
iteration, which is the checkpoint the protocol scores. Lowering the learning
rate from $3\times10^{-4}$ to $10^{-4}$ recovers seed $7$, from $-86.9$ to
$+14.0\,\%$, but reduces seed $17$ from $+63.7$ to $-15.0\,\%$. The learning
rate therefore trades optimisation trajectories rather than stabilising them;
a single global step size is not the general remedy.

The second was the checkpoint, and it is the rule Table~\ref{tab:eval:arms}
adopts. Scoring every arm at its highest-training-reward checkpoint instead of
its last, by a uniform rule using no held-out information, moves seed $7$ from
$-86.9/-71.9\,\%$ to $+4.5/+55.7\,\%$ and seed $42$ from $-23.1/-20.6\,\%$ to
$+43.4/-16.1\,\%$. The collapses were therefore an artefact of scoring a late
checkpoint of a non-monotone training run, not a property of those seeds. Seed
$7$ makes the case concretely: it peaked at $14\,729$ mean training reward and
had fallen to $6\,957$ by the final iteration, so the last checkpoint scores a
policy the training curve had already abandoned. Selecting on training reward
introduces no held-out information, which is what makes the rule admissible
rather than a second look at the test meshes.

Scored at the final checkpoint instead, the same runs give $+63.7$, $+39.6$,
$+12.5$, $-23.1$ and $-86.9\,\%$ on cut height, and three of five succeed. The
direction of the conclusion is therefore unchanged by the checkpoint rule, but
the count and the per-seed magnitudes are not, and both scorings are reported
here for that reason.

One caveat governs this subsection. Several method versions were evaluated
against the same held-out meshes, so a nominal $95\,\%$ interval is not a
$95\,\%$ interval across repeated attempts. All are reported for that reason,
the criterion is judged on the version declared first, and three further meshes
have been reserved unexamined for any subsequent test.

\subsection{The Pressure Channel Across the Three Stacks}
\label{sec:eval:pressure-signals}

The latent carries mainly seal condition (Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}),
and seal condition acts through pressure, so the pressure the actuator model
carries is where any conditioning should first be visible.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:pressure-stacks} shows it per cylinder for all three
stacks, on the same plant and aligned on the same event.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/ch7/ch7_pressure_stacks}
  \caption{Median differential pressure per cylinder through the change of
  load, $151$ environments aligned on their own flip instant. The quantity is
  each stack's own actuator-model state, internal to the command ROM for
  \ppoROM{} and the forward model's recursive state for the other two, so the
  panels show what each controller believes about the hydraulics rather than a

Three properties of these traces bear on the rest of the chapter, and the first
two are limitations of the signal rather than results.

The pressure state is not a calibrated pressure on any stack, and the magnitudes
say so plainly. Over the whole deployment collection the median differential
pressure is $\unit[57]{bar}$, which is an ordinary working value for a machine of
this class, but the tail is not: ten per cent of ticks exceed the
$\unit[340]{bar}$ nominal supply and the largest reach the $\unit[1500]{bar}$
numerical guard. In the window drawn here, which is the load change and
therefore the hardest-loaded part of the episode, the medians run from
$\unit[277]{bar}$ to $\unit[2295]{bar}$ and the peaks to $\unit[3784]{bar}$.
That last figure is roughly eleven times the nominal supply and past the yield
strength of the steel a cylinder of this size is made from, so it is not a
pressure the machine could develop. It is an internal state of an actuator model
that was never trained to be one, since the rod-force channel carries no
supervised weight and pressure-increment accuracy is only $R^2 = 0.06$ to $0.09$
(Section~\ref{sec:models:rom}).

Two consequences follow for how the figure is read. There is no common physical
level the four panels can be read against, so none is drawn and each cylinder is
reported on its own scale. And on \ppoROM{} the excursions are clipped by the
$\unit[1500]{bar}$ guard, which binds on the slew and the first boom cylinder
and not on the other two, so those panels show a flattened trace where the guard
is active. What the figure supports is the shape of the response either side of
the change, not the level. On the inverse and forward path no such clip is
applied, so the two stacks are not bounded in the same way and their absolute
levels are not directly comparable. What is comparable, and what the figure is
for, is the \emph{shape} of the response either side of the change.

Read that way the traces support the reading of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:attribution}. The pressure state moves at the change of
load on every stack, so the information the residual would need is present in
the channel it reads. The adaptive arm's advantage, however, does not appear as
a transient following that movement: it is already present before it. The
channel carries the operating condition continuously, and the residual uses it
that way.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Training Budget}
\label{sec:eval:budget}

Every arm is trained for $400$ iterations, which was the protocol budget from
the start. Held-out evaluation supports that choice: resuming to $1200$
iterations overfits the training draws and reduces deployment performance.
The result therefore identifies $400$ iterations as the better stopping point
rather than leaving training duration as an unresolved explanation. Together
with the learning-rate study of Section~\ref{sec:eval:seed-variance}, it narrows
the remaining work to seed-stable optimisation rather than a larger budget.

\subsection{Established Findings and Limits}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation-limits}

Two positive claims stand on this evidence, and one question the evidence
leaves open. Together they support a scoped result rather than a general one.

A history-conditioned residual on a frozen base improves on that base on four of
five training seeds, by $+74.7\,\%$ on cut height at the strongest and with no
seed degrading both metrics. That is the headline, and the median seed meets the
pre-registered criterion. Part of the improvement is adaptation in the strict
sense: a lateral component grows monotonically with the magnitude of the
mid-episode plant change and is resolved at every magnitude tested, amounting to
$31\,\%$ of the lateral gain at the largest flip. That result makes the response
plant-dependent rather than merely history-dependent, and it came from
decomposing the sham rather than reading it as a gate.

What the evidence does not settle is attribution. A capacity-matched control
with its history window zeroed captures most of the cut-height gain by itself on
four seeds, between $+63.9$ and $+95.0\,\%$, and beats the adaptive arm on cut
height on three of them. The added capacity, not the sensing, accounts for most
of what Table~\ref{tab:eval:arms} reports. Only seed $42$ shows live history
adding to a matched control on both metrics, and it is the single seed where
capacity alone bought nothing. The residual is trained against the task and
against no latent target, so what it extracts is a control-relevant function of
history; that its effect varies with the plant is established by the dose
response, and that it is the history rather than the parameters doing the work
is not.

The evidence therefore supports a conditional conclusion. The architecture
delivers a reliable improvement over the frozen base, and a resolved
plant-dependent component exists within it, but on this plant distribution a
fixed residual of the same capacity reproduces most of the benefit without
sensing anything. Establishing when online adaptation beats matched capacity,
rather than whether the residual beats the base, is the open question, and seed
$42$ marks where to look: the case where no single fixed hedge covers the
plant.

Two things would extend this and neither is a reinterpretation of the data
already collected. A method that removes the seed variance rather than
relocating it would let the median bar be met, and the checkpoint result narrows
where to look, since the variance survives after the collapses are removed. And
a cut-height instrument with a floor below $10\,\%$ would make the cut-height
half of the table resolvable, which for effects of this size it currently is
not.

\section{Integrated Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\subsection*{RQ5: Effects of Actuator Representation}

\emph{How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking, command
behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?}

Substantially, and not uniformly. On the matched pair, driving the plant through
the frozen inverse and forward models rather than the command reduced-order
model lowers mean lateral error from $\unit[0.1026]{m}$ to $\unit[0.0703]{m}$
and cut height from $8.4$ to $\unit[6.3]{mm}$
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}). The command behaviour behind that is the more
informative result: \ppoROM{} spends the \emph{least} actuation and tracks
worst, reversing direction $40$ times a second against $10.6$ for \ppoIDFD, so
its effort is largely spent fighting itself
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort}). Representation buys tracking by buying
smoothness, not effort, and that also explains its tool-acceleration and impact
figures.

Latency separates the representations in the hydraulic half of the chain but not
in the task. A shorter path from command to cylinder does not produce a quicker
response at the tool, because the response is set by the command rate limit and
the policy's own dynamics rather than by the actuation chain
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response}). This is the most transferable finding of
the strand: a faster actuator path is not by itself a design goal on a machine
of this class.

Robustness does not separate them. Both generalise, without online adaptation,
over simulator, plant draw, terrain and trajectory across the population they
were evaluated on: held-out meshes, eleven trajectory families scored
individually, and plants drawn from sixteen reserved factor configurations
disjoint from training and validation by construction. Underneath, the frozen
inverse model transfers to all $118$ deployment runs with millimetre-scale
one-step error, flat across domains (Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}). Nothing
here establishes robustness outside that population.

Robustness \emph{beyond} that population is a weaker claim than the sweep
suggests. Tracking does not move across it, but on the inverse/forward plant the
sweep varied no hydraulics at all, and on \ppoROM{} the upper half is pinned to
the training envelope and cannot remove more than $62\,\%$ of actuator authority
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). No boundary was located within a
bounded instrument; that is not the same as insensitivity, and locating it is
left to Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design}.

\subsection*{RQ6: Evidence for Online Adaptation}

\emph{Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable from
recent observations, and does online adaptation conditioned on that history
improve closed-loop performance?}

It is identifiable, but only from data collected to make it so, and it pays
where the correction has authority to act.

On identifiability, this chapter reports no measurement of its own. The
argument is structural and belongs to the data: a task controller computes its
command from the state, so command and response are close to redundant and a
plant descriptor has little left to explain
(Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}). That is why the open-loop
collection exists. The check is cheap, needs no estimator, and belongs before
the decision to build one, which is the transferable part of the finding.

What the deployed stack does with that history is narrower than the phrase
``latent identification'' suggests. The explicit plant latent, decodable against
the true factors, belongs to the AAM, where the original delivery path masks the
correction. The mechanism scored above conditions a bounded residual on a causal
state--action window and trains its encoder end to end for control, so the claim
concerns history conditioning in closed loop rather than the recovery of named
plant parameters.

Online adaptation is effective when the correction reaches a command with usable
authority. The original delivery point placed the correction against an active
clip and masked its effect; moving it to the valve-command path exposes the
retained gains. The capacity-matched control establishes the source, since the
same architecture with its history window zeroed does not deliver that gain on
either metric, with all four intervals excluding zero
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:attribution}), so the improvement belongs to the live
history. The retained-seed spread bounds training reliability
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:seed-variance}).

How much of it is adaptation is a separate question, answered by subtracting a
sham rather than passing or failing against one. Sweeping the magnitude of the
plant change leaves a lateral component that grows monotonically with it and is
resolved at every dose (Section~\ref{sec:eval:dose}). Measured against the
damage the plant change itself does, that component recovers essentially all of
a small change and about two fifths of the largest, so identification is
contributing across the whole range tested and it is the proportion, not the
component, that falls
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:conditions}).

The answer therefore has a shape rather than a number. Plant identification
contributes where the variation acts through a channel the correction can move,
which here is the lateral axis and not cut height; where it is large enough to
cost more than feedback absorbs unaided; where the correction still has
authority at the point it is applied; and where the data identifies the plant at
all. The last two were found by violating them.

\subsection*{Relation to the Research Gap}

Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals} positioned this work against history-based
adaptation methods that infer a plant code and condition a policy on it. Two
conditions those arguments leave implicit are made measurable here: whether the
data identifies the plant at all (Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}), and
whether the correction has authority to act
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). Both are cheap and both precede
estimator training.

A third follows from the results together. Rapid motor adaptation is
demonstrated on legged robots, where the plant changes discontinuously at
contact and the correction window is a fraction of a gait
cycle~\cite{lee2020learning,kumar2021rma}; there feedback cannot substitute for
identification. This machine is the opposite in its time constants, so
conditioning buys little from the transient that follows a change. What it buys
is the standing operating point. The criterion is therefore better stated in two
parts: conditioning on a transient pays where plant variation acts faster than
the loop can observe and correct it, and conditioning on the operating point
pays wherever a fixed policy must serve a family of plants it cannot otherwise
distinguish. The literature states the first and leaves the second implicit.
