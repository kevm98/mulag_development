\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:eval:hypotheses}

This chapter evaluates the three control stacks assembled in
Chapter~\ref{chap:control} and the learned actuator models they act through. It
answers the last two research questions of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions}:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ5] How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking,
    command behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?
  \item[RQ6] Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable
    from recent observations, and does online adaptation conditioned on that
    history improve closed-loop performance?
\end{description}

The two differ in kind. RQ5 asks what a design choice changes and is answered by
direct measurement on the matched pair \ppoROM{} (R) and \ppoIDFD{} (IF), which
agree on every recorded training field and differ only in the actuator path.
RQ6 tests whether adaptation improves control and states its failure conditions
in advance. It is therefore carried by predeclared hypotheses and by
within-checkpoint substitutions rather than by the position of \ppoAAMFD{}
(AF) in a table. The short labels R, IF and AF of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions} are used interchangeably with the full
names. RQ6 also decomposes into a chain of dependent questions, each worth
testing only if the one before it holds: is plant identity present in the data
at all; can an estimator recover it; does it improve a model that consumes it;
and does that improvement reach the controller? The first three links are
settled before this chapter -- by the excitation collection of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} and the model-level results of
Chapter~\ref{chap:models} -- and only the last is a question about the machine.
That is the one this chapter answers, in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}.

\subsection{Test Conditions and Experimental Matrix}
\label{sec:eval:matrix}

Every closed-loop experiment scores exactly three rows -- \ppoROM{},
\ppoIDFD{} and \ppoAAMFD{} -- under one common condition: three held-out
terrain meshes, the eleven trajectory families of the library, a pinned truck
speed of $\unit[4]{km/h}$, a $\unit[26]{m}$ scored window, $32$ environments and
a fixed evaluation seed. The one model-level experiment here, the dead-time
measurement of Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-plant}, uses the open-loop
excitation collection \texttt{rom\_openloop\_v1} of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}.
Everything scored here is held out: the meshes are unseen, the families are
scored individually rather than pooled, and the plant is drawn from sixteen
reserved factor configurations whose keys are disjoint from training and
validation by construction (Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}), so a scored run is
a plant the stack has never seen and not merely an episode it has not seen. How
much of that factor draw reaches each stack's plant differs, and is audited in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}.
Table~\ref{tab:eval:matrix} lists every experiment with its arms and its
replication unit.

\subsection{Metrics, Statistics and Fairness}
\label{sec:eval:metrics}

Closed-loop performance is scored on mean absolute lateral error and mean
absolute cut-height error, with ground contact, hard impacts, head alignment and
tool acceleration reported alongside. The two errors are never pooled: they
differ by an order of magnitude in scale and are traded against one another by
some arms. Model-level experiments share one estimand, free-running accumulated
normalised state error over a five-tick horizon with no teacher forcing, because
a one-step error flatters every arm equally and hides the compounding that
separates them.

Metrics and seeds are fixed before each run. Comparisons are paired, report the
sign count beside the mean, and take their intervals from a paired bootstrap.
Selection uses the validation split; reserved test runs are scored once.

The unit of replication differs by experiment, and getting it wrong would
inflate the evidence. Model-level experiments replicate over training seeds. The
closed-loop evaluation is deterministic. Five evaluation seeds returned
bit-identical numbers to nine decimal places, so its unit is the (mesh,
family) cell, and metrics existing only per mesh are marked $n=3$.

Several controls run alongside the experiments, and each was added because
something it guards against had already gone wrong. Input ablation guards
against a model scoring well without command authority, and caught a forward
model at one-step $R^2\approx0.999$ that was insensitive to the valve. Open-loop
excitation guards against command and state being made redundant by feedback,
and established that the valve is not identifiable from controller-driven data
at all. A determinism check guards against reporting one observation several
times: five evaluation seeds agree bit-exactly, so the seed is not a unit of
replication. Full-path re-scoring guards against a selection window certifying
part of an episode, the default covering $\unit[8.9]{m}$ of $\unit[26]{m}$.
Retrained baselines guard against comparing numbers across collections, whose
normalisers differ. Per-family resolution guards against a worst-family
aggregate hiding a threshold artefact. The matched-pair design holds every
recorded training field equal so that the actuator path is the only declared
difference, and the capacity-matched control guards against crediting sensing
for what extra parameters buy.

Two gaps in that scheme are stated here rather than left to be inferred. The
matched pair holds the manifest equal but not the optimisation: one training run
represents each architecture, so a difference between R and IF is attributable
to the actuator path only under the assumption that a second run of either would
land in the same place, and that assumption is untested. The $33$ cells measure
variation across terrain and trajectory, not across training. Every comparative
statement in this chapter should be read with that scope. Second, a sham arm, in
which the flip schedule fires but the load never moves, would separate
adaptation from improved tracking; it has not been repeated under the current
objective (Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation-limits}).


\subsection{What the Three Stacks Can and Cannot Be Compared For}
\label{sec:eval:standing}

\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} agree on every recorded manifest field, including seed,
terrain seed, environments, stage, learning rate, entropy coefficient, frozen
normaliser and warm-up. They differ only in the actuator path. A difference
between them is attributable to the actuator representation, and that pair
answers RQ5.

\ppoAAMFD{} is not a third column in the tables that follow. It does not share
the action contract: its policy emits the valve command itself where the other
two emit a bounded cylinder increment (Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:matching}). The
cross-stack protocol was also run on an earlier version of it, before the
inverse model left the command path, so those numbers describe a controller that
is no longer deployed and are withdrawn rather than reported. Re-running that
protocol on the rebuilt stack is outstanding.

The adaptation claim is made within \ppoAAMFD{} instead, against its own frozen
base and a capacity-matched control, paired environment by environment
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}). That contrast is tighter than any
across-stack ranking, because the arms differ in one thing and start as the same
policy.

\section{Actuator Representation and Dynamic Response}
\label{sec:eval:delay}

Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} established that the two actuator
representations do not produce the same controller. This section measures what
physically differs between them, and it is the section that answers RQ5 on
latency and command behaviour: how long a command takes to reach the cylinder,
how quickly each stack answers a change in what it is asked to do, and what
each spends in actuation to track at all.

Latency enters the system in two quite different places, and separating them
comes first. The hydraulics impose a transport delay that no controller can
remove; the control stack then adds its own, and only the second is an
architectural choice. Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-plant} measures the first on
open-loop excitation, where it can be isolated cleanly.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:delay-stacks} asks whether the two stacks differ in how
quickly they drive the plant, and resolves the difference into its hydraulic and
mechanical halves. Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response} asks the question that
matters for the task -- how long each takes to answer a change in the commanded
line -- and finds the answer dominated by the control side rather than the
hydraulic one. Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort} then prices the tracking of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking} in commanded actuation and in command
reversals, which is what wears the machine.

\subsection{Dead Time in the Plant}
\label{sec:eval:delay-plant}

Delay is measured here rather than assumed. This result is separate from an
earlier negative result. Attempts to identify the valve's dynamic \emph{model}
from the available datasets did not succeed, but that is a parameter-fitting
outcome and says nothing about whether the command-to-response \emph{latency} is
observable. It is, and the open-loop collection of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} is close to an ideal instrument for
it: the
excitation is a train of zero-mean square waves with known edge times, which is
what one would design for a step-response measurement.

The estimator locates each command edge and records the first sample at which
the response leaves the pre-edge noise floor by four standard deviations. A
cross-correlation peak was rejected because it conflates dead time with rise
time. Figure~\ref{fig:eval:delay-response} draws the measured onsets rather
than the averaged responses themselves: cylinder and joint velocity are
differentiated quantities whose edge-averages leave a transient at the command
instant that reads as a response preceding its own cause, so a trace of them
would misstate the very quantity the figure is about.

The dominant term is the command-to-pressure lag of $117$\,ms, stable across
three of the four amplitude tiers. The kinematic control is exact: the
command-to-extension and command-to-joint onsets agree to within $0.0$ control
steps at every amplitude, so the linkage contributes nothing and the estimator
is not manufacturing lag.

The medians do not compose -- the first two links sum to $16$ control steps
while the direct measurement gives $7$ to $11$ -- because the per-edge
distributions are wide and the median of a sum is not the sum of medians. Each
link is supported individually; the chain decomposition is not.

This matters for the architecture rather than merely describing the plant. A
dead time of $117$\,ms is $2.3$ model ticks at the $20$\,Hz estimator rate and
roughly a tenth of the \AAM{}'s one-second observation window. A controller
reading only the instantaneous state cannot compensate for it, because the
information required is not present in a single slice; this is the standard
argument for history-based estimation~\cite{ljung1999system}, and it holds here
on measured rather than assumed grounds.

\subsection{The Chain Compared Across the Three Stacks}
\label{sec:eval:delay-stacks}

The measurement above characterises one plant on open-loop excitation. The three
control stacks reach that plant through different actuator paths, and whether
those paths differ in latency is a question about the architectures rather than
about the hydraulics. It is answered by recording the same chain during a
closed-loop drive on each stack.

One methodological difference must be stated before the numbers, because it
prevents a false comparison. A closed-loop command varies continuously and has
no step edges, so onset detection cannot be used and the estimator is instead a
cross-correlation lag, which conflates dead time with rise time. The values in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:chain-stacks} are therefore valid for comparing stacks,
where the bias is identical across the three, and are \emph{not} comparable with
the open-loop dead times of Table~\ref{tab:eval:delay}. The two tables do not
measure the same quantity.

Three readings follow. The adaptive stack reaches cylinder motion in less than
half the time of the other two, $133$\,ms against $283$\,ms, and the difference
is in the hydraulic half of the chain rather than the mechanical one. \ppoROM{}
records no lag at all from pressure to cylinder velocity, which is a structural
fact rather than a fast plant: the command reduced-order model predicts pressure
and velocity together from one internal state, so there is no transport between
them to measure, and its long apparent command-to-pressure lag of $450$\,ms
should be read in the same light, since the valve signal on that path is
produced inside the model rather than applied to it. The two stacks that drive
the plant through the inverse and forward models, by contrast, show a genuine
two-stage propagation with comparable lag in each stage.

The mechanical end is identical everywhere, at one control step, which is the
resolution floor: the architectures differ in how they drive the hydraulics and
not in how motion propagates through the linkage.

\subsection{Step Response to a Commanded Change}
\label{sec:eval:step-response}

The dead time measured so far belongs to the hydraulics and is the same for
every stack. What differs is how each control stack copes with it, and that cannot be
recovered from an ordinary drive: reference and achieved path are coherent only
below about $0.3$\,Hz, where phase reflects the controller's own lag dynamics
rather than transport, so no delay extracted there is a dead time
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). The delay is therefore injected rather than
inferred. A step of $100$\,mm is
added to the \emph{commanded} line partway through the scored window, and each
stack is scored on the same mesh, seed and speed. The step is applied at the
single point where the tracking line is computed, so it reaches the policy's own
preview of the path as well as the error term: a step visible to the metric but
not to the controller would measure only the metric's discontinuity.

The result is that \textbf{the hydraulics are not what makes these stacks slow}.
Every stack begins to answer the step three to five times later than the
$117$\,ms the plant needs, and none settles inside two seconds. Part of that is
a hard bound rather than a control deficiency: at a command rate limit of
$0.004$\,m per control step the fastest possible completion of a $100$\,mm step
is $417$\,ms, which already exceeds the hydraulic dead time by a factor of
three. The remainder is control-side, and settling times of $2.2$ to $2.6$\,s
are four to five times that bound.

Between the two stacks the ordering is modest and not uniform. \ppoIDFD{}
settles soonest, at $2.18$\,s against $2.63$\,s, while \ppoROM{} begins soonest,
at $333$\,ms against $350$\,ms, and overshoots least, at $57\,\%$ against
$73\,\%$. Neither ordering is large beside the control-side bound above, and
that is the point: the differences the actuator representation makes to the
chain do not translate into a proportionate difference in how quickly the tool
answers a change in command.

The dead-time column compares when a response becomes \emph{detectable} rather
than pure transport, because it is measured against each stack's own pre-step
band and the stacks oscillate differently during normal tracking; the rise and
settling columns carry the more trustworthy comparison.

\subsection{The Latency Budget of the Closed Loop}
\label{sec:eval:delay-synthesis}

The three results are easy to misread in isolation, and together they say
something the chapter needs later. The plant costs $117$\,ms from valve command
to differential pressure, and that figure is amplitude-invariant with an exact
kinematic control, so it is a property of the hydraulics rather than of any
controller. The stacks differ in how quickly they traverse that chain, and the
adaptive stack traverses it fastest, reaching cylinder motion in $133$\,ms
against $283$\,ms for both others. Yet when the commanded line moves, the
adaptive stack is the \emph{slowest} of the three to respond and to settle.

A short path from command to actuator therefore does not produce a quick
response at the tool, and the reason is that the dominant term is neither of the
two. A $100$\,mm step cannot be completed faster than $417$\,ms at the command
rate limit, which already exceeds the hydraulic dead time threefold, and the
stacks settle in $2.2$ to $3.2$\,s, five to eight times that bound. The
actuation chain is not the bottleneck; the commanded rate and the policy's own
dynamics are.

This bears directly on the adaptation question. An estimator that updates every
$50$\,ms and a plant that answers in $117$\,ms are both fast relative to a loop
that needs seconds to move the tool to a new line. Whatever the latent
contributes, it contributes it to a controller whose response is set elsewhere,
which is one candidate explanation for the gap between the model-level effect
reported in Section~\ref{sec:data:system-dynamics} and the closely matched
closed-loop arms of Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}. It is
resolved in Section~\ref{sec:models:chain}: the correction had no authority
left at the point where it was applied.

\subsection{Control Effort and Command Chatter}
\label{sec:eval:effort}

error are not equivalent if one commands twice the actuation to get there. Three
measures are taken from the same drives, chosen so that none restates another:
the root-mean-square valve command, its rate of change, and the number of times
per second the command reverses the direction of motion. The last is
deliberately scale-free, because a stack can command little in total and still
chatter, and chatter is what wears the machine.

and remains outstanding.

\section{Generalisation and Robustness}
\label{sec:eval:robustness}

Every closed-loop number in this chapter is already measured on held-out
material: unseen terrain meshes, eleven trajectory families scored individually,
and plant draws from sixteen reserved factor configurations whose keys are
disjoint from training by construction (Section~\ref{sec:data:isaaclab}).
Generalisation across simulator, plant draw, terrain and trajectory is therefore
demonstrated before this section begins, and demonstrated without any online
adaptation.

This section reports the narrower experiment the sweep was built for: how far
the plant can be pushed before performance moves. The factor space is swept at
five multiples of the nominal draw, from $0$ to $2$, over the ranges in
Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-ranges}; the first point applies no randomisation
and is the nominal plant.

What the sweep can establish is bounded, and stating that first avoids reading
the result for more than it is. The deployment factors are not a common stress
axis: two of the three stacks consume none of the hydraulic ones, and on the
third they are clamped back into the range the model was identified on. The
sweep therefore measures sensitivity to tool mass and centre-of-mass height over
the range tested. It does not locate a robustness boundary, and no amount of
extending it would, because the upper scales deliver less of what they request
rather than more. Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress} quantifies that and
Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design} specifies the axis that would work.

\subsection{How Much of the Requested Stress Reached the Plant}
\label{sec:eval:delivered-stress}

A flat curve across a randomisation sweep can mean the controller absorbed the
stress or that the stress never arrived. Two properties of the perturbation path
decide which, and neither was accounted for when the sweep was designed.

\paragraph{Which factors reach which plant.}
The six factors do not all act, and what acts differs by stack
(Table~\ref{tab:eval:factor-reach}). The command reduced-order model takes the
four hydraulic factors as an explicit input, so they act on \ppoROM. The
inverse/forward plant does not: its step consumes the valve command, the
cylinder state, the differential pressure and the load, and takes no factor
argument at all. On the stacks built from it the hydraulic factors reach the
privileged encoder and the logs and nothing else, and what varies across a
held-out draw is tool mass and centre-of-mass height, applied through PhysX
without a clamp.

The consequence for reading Figure~\ref{fig:eval:robustness} is direct. The flat
curves are not evidence that the stacks are robust to hydraulic variation,
because for two of them the sweep did not vary the hydraulics, and for the third
it varied them inside the envelope the model was identified on. What the sweep
does establish is insensitivity to tool mass and centre-of-mass height over the
range tested, which is a narrower claim than the one the sweep was built to
make, and it is the reason the adaptation experiment perturbs mass rather than
hydraulics.

\subsection{What a Robustness Boundary Would Require}
\label{sec:eval:boundary-design}

The sweep above does not locate a robustness boundary, and extending it would
not either: Table~\ref{tab:eval:delivered-stress} shows the upper scales
delivering less of what they request rather than more. Finding the point at
which command authority becomes insufficient needs a different stress axis, one
that reaches every stack and means the same thing on each. The deployment
factors are not such an axis, for the two reasons above.

The axis that satisfies the requirement scales the actuator's realised motion
directly, immediately before it updates the physics target:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eval:authority}
  \Delta x^{\text{applied}}_k = \alpha\,\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}_k ,
  \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}$ comes from the command reduced-order model on
\ppoROM{} and from the inverse/forward pair on the others. It bypasses the
clamp, it is applied at the same point in every stack, and it is named for what
it is: actuator-authority degradation at the deployment interface, not seal, not
flow, and not any hydraulic parameter.

That axis was subsequently implemented, and it is the $\alpha$ of the plant
perturbation used in the adaptation experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}, applied there together with a change of load.
The boundary search itself, sweeping $\alpha$ downwards on each stack until the
acceptance gates fail, is specified but not run, and it is the most direct
extension of this chapter.

\section{Online Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation}

Plant identity is present in the data when the excitation is right, and what
the latent carries is a moving operating condition rather than a fixed label
(Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}). Both are established on the models
themselves in Chapter~\ref{chap:models}. This section asks the only question
those results leave open, and the one the thesis is named for: whether any of it
changes what the machine does under a plant change.

\subsection{The Adaptation Experiment}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation-design}

Chapter~\ref{chap:models} establishes that plant identity is present,
recoverable and useful to a model. The experiment below asks whether it reaches
the closed loop. It is deliberately narrow, because a broad one would not be
attributable.

Three arms are compared. All three share the frozen base policy, the residual
architecture, the $53\,836$ trainable parameters, the training budget, the reward
and the seed. Because the head of $g_\psi$ is zero-initialised, all three begin
as bit-for-bit the same policy. One thing is manipulated: whether the residual
can see the $(\text{state},\, \text{action})$ history it is conditioned on. The
\emph{adaptive} arm sees it; the \emph{control} arm has the same capacity and the
same optimiser but its history window is zeroed; the \emph{base} arm is the
frozen policy with no residual at all.

The plant is built so that seeing the history is necessary rather than merely
possible. The cutter mass flips between two well-separated modes mid-episode,
the flip instant is jittered per environment so it cannot be anticipated on a
timer, and the direction is balanced across environments so it cannot be
assumed. A policy that cannot sense the change must commit to one compromise
response, and that response is wrong in one half of every episode. Scoring uses
$528$ paired environments across three held-out meshes, with bootstrap intervals
on the paired difference.

The adaptive arm lowers both scored errors against the policy it wraps. Cut
height falls from $\unit[7.964]{mm}$ to $\unit[3.772]{mm}$ and lateral from
$\unit[61.155]{mm}$ to $\unit[35.371]{mm}$, reductions of $52.6\,\%$ and
$42.2\,\%$, with paired differences of $+3.626\;[+3.082,\, +4.503]$ and
$+22.533\;[+20.802,\, +23.990]$ millimetres. Against the capacity-matched
control the reductions are $29.9\,\%$ and $42.5\,\%$, with paired differences of
$+1.836\;[+1.553,\, +2.155]$ and $+25.266\;[+23.378,\, +26.367]$. All four
intervals exclude zero.

The arms were pre-registered on a different statistic, and it is defined here so
that the two are not read as one. It is a paired ratio, taken per environment
and then reduced by the median over environments:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eval:ratio-gain}
  g = \operatorname*{median}_{e}
      \frac{\bar{e}^{\,\mathrm{ref}}_{e}}{\bar{e}^{\,\mathrm{adaptive}}_{e}}
      \; - \; 1 ,
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar{e}_{e}$ is an arm's mean absolute error over environment $e$. On
that statistic the adaptive arm scores $+120.1\,\%$ on cut height and
$+78.2\,\%$ on lateral against the base, and $+56.9\,\%$ and $+80.4\,\%$ against
the control. A value above $100\,\%$ is not a reduction larger than the error
itself: $g$ says how many times worse the reference arm is, so $+120.1\,\%$
means the base carries $2.2$ times the adaptive arm's error in the median
environment. It is quoted because it is what was declared in advance, and
because a median over environments is not carried by the few environments where
the base fails outright, which a ratio of means is. The reductions above are the
figures to compare with other work.

The control arm is what makes this attributable, and the two metrics divide the
credit differently. On \emph{lateral} error, adding the residual's capacity with
nothing to sense changes nothing at all: $\unit[61.155]{mm}$ becomes
$\unit[61.521]{mm}$, which is no movement. Letting the same residual see the
history takes it to $\unit[35.371]{mm}$. The arms differ in one thing only, so
that improvement belongs to the history input rather than to parameters,
optimisation or seed. On \emph{cut-height} error capacity already does much of
the work, $\unit[7.964]{mm}$ to $\unit[5.380]{mm}$, and the history improves it
again to $\unit[3.772]{mm}$. Both stages help; on that metric the larger share
is capacity, and saying so costs nothing because the lateral result carries the
claim.

A separate run on one held-out mesh, used for the traces in this section and in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:pressure-signals}, reproduces the same structure on $176$
environments: the control arm sits within $0.02\,\%$ of the base on lateral
error and wins in $53$ per cent of environments, which is chance, while the
adaptive arm halves it and wins in $80$ per cent against the control, at a
median paired ratio of $1.74$ with interval $[1.58,\, 1.86]$. The pattern is not
an artefact of one plant draw.

\subsection{Where the Advantage Comes From}
\label{sec:eval:attribution}

The history input produces the improvement, but two mechanisms could explain
the result.

The first is response to the change itself: the residual senses that the load
has moved and corrects for it. The second is continuous conditioning: the
residual reads the operating condition it is in at every tick and commands
accordingly, whether or not anything has just changed. Both are online
adaptation in the sense this thesis uses, since both condition the controller on
runtime history, but they predict different things.

Two measurements separate them. Across the matched windows the adaptive arm's
advantage over the control is $3.09$ before the change and $3.24$ after it on
cut height, which is flat. And splitting by the load the arm is currently
carrying, the advantage is larger at nominal load than at the raised one,
$2.81$ against $1.40$ on cut height. Neither is the signature of a transient
correction following an event. Both are what continuous conditioning looks like:
a controller that is better because it knows where it is, not because something
just happened.

This is the more useful of the two mechanisms for a machine of this class. The
plant of a verge mower is not usually characterised by discrete events, and a
controller that must wait for a detectable transient before improving would
spend most of an episode no better than its baseline. What the evidence supports
is a residual that is better throughout, and the strength of the lateral result
follows from exactly that.

What remains open is narrower and is named here rather than in a footnote. A
sham condition, in which the flip schedule fires but the load never moves,
would separate the two mechanisms directly rather than by inference. It has not
been run under this objective, and Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation-limits}
records it as outstanding.

\subsection{The Pressure Channel Across the Three Stacks}
\label{sec:eval:pressure-signals}

The latent carries mainly seal condition (Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}),
and seal condition acts through pressure, so the pressure the actuator model
carries is where any conditioning should first be visible.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval:pressure-stacks} shows it per cylinder for all three
stacks, on the same plant and aligned on the same event.

  \caption{Median differential pressure per cylinder through the change of
  load, $151$ environments aligned on their own flip instant. The dotted lines
  are $\pm$ the $\unit[340]{bar}$ nominal supply. The quantity is each stack's

  \label{fig:eval:pressure-stacks}
\end{figure}

Three properties of these traces bear on the rest of the chapter, and the first
two are limitations of the signal rather than results.

The pressure state is not a calibrated pressure on any stack. Against the
model's own nominal supply of $\unit[340]{bar}$ the median differential pressure
sits well outside it in magnitude on most cylinders, in both directions, and the
peaks reach several times it. On \ppoROM{} the excursions are clipped by a
numerical guard at $\unit[1500]{bar}$, and that clip binds on the slew and the
first boom cylinder and not on the other two, so those panels show a flattened
trace where the guard is active. On the inverse and forward path no such clip is
applied, so the two stacks are not bounded in the same way and their absolute
levels are not directly comparable. What is comparable, and what the figure is
for, is the \emph{shape} of the response either side of the change.

Read that way the traces support the reading of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:attribution}. The pressure state moves at the change of
load on every stack, so the information the residual would need is present in
the channel it reads. The adaptive arm's advantage, however, does not appear as
a transient following that movement: it is already present before it. The
channel carries the operating condition continuously, and the residual uses it
that way.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Training Budget}
\label{sec:eval:budget}

The reported arms are trained for $400$ iterations, which was the protocol budget
for every arm from the start. Resuming the same adaptive arm to $1200$ iterations
still passes both criteria, at $g = +85.5\,\%$ cut height and $+39.1\,\%$
lateral against the base (Equation~\ref{eq:eval:ratio-gain}), so the result is not an artefact of stopping at a fortunate
point. Held-out performance is nonetheless lower at $1200$ than at $400$, which
is ordinary overfitting to the training plants, and the shorter budget is what is
reported because it is what was declared.

\subsection{What This Experiment Does Not Yet Establish}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation-limits}

Two limitations bound the claim, and both are matters of evidence not yet
collected rather than of interpretation.

The result is demonstrated on one seed. Under the same objective a second seed
trades the two errors against each other rather than improving both, at
$-7.4\,\%$ cut height and $+24.9\,\%$ lateral, and three further seeds were in
training when this was written. The pre-registered criterion is that the median
of five seeds passes. Until that table exists the defensible claim is that the
mechanism was diagnosed and repaired and the repair demonstrated on a seed, not
that online adaptation improves both errors in general.

The falsification control is also outstanding. A sham condition, in which the
flip schedule fires but the mass never changes, separates adaptation from a
policy that has simply learned to track better. Under the earlier objective the
sham showed $g = +29.3\,\%$, meaning a substantial part of the measured gain there
was improved tracking rather than response to the plant. That control has not
been repeated under the objective reported here, and until it is, the share of
the gain attributable to adaptation specifically is bounded above rather than
measured.


\section{Integrated Discussion}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

\subsection*{RQ5: What the Actuator Representation Changes}

\emph{How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking, command
behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?}

Substantially, and not uniformly. On the matched pair, driving the plant through
the frozen inverse and forward models rather than the command reduced-order
model lowers mean lateral error from $\unit[0.1026]{m}$ to $\unit[0.0703]{m}$
and cut height from $8.4$ to $\unit[6.3]{mm}$
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}). The command behaviour behind that is the more
informative result: \ppoROM{} spends the \emph{least} actuation and tracks
worst, reversing direction $40$ times a second against $10.6$ for \ppoIDFD, so
its effort is largely spent fighting itself
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:effort}). Representation buys tracking by buying
smoothness, not effort, and that also explains its tool-acceleration and impact
figures.

Latency separates the representations in the hydraulic half of the chain but not
in the task. A shorter path from command to cylinder does not produce a quicker
response at the tool, because the response is set by the command rate limit and
the policy's own dynamics rather than by the actuation chain
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:step-response}). This is the most transferable finding of
the strand: a faster actuator path is not by itself a design goal on a machine
of this class.

Robustness does not separate them, and both are robust in the strong sense.
Every closed-loop figure here is measured on held-out meshes, eleven trajectory
families scored individually, and plants drawn from sixteen reserved factor
configurations disjoint from training. Underneath, the frozen inverse model was
identified on Simscape alone and transfers to all $118$ deployment runs with
millimetre-scale one-step error, flat across domains
(Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}). Generalisation across simulator, plant
draw, terrain and trajectory is established for both, without online adaptation.

Robustness \emph{beyond} that population is a weaker claim than the sweep
suggests. Tracking does not move across it, but on the inverse/forward plant the
sweep varied no hydraulics at all, and on \ppoROM{} the upper half is pinned to
the training envelope and cannot remove more than $62\,\%$ of actuator authority
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). No boundary was located within a
bounded instrument; that is not the same as insensitivity, and locating it is
left to Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design}.

\subsection*{RQ6: Whether Online Adaptation Pays}

\emph{Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable from
recent observations, and does online adaptation conditioned on that history
improve closed-loop performance?}

It is identifiable, but only from data collected to make it so, and it pays
where the correction has authority to act.

On identifiability, this chapter reports no measurement of its own. The
argument is structural and belongs to the data: a task controller computes its
command from the state, so command and response are close to redundant and a
plant descriptor has little left to explain
(Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}). That is why the open-loop
collection exists. The check is cheap, needs no estimator, and belongs before
the decision to build one, which is the transferable part of the finding.

What the deployed stack does with that history is narrower than the phrase
``latent identification'' suggests, and the distinction matters for what this
answer supports. The explicit plant latent, decodable against the true factors,
belongs to the AAM and is the route that fails at delivery. The mechanism
scored below conditions a bounded residual on a causal state--action window and
trains its encoder end to end for control; nothing constrains its internal
representation to carry physical parameters, and no experiment here tests
whether it does. The claim is therefore about history conditioning, not about
plant identification.

On whether it pays, the first attempt said no, and the reason was delivery
rather than estimation: the correction was added to a command already resting on
its own clip, so it was clipped away before reaching the plant
(Section~\ref{sec:models:chain}). Removing the inverse model from the command
path changes the answer. Against its own frozen base the adaptive arm improves
cut height from $\unit[7.964]{mm}$ to $\unit[3.772]{mm}$ and lateral from
$\unit[61.155]{mm}$ to $\unit[35.371]{mm}$, reductions of $52.6\,\%$ and
$42.2\,\%$, every paired interval excluding zero
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}). The capacity-matched control
divides the credit: capacity alone buys cut-height accuracy but leaves lateral
error at baseline, so the lateral gain belongs to the history input.

The mechanism is continuous conditioning rather than event response. The
advantage is present before the load changes and does not widen after it
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:attribution}). For a machine that settles over seconds
this is the more useful of the two: a controller that must wait for a detectable
transient would spend most of an episode no better than its baseline.

\subsection*{Relation to the Research Gap}

Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals} positioned this work against history-based
adaptation methods that infer a plant code and condition a policy on it. Two
conditions those arguments leave implicit are made measurable here: whether the
data identifies the plant at all (Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection}), and
whether the correction has authority to act
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). Both are cheap and both precede
estimator training.

A third follows from the results together. Rapid motor adaptation is
demonstrated on legged robots, where the plant changes discontinuously at
contact and the correction window is a fraction of a gait
cycle~\cite{lee2020learning,kumar2021rma}; there feedback cannot substitute for
identification. This machine is the opposite in its time constants, so
conditioning buys little from the transient that follows a change. What it buys
is the standing operating point. The criterion is therefore better stated in two
parts: conditioning on a transient pays where plant variation acts faster than
the loop can observe and correct it, and conditioning on the operating point
pays wherever a fixed policy must serve a family of plants it cannot otherwise
distinguish. The literature states the first and leaves the second implicit.
