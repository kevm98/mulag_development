\chapter{Experimental Evaluation: Representation, Robustness, and the Value of Adaptation}
\label{chap:evaluation}
\chaptermark{Experimental Evaluation: Representation, Robustness, and the Value of Adaptation}
\fancyhead[RE]{\headfont\small\nouppercase{\leftmark}}

This chapter evaluates the closed-loop stacks defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:control}
as an evidence chain. Sections~7.2 to~7.4 first ask whether the actuator
representation matters in closed loop and how much plant variation the trained
controllers already absorb. Section~7.5 then asks what remains for a residual to
recover inside \ppoAAMFD{}, whether live history explains that recovery, and
whether any component varies with cutter mass. Section~7.6 states the conditions
under which the evidence supports online adaptation.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:eval:hypotheses}

\subsection{Test Conditions and Comparisons}
\label{sec:eval:matrix}

All closed-loop results use held-out terrain meshes, eleven trajectory families,
a truck speed of \unit[4]{km/h}, a \unit[26]{m} scoring window, and sixteen
reserved plant configurations. The matched comparison is between \ppoROM{} and
\ppoIDFD{}. Adaptation is assessed only inside \ppoAAMFD{}, by comparing a
frozen base, a capacity-matched residual without history, and a
history-conditioned residual.

The results are paired by environment or by mesh and trajectory family. Error
metrics are reported separately for lateral tracking and cut height because the
two errors describe different aspects of the task. Confidence intervals are
obtained by paired bootstrap. Checkpoints are selected using the validation or
training criterion specified for each experiment and are then evaluated once on
the held-out set.

\subsection{Comparative Scope and Scoring}
\label{sec:eval:standing}

\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} form the cross-stack comparison. \ppoAAMFD{} has a
different action contract and is therefore not ranked against that pair. Its
results are interpreted only through within-stack comparisons.

\subsection{Scoring Horizon}
\label{sec:eval:horizon}

All reported closed-loop results use the full \unit[26]{m} trajectory rather
than a shorter selection window. The earlier \unit[8.9]{m} selection window
covered only about one third of an episode and is therefore treated as an
evaluation-protocol limitation, not as evidence about adaptation.

\section{Actuator Representation and Dynamic Response}
\label{sec:eval:delay}

The same matched pair is now examined in the actuation channel: transport
delay, closed-loop step response, and command effort.

\label{sec:eval:delay-plant}

\unit[117]{ms}. Cylinder extension and joint motion began at the same time,

similar values across excitation amplitudes indicate that this delay is a stable

\section{Generalisation and Robustness}
\label{sec:eval:robustness}

The preceding sections use the held-out evaluation set. This section establishes
the robustness already present before adaptation is considered, and then applies
a common authority reduction to all stacks.

The matched stacks generalise to the held-out meshes, trajectory families, and
reserved plant configurations used in this chapter. The randomisation sweep
showed little change in tracking over the tested mechanical variation. Its
hydraulic interpretation is limited because the inverse and forward plant does
not apply the hydraulic factors, while the reduced-order model constrains them
to its identification range.

The held-out evaluation therefore establishes transfer within the tested
simulation population. It does not establish performance on arbitrary hydraulic
conditions or physical hardware.

\subsection{Delivered Stress}
\label{sec:eval:delivered-stress}

The sweep therefore supports robustness to the tested changes in tool mass and
centre-of-mass height. It does not establish robustness to unbounded hydraulic
variation. A common authority perturbation was used to compare all stacks more
directly. This separates a reduction in realised actuator motion from the
specific randomisation factors used during deployment.

\subsection{Actuator Authority}
\label{sec:eval:boundary-design}

Actuator authority was reduced by scaling the realised actuator displacement:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eval:authority}
  \Delta x^{\text{applied}}_k = \kappa\,\Delta x^{\text{actuator}}_k,
  \qquad \kappa \in (0,1].
\end{equation}

Here, $\kappa=1$ represents nominal authority and smaller values reduce the
motion delivered to the plant. The same perturbation is applied after each
stack's actuator representation, which makes the comparison independent of the
individual factor parameterisations.

  \label{fig:eval:kappa-stacks}
\end{figure}

The learned controllers retain a broad margin under authority loss. A hard

No stack reached the contact or safety gate. Within \ppoAAMFD{}, the adaptive
residual holds the frozen residual's nominal lateral error down to
$\kappa=0.30$. This is an authority margin inside \ppoAAMFD{}. It is not a
ranking of \ppoAAMFD{} against \ppoROM{} or \ppoIDFD{}.

\subsection{What Robustness Leaves for Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:robustness-residual}

The robustness result sets the scale for the following experiment. The frozen
controllers already absorb substantial variation, and the authority sweep does
not reach a contact or safety failure. The adaptation study therefore measures
residual performance differences beyond a robust learned controller. Modest,
channel-specific, or seed-dependent gains are compatible with this setting
because the baseline leaves only a limited deficit to recover.

\section{Online Adaptation Beyond the Robust Baseline}
\label{sec:eval:adaptation}

The following experiments remain inside \ppoAAMFD{} and use the identified
forward model from Chapter~\ref{chap:models}. They measure three distinct
effects: improvement over the frozen base, improvement due to live history
beyond matched residual capacity, and variation of the residual advantage with
cutter mass. Keeping these effects separate prevents additional model capacity
from being interpreted as online identification.

\subsection{Experiment and Measurement}
\label{sec:eval:instrument}

The online-adaptation experiment changes cutter mass during each episode. The
change time and direction vary across environments. The three compared arms
share the frozen base policy, residual architecture, training budget, and
reward. The base has no residual, the control has a residual with a zeroed
history window, and the adaptive arm receives recent state-action history.

The primary plant configuration assigns nominal and heavy cutter masses across
the environment batch and changes the assignment at a jittered time. The varying
event time prevents the residual from relying on one fixed schedule. Since the
history-conditioned residual is active throughout the episode, this experiment
tests continuous conditioning on the operating point as well as the response to
the event.

Results use $880$ paired environments across five held-out meshes. Lateral and
cut-height effects are evaluated separately. Cut-height effects smaller than
approximately $10\,\%$ are treated as unresolved because of the measurement
resolution; the corresponding lateral threshold is approximately $1.5\,\%$.
The paired design ensures that each arm is assessed under the same terrain,
trajectory, and mass condition. The pre-registered primary criterion requires
both error channels to improve with paired intervals excluding zero. Outcomes
that improve one channel while leaving the other unresolved are reported as
secondary trade-offs; they do not change the primary criterion.

\label{sec:eval:dose}

the event schedule runs but the mass does not change. In the higher-dose runs,
half of the environments start at the heavier mass and the later flip exchanges

\label{sec:eval:pressure-signals}

internal hydraulic state changes across the load event; it is not used to
quantify adaptation performance or hydraulic accuracy. Figure~\ref{fig:eval:pressure-stacks}
shows the internal state per cylinder for all three stacks on the same plant and
aligned on the same event.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/ch7/ch7_pressure_stacks}
  \caption{Median differential pressure per cylinder through the change of load,
  $151$ environments aligned on their own flip instant. The quantity is each
  stack's own actuator-model state, internal to the command ROM for \ppoROM{}

Two properties of the signal govern how the figure is read. The pressure state
is not a calibrated pressure on any stack: in this window the medians run to
\unit[2295]{bar} and the peaks to \unit[3784]{bar}, which is past what a
cylinder of this size could develop. The rod-force channel carries no supervised
weight and pressure-increment accuracy is only $R^2 = 0.06$ to $0.09$
(Section~\ref{sec:models:rom}). On \ppoROM{} the excursions are additionally
clipped by the \unit[1500]{bar} numerical guard, which binds on the slew and
the first boom cylinder and not on the other two. There is therefore no common
physical level the panels can be read against, and each cylinder is drawn on its
own scale. What the figure supports is the shape of the response either side of
the change, not the level.

Read that way, the traces show that the model-derived pressure state responds
through the event. The adaptive advantage is already present before it, which is
consistent with continuous conditioning on the operating point. The uncalibrated
state is not evidence for the magnitude or cause of the tracking improvement.

\section{Conditions for Useful Online Adaptation}
\label{sec:eval:discussion}

The experiments identify four conditions that govern whether online adaptation
adds value beyond a robust learned controller.

\textbf{A control-relevant deficit must remain.} The transferred controllers
already absorb substantial plant variation and retain a broad authority margin.
Adaptation has little to recover when that robustness keeps the tracking error
nearly unchanged.

\textbf{The relevant variation must be observable.} The identifiability results
of Chapters~\ref{chap:data} and~\ref{chap:models} show that plant information is
present only under suitable excitation. A history encoder cannot recover a
factor that the measured trajectory does not reveal.

\textbf{The correction path must retain authority.} The earlier explicit
latent adapter produced no closed-loop effect because clipping removed the
correction before it reached the plant. The residual on the valve path has
bounded authority and can change the realised command.

\textbf{History must add value beyond residual capacity.} At the primary
endpoint, live history improves both channels on the two successful seeds. The
secondary checkpoint analysis shows that a fixed residual can explain much of
the cut-height gain on other selections. History is therefore useful
conditionally, and estimator quality alone does not establish a closed-loop
benefit.

The chapter supports an architecture-level result: a history-conditioned
residual can produce a resolved plant-dependent correction on a command path
with remaining authority. The method-level result is weaker because the primary
criterion is met by two of five seeds. Robustness, observability, residual
capacity, and correction authority must therefore be evaluated together before
online adaptation is justified.
