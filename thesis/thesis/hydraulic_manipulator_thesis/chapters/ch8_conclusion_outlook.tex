\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}
\fancyhead[RE]{\headfont\nouppercase{\leftmark}}

This thesis asked how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from high-fidelity
simulation can be reduced, transferred into a computationally efficient robotic
simulation and used for reinforcement-learning control, and under what
conditions online adaptation adds a further increment to the robustness those
learned models already provide. The six research questions of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions} are answered in the order they were
asked, which is also the order in which the answers depend on one another.

\section{Summary of Findings}

The findings are stated in the order of the research questions. The first four
concern how hydraulic actuation is learned, transferred and controlled, and the
last two concern robustness and online adaptation. Figure~\ref{fig:concl:evidence-chain}
makes the dependency between those experiments explicit.

\textbf{The inverse and forward representation identified in Simscape transfers
to the deployment simulation without retraining (RQ1, RQ2).} The inverse model
was trained on Simscape data alone and evaluated on all $118$ IsaacLab
deployment runs, drawn entirely outside that training distribution, where its
teacher-forced one-step extension error is millimetre-scale and nearly flat
across domains: $\unit[2.36]{mm}$ on the combined runs against $\unit[3.36]{mm}$
on nominal. This claim is limited to the one-step regime used by the policy.
The inverse model remains frozen downstream. The reduced-order model received a
small bounded calibration against the deployment plant.

\textbf{Predictive accuracy must be accompanied by a control-authority test
(RQ1).} Input ablation and open-loop excitation showed that a learned actuator
must be tested for dependence on the command, not only for prediction error.
With the accepted excitation design, removing the valve input increases rollout
error by a factor of $1.81$. This is a general validation requirement for
learned models used in control.

\textbf{Two actuator representations both support closed-loop control, and they
do not produce the same controller (RQ3, RQ5).} The command-conditioned
reduced-order model reaches $R^2 = 0.998$ on the internal valve state and $0.97$
on extension, with a diagonally dominant extension--command Jacobian of
condition number $2.14$, and it runs inside GPU-parallel training. The
inverse--forward chain composes two separately learned models in series, which
could have compounded their errors and did not. The two representations differ
in the control strategy they induce from the same policy interface. \ppoROM{}
spends the least command and reverses about $40$ times per second, against
$10.6$ for \ppoIDFD, which converts greater actuator effort into lower lateral
error. The choice of representation therefore affects the resulting controller
and is not an implementation detail.

\ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} agree on every recorded training field and differ only
in the actuator path, so the difference above \emph{is} attributable to the
representation. \ppoAAMFD{} is a separately trained run at a different seed, a
lower actor learning rate and no frozen-actor warm-up, reported as a deployed
controller rather than a ranked competitor. Only one training run exists per
stack, so seed variance is not estimated even for the matched pair, and claims
about the adaptation path are carried instead by the paired within-stack
comparison of Section~\ref{sec:eval:attribution}.

\textbf{A learned policy can replace the classical controller at runtime
(RQ4).} The damped-least-squares controller supplies privileged demonstrations
during training and is then removed; the distilled policy emits desired cylinder
motion directly into the learned actuator and is the entire deployed controller.
Over $33$ evaluation cells \ppoIDFD{} holds a mean lateral error of
$\unit[70.3]{mm}$, a median of $\unit[28.1]{mm}$ and $95.29\,\%$ ground contact
(Table~\ref{tab:eval:tracking}). The deployed policies remain anchored to the
demonstrated behaviour because the reward is an imperfect proxy for the
evaluation gates.

\textbf{The robust stacks retain a broad margin without online adaptation
(RQ2, RQ5).} Every closed-loop result is
measured on held-out meshes, eleven trajectory families scored individually, and
plants drawn from sixteen reserved factor configurations disjoint from training
by construction. Under those conditions \ppoIDFD{} holds the lateral criterion in
$32$ of $33$ cells at a median of $\unit[0.0281]{m}$, and the frozen inverse
model beneath it, identified on Simscape alone, transfers to all $118$
deployment runs with millimetre-scale one-step error, flat across domains. This
is the broadest claim the work supports and it requires no plant estimation.

The factor sweep supports the tested mechanical variation, but not unbounded
hydraulic variation. On the inverse and forward path, hydraulic factors are not
applied; on \ppoROM{} they remain inside the identification range
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). Reducing realised actuator motion to
one per cent of nominal did not trigger the safety or ground-contact gates.
The result therefore establishes an authority margin, not a hard robustness
boundary (Section~\ref{sec:eval:boundary-design}).

\textbf{Online plant context is usable, and whether it reaches the closed loop
is set by the correction interface (RQ6).} The explicit mechanism estimates a
plant latent from recent interaction history. Its latent-conditioned adapters
beat a zero-context control on the adapter objective on all five seeds, and the
same correction changes no closed-loop metric, because it is added to an inverse
command already resting against its limit for most of the scored window. Moving
the correction onto the valve path restores the authority the residual needed.
At the pre-registered endpoint the history-conditioned residual there succeeds
on four of five training seeds, so the median seed passes; three of those seeds
improve both channels. The fixed final
checkpoint returns three of five under the same rule and preserves the
direction, so the result does not depend on the checkpoint rule. Capacity-matched
controls then locate the source of that gain: an equal-capacity residual reading
zeroed history also beats the frozen base on four of five seeds, so added
residual capacity rather than live history accounts for the median improvement.
Live history separates from that control on one seed at the selected checkpoint
and on a different seed at the final one. The cutter-mass dose experiment
resolves a plant-dependent lateral component that grows with load, but it scores
the residual against the frozen base rather than against the zero-history
control, so it does not say whether that component is capacity or history. The
supported finding is therefore a method-level gain from the bounded residual,
with online adaptation as a conditional increment whose evidence is
seed-specific.

\section{Design Implications}

The results carry design consequences for each of the three parts of this work:
how a hydraulic actuator is identified and transferred, how a policy is trained
to act through it, and how plant adaptation is fitted around both. They are
stated below in that order, because each stage constrains the next.

\subsection*{Identification and Transfer}

\textbf{Design the excitation for identifiability, and test the model by input
ablation.} Task-control data can make state and command nearly redundant.
Dedicated excitation makes command effects identifiable. A learned actuator
should therefore be accepted on both prediction accuracy and loss of performance
when the command input is removed.

\textbf{Embed known physics and bound learned corrections.} The reduced-order
model propagates pressure, force, velocity, and extension through explicit
updates. Its learned terms are bounded, preventing unphysical state changes
inside the training loop. This structure also represents the pressure dependence
of valve authority directly.

\textbf{Prefer a frozen model with a bounded, gated correction to retraining.}
The inverse model transferred across the deployment plant family at
millimetre scale in every domain, from $\unit[2.36]{mm}$ to $\unit[3.36]{mm}$
one-step, and with an ordering that does not follow plant severity: the nominal
plant scores worst and the combined draws best
(Table~\ref{tab:models:id-trust}). It was therefore deployed frozen everywhere
downstream. Where a deployment offset did have to be absorbed, it was absorbed
by a correction capped at $\unit[3]{mm}$ and gated to vanish at zero command,
with every hydraulic parameter frozen and the original data retained as replay.
That construction preserves the identified physics, keeps a commanded hold a
hold, and confines the deployment-specific part of the model to a component
whose authority is bounded by construction.

\subsection*{Control Methodology}

\textbf{Constrain policy updates when the reward is not the evaluation
criterion.} The physical evaluation gates and the reward are not identical. A
forward-KL anchor to a verified reference policy bounds policy drift, and the
anchor advances only through checkpoints that satisfy the gates. Reward terms
should represent quantities that are also treated as evaluation constraints.

\textbf{Where no baseline is inherited, the action contract is the safety
argument.} The deployed policy is not a residual: a zero action leaves the arm
stationary, and nothing is summed with the policy output at runtime. Authority
is therefore bounded by the chain the action passes through rather than by
proximity to a nominal controller, with a rate limit, a filter, a shield that
fades authority as travel headroom is consumed, a command scale, and an
anti-windup cap on how far the integrated target may lead the measured
extension. That last cap is load-bearing and axis-dependent: relaxing it from
$\unit[18]{mm}$ to $\unit[50]{mm}$ halved lateral error on the families needing a
sustained standing offset, while the same relaxation on the head-pivot cylinder
drove alignment from $0.9507$ to $0.4636$.

\subsection*{Plant Adaptation}

\textbf{Measure identifiability and correction authority before training an
estimator.} The identifiability envelope determines whether plant identity is
present in the data. The delivered-stress check determines whether a
perturbation reaches the plant and whether a correction can influence the
command. Both tests should precede estimator training.

Where the estimate is used also matters. A dynamics model can integrate a
changing estimate over time, whereas a memoryless policy receives that change as
input variation. Filtering on the policy path may therefore be useful, but its
appropriate time constant remains to be determined on the deployed stack.

\section{Contributions}

Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions} stated six contributions. Their standing
at the end of the work is as follows, with the supporting evidence as summarised
above rather than repeated.

\textbf{Transferable hydraulic actuator learning} is delivered: both
representations were identified from Simscape and realised in IsaacLab, and the
inverse model transfers without deployment-specific retraining. The
\textbf{reduced-order actuator model} is delivered and carries everything
downstream, being accurate enough to serve as the deployment plant and cheap
enough to evaluate inside GPU-parallel training, which the stiff implicit solver
of the model it replaces cannot support; it was calibrated against IsaacLab and
is therefore the reference condition rather than an independent competitor.

The \textbf{control-relevant identification and validation} procedure is
delivered and is the contribution most likely to transfer independently of this
machine, covering both the input-ablation test that exposed a forward model with
no command authority and the identifiability audit that established the
excitation envelope.

\textbf{Policy-only control through learned hydraulics} is demonstrated. After
the classical teacher is removed, \ppoIDFD{} holds a mean lateral error of
\unit[70]{mm} and a median of \unit[28]{mm} over $33$ held-out cells at
$95\,\%$ ground contact (Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}). Worst-cell
aggregation still misses two gates; that bound is stated in
Section~\ref{sec:concl:limitations}.

The \textbf{decision procedure for online adaptation} is delivered, and its main
product is the discipline that produced it rather than any single number. It
separates residual plant sensitivity, observability, correction authority and
residual capacity before a gain is attributed to adaptation. The same evaluation
discipline also exposes a broader control-design lesson: a quantity which is
gated but not priced is traded away by the optimiser.

\textbf{Where online adaptation is effective} is answered at two levels rather
than with a single verdict. The explicit mechanism is informative at the model
level and has no closed-loop effect through the saturated inverse command, and
the implicit mechanism that replaced it acts on the valve path, where authority
remains. The primary evaluation yields four successful
seeds and one trade-off, and the final-checkpoint analysis returns three
successes, so the median training run improves under both rules. The
capacity-matched comparison then locates the source of that gain: a residual of
the same capacity reading zeroed history reproduces most of it, while live
history separates from that control on one seed of five under either checkpoint
rule. The cutter-mass dose study resolves a plant-dependent lateral component,
but does not separate static mass dependence from the later mass exchange. The
supported method-level claim is a bounded residual on the valve path. The
supported adaptation-specific claim is narrower: live history contributes where
residual capacity alone does not, and where load variation stays visible in the
scored channel.

Two enablers are recorded rather than claimed. The causal plant estimator and
the bounded adaptation path behave as specified, with no privileged access, no
future information, bounded output and reversion to the robust actuator path on
invalid input. Of the eight-gate promotion criterion, one property is worth
reuse: five gates separate the matched pair and carry the comparative claims,
while lateral, hard impacts and tool acceleration do not
(Section~\ref{sec:sys:gates}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl:limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion:limitations}

\textbf{Scope of plant validation.} The evidence is simulation-to-simulation,
and the deployment plant is itself a learned model. No claim here is a claim
about hardware.

\textbf{Scope of hydraulic observability.} The differential pressure the estimator consumes is produced by the actuator
model rather than measured, so transferring the model-level result to hardware
requires instrumentation the FME~500 does not carry. An observation contract
remains unresolved alongside this: every result uses the pressure-inclusive row,
while the stricter deployable alternative excludes measured differential
pressure. Since the estimator's advantage is concentrated in that channel, the
deployable variant should be expected to perform worse, and by how much is not
established.

\textbf{Scope of controller comparison.} On worst-mesh, worst-family statistics
both stacks fail the lateral gate and \ppoIDFD{} does not satisfy the
head-alignment gate under worst-case aggregation
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:tracking}), so neither stack satisfies the whole
eight-gate evaluation contract. The closed-loop comparisons resolve differences to a few per cent over $33$
paired conditions and do not establish equivalence. The available plant
variation bounds the disturbance rather than the architecture, and more tightly
than the sweep's labels suggest: a stack shown to absorb a $40\%$ reduction in
valve effectiveness and a nominal doubling of every deployment factor has been
shown to tolerate what actually reached it, which on the inverse/forward plant
is a mechanical perturbation only. The three
stacks are matched on evaluation but not on training budget, so the comparison
describes three deployed controllers rather than three equally tuned training
procedures.

\textbf{Scope of training variation.} Policy-training variation is not quantified for
the matched R and IF pair, and it bounds
two claims differently. R and IF are each represented by one training run, so
the $33$ paired cells measure terrain and trajectory variation and say nothing
about how far a second run of either would move; the actuator-representation
result rests on that assumption. The adaptation result includes five training
seeds and meets the primary criterion on four, so it establishes a median
improvement and not a uniform one. The defensible claim is an
architecture-level correction on the identified valve path, together with a
plant-dependent lateral term under the tested cutter-mass doses.

\textbf{Scope of the adaptation evidence.} The commanded step cannot finish faster than $417$\,ms at the command-rate limit,
while the plant answers in about $117$\,ms. That loop is a hard case for
adaptation understood as a reaction to a single event and favours continuous
conditioning on the operating point.
Appendix~\ref{app:pressure-signals} is consistent with the second reading:
the adaptive advantage is already present before the load change.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:dose} shows a cutter-mass-dependent lateral term relative
to the $1.0$ condition, which the coupled dose design does not resolve into a
transient adaptation response.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl:future}

The evidence in this thesis is simulation-to-simulation. The next step is to
evaluate the learned actuator models and distilled policy on the target machine.

That step begins with instrumentation, because the FME~500 as delivered carries
no cylinder-chamber pressure transducers, and the differential pressure the
models propagate exists here only as an actuator-model state. Fitting pressure
transducers, extension sensing and a rod load pin converts three modelled
quantities into measured ones. The collection instrument is already designed and
validated: the open-loop excitation schedule of Chapter~\ref{chap:data} was built
to make the valve causally identifiable, and the identifiability audit of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} applies to machine data unchanged.

One simulation experiment should precede that step, because it targets the
weakest link in the present result. Every history encoder trained here reads the
full observation, $1400$ numbers over the window, of which almost all describe
terrain and task state rather than plant identity, and a head of this capacity
can fit that noise. Restricting the encoder input to plant-relevant channels, or
fitting it by regression onto a privileged plant vector instead of through the
policy gradient as rapid motor adaptation does~\cite{kumar2021rma}, is the
direct test of whether the missing history effect is a property of the plant or
of the encoder.

Hardware data would support a bounded correction to the frozen inverse model,
with hydraulic parameters retained and the original dataset used for replay.
Input ablation should verify that the correction remains dependent on the valve
command. The reference-anchored policy provides a suitable starting point
because it limits policy drift from a verified checkpoint.

The central design lesson is conditional. Robust training absorbs a large part
of the plant variation before online adaptation is introduced. A remaining
correction is useful only when the variation is observable, the fixed controller
leaves a deficit to recover, and the command path retains authority. Apparent
adaptation gains must also be separated from gains due to residual capacity.
Evaluating these conditions together provides a defensible basis for deciding
when online adaptation is justified on a hydraulic manipulator.
