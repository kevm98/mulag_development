\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis asked how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from high-fidelity
simulation can be reduced, transferred into a computationally efficient robotic
simulation and used for reinforcement-learning control, and under what
conditions online adaptation adds anything to the robustness those learned
models already provide. The six research questions of
Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions} are answered in the order they were
asked, which is also the order in which the answers depend on one another.

\section{Summary of Findings}

The findings are stated below in the order of the research questions. The first
four concern how hydraulic actuation is learned, transferred and controlled. The
last two concern online adaptation.

\textbf{The inverse and forward representation identified in Simscape transfers
to the deployment simulation without retraining (RQ1, RQ2).} The inverse model
was trained on randomised Simscape campaigns and evaluated on all $118$ IsaacLab
deployment runs, drawn entirely outside that training distribution, where its
teacher-forced one-step extension error is millimetre-scale and nearly flat
across domains: $\unit[2.36]{mm}$ on the combined runs against
$\unit[3.36]{mm}$ on nominal, with the held-out group no worse than the domains
used in training. The transfer claim is made in that one-step regime, which is
how the policy uses the model; fed its own predictions instead, the composite
diverges in every run, for the saturation reason set out in
Section~\ref{sec:models:id-trust}. No deployment-specific retraining was required, and
the inverse model stays frozen everywhere downstream. The reduced-order model is
the exception and is treated as one throughout: it received a small bounded
calibration against the deployment plant, so it is transferred with a correction
rather than without one.

\textbf{Supervised accuracy is not evidence of control authority (RQ1).} The
first forward model scored near-perfectly and had learned to ignore the valve
command, reconstructing motion from the state history instead. Nothing in the
validation loss showed it. Ablating the command exposed it, and redesigning the
excitation repaired it. Rollout error fell from $\unit[75.2]{mm}$ to
$\unit[12.4]{mm}$ at $\unit[0.5]{s}$ and from $\unit[157.9]{mm}$ to
$\unit[27.3]{mm}$ at $\unit[1.0]{s}$, and removing the valve input from the
repaired model then costs a factor of $1.81$. Excitation design and input
ablation are therefore reported here as required tests for a learned actuator
intended for control. This finding concerns how such a model is validated rather
than what it represents, so it applies beyond the machine studied here.

\textbf{Two actuator representations both support closed-loop control, and they
do not produce the same controller (RQ3, RQ5).} The command-conditioned
reduced-order model reaches $R^2 = 0.998$ on the internal valve state and $0.97$
on extension, with a diagonally dominant extension--command Jacobian of
condition number $2.14$, and it runs inside GPU-parallel training. The
inverse--forward chain composes two separately learned models in series, which
could have compounded their errors and did not. The two representations differ
in the control strategy they induce from the same policy interface. \ppoROM{}
spends the least command and reverses about $40$ times per second, against
$10.6$ for \ppoIDFD, which converts greater actuator effort into lower lateral
error. The choice of representation therefore affects the resulting controller
and is not an implementation detail.

The standing of that comparison is stated here because it governs how the three
stacks are read throughout. \ppoROM{} and \ppoIDFD{} agree on every recorded
training field and differ only in the actuator path, so the contrast between
them is controlled and the difference above \emph{is} attributable to the
representation. \ppoAAMFD{} is a separately trained run at a different seed,
a lower actor learning rate and no frozen-actor warm-up
(Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:constraints}); it is reported as a deployed controller
demonstrating the adaptive stack end to end, not as a ranked competitor, and no
claim here rests on its position relative to the other two. Only one training
run exists per stack, so seed variance is not estimated even for the matched
pair. Claims about the adaptation path are carried instead by a paired within-stack
comparison, in which three arms share the frozen base, the architecture, the
budget, the reward and the seed, and differ only in whether the residual can see
its history.

\textbf{A learned policy can replace the classical controller at runtime
(RQ4).} The damped-least-squares controller supplies privileged demonstrations
during training and is then removed; the distilled policy emits desired cylinder
motion directly into the learned actuator and is the entire deployed controller.
Over $33$ evaluation cells \ppoIDFD{} holds a mean lateral error of
$\unit[70.3]{mm}$, a median of $\unit[28.1]{mm}$ and $95.29\,\%$ ground
contact. Reward ascent alone does not deliver this result. Unconstrained
fine-tuning raised total return while degrading the primary tracking objective
by up to a factor of $12.9$, with the critic remaining healthy throughout. The
failure is therefore one of objective mismatch rather than of optimisation, and
it is the reason the deployed policies are anchored to the demonstrated
behaviour.

\textbf{The robust stacks generalise without online adaptation, but no
robustness boundary was located (RQ2, RQ5).} Every closed-loop result is
measured on held-out meshes, eleven trajectory families scored individually, and
plants drawn from sixteen reserved factor configurations disjoint from training
by construction. Under those conditions \ppoIDFD{} holds the lateral criterion in
$32$ of $33$ cells at a median of $\unit[0.0281]{m}$, and the frozen inverse
model beneath it, identified on Simscape alone, transfers to all $118$
deployment runs with millimetre-scale one-step error, flat across domains. This
is the broadest claim the work supports and it requires no plant estimation.

Pushing beyond that population establishes less than it appears to. Doubling
every deployment-factor deviation moves no stack's median, but the perturbation
path cannot deliver the stress it requests: the inverse/forward plant consumes
no factor input, so there the sweep varied tool mass and centre-of-mass height
alone, and on \ppoROM{} the hydraulic factors are clamped back to their
identified ranges above scale $1$, with at most $62\,\%$ of actuator authority
removable by that route (Section~\ref{sec:eval:delivered-stress}). What the
closed loop absorbs is therefore established only for the perturbations this
work can apply, and the point at which command authority becomes insufficient
remains unmeasured.

The remaining findings concern adaptation itself (RQ6).

\textbf{Under the controller, signal set and excitation regime examined here,
plant identity is recoverable only from deliberate excitation, and the limit has
a mechanism.} Data gathered while this task controller drives the machine does
not separate plants, because the command is computed from the state and the two
are nearly redundant. This is a statement about the dataset the controller
produces, not a claim that closed-loop identification is impossible in general:
dedicated closed-loop identification methods exist and were not applied.
Open-loop excitation repairs it, which is why the second collection of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} exists and why every model-level
result in this thesis is fitted on it. The amplitude at which identity stops
being recoverable, and whether the cause is actuator saturation or
operating-point drift, is a property of the collection rather than of any
controller; measuring it is listed in Section~\ref{sec:concl:future} among the
work still to be done.

\textbf{The estimator is not the limiting component (H1, H2, RQ6).} Under the
chain gate of Section~\ref{sec:models:chain} the causal estimate is not worse
than the privileged latent read from the true factors, so whatever the chain
loses, it does not lose it at estimation. What the estimate carries is mainly
seal condition (Section~\ref{sec:models:aam-content}), which acts through
pressure, and pressure is the one channel where the latent's advantage is
measurable. That is also the channel the controller does not integrate, which is
the first sign of the delivery problem below.

\textbf{Online adaptation pays where the correction has authority to act.}
Placing a bounded correction on the inverse model's output produced no
closed-loop effect, and the reason was delivery rather than estimation: that
command already rested on its own clip for most of the scored window, so the
correction was removed before it reached the plant. Rebuilding the stack so that
the policy emits the valve command itself, with the residual acting on that
command, changes the outcome. Under a mid-episode change of load the adaptive
arm lowers mean cut-height error from $\unit[7.964]{mm}$ to $\unit[3.772]{mm}$
and mean lateral error from $\unit[61.155]{mm}$ to $\unit[35.371]{mm}$, cuts of
$52.6\,\%$ and $42.2\,\%$, with every paired interval excluding zero
(H3, Section~\ref{sec:eval:adaptation}).

A capacity-matched control arm, identical but for a zeroed history window,
divides that result honestly. Added capacity alone already buys cut-height
accuracy, so part of that gain is not adaptation. It does not move lateral error
at all, leaving it within half a millimetre of the baseline, while the adaptive
arm improves it by more than three quarters. Lateral error moves only when the
history is live, and that is what attributes the effect to online sensing rather
than to parameters (H4). The claim is demonstrated on one seed and the
pre-registered bar of five is not yet met, so it stands as a demonstration
rather than a general result.

\section{Design Implications}

The results carry design consequences for each of the three parts of this work:
how a hydraulic actuator is identified and transferred, how a policy is trained
to act through it, and how plant adaptation is fitted around both. They are
stated below in that order, because that is the order in which they constrain a
successor design. Several were established by a measured failure rather than by
a successful result, and are stronger for it.

\subsection*{Identification and Transfer}

\textbf{Design the excitation for identifiability, and accept the model on an
ablation rather than on a score.} A task controller computes its command from
the state, so the two are nearly redundant and the valve is not causally
identifiable from the resulting data however varied the control effort looks:
the closed-loop batch spends a quarter of its time saturated and half at
mid-authority, and is still uninformative. A forward model trained on it reached
one-step $R^2 \approx 0.999$ while ignoring the valve entirely, and no
validation loss computed on that distribution could have revealed it. Zeroing the
command did, and open-loop excitation repaired it, at which point removing the
valve cost a factor of $1.81$. The implication is that a learned actuator
intended for control needs a dedicated excitation campaign and an input-ablation
acceptance test, and that predictive accuracy should never be the criterion on
which such a model is promoted.

\textbf{Embed the physics that is known and bound what is learned.} The
reduced-order model propagates pressure, force, velocity and extension through
explicit updates with trainable effective parameters, and confines the neural
terms to valve realisation, configuration load and residuals bounded to
$10\,\%$ of the pressure and velocity step limits and $\unit[1]{cm}$ on
position. That bound is what makes the model safe to place inside a training
loop: even a badly fitted residual cannot produce an unphysical state jump. The
structure also earns its keep at identification time, since encoding the
orifice and continuity relations makes the pressure dependence of valve
authority architectural rather than something to be rediscovered. Pinning a
single global valve gain instead was tried and rejected, because measured
authority varies by a factor of $15$--$75$ across pressure quartiles and can
change sign.

\textbf{Prefer a frozen model with a bounded, gated correction to retraining.}
The inverse model transferred across the deployment plant family with a spread
of only a factor of $1.09$ between the best and worst domain, and an ordering
that does not follow plant severity, so it was deployed frozen everywhere
downstream. Where a deployment offset did have to be absorbed, it was absorbed
by a correction capped at $\unit[3]{mm}$ and gated to vanish at zero command,
with every hydraulic parameter frozen and the original data retained as replay.
That construction preserves the identified physics, keeps a commanded hold a
hold, and confines the deployment-specific part of the model to a component
whose authority is bounded by construction.

\subsection*{Control Methodology}

\textbf{When the acceptance criterion is not the reward, bound the ascent and
price what is gated.} Unconstrained PPO from a distilled checkpoint raised total
return while degrading the primary tracking objective by up to a factor of
$12.9$, with critic explained variance healthy throughout. That is the optimizer
working correctly on the wrong functional: the reward is a proxy, acceptance is a
vector of physical gates, and the proxy's optimum lies outside the feasible set
while the distilled policy lies inside it. The remedy is to constrain the update
rather than rewrite the objective. A forward-KL anchor to a frozen reference
penalises the \emph{abandonment} of reference behaviour, the exploration scale is
held fixed so entropy collapse cannot shrink the divergence while the commanded
action drifts, and the anchor advances only through checkpoints that have passed
the gates.

The same discipline applies to the reward itself: a quantity that is gated but
not priced is traded away. The only adaptive checkpoint to clear the
ground-contact gate over the full path was the one whose reward priced cylinder
headroom explicitly, and an inherited formulation of $53$ weighted fields carried
three terms working against the task for thirteen campaigns, undetected because
logging reported an aggregate and omitted the two largest lateral terms. Shaping
must also retain a far-field gradient; the Gaussian form previously used was a
factor of $520$ weaker at $\unit[0.5]{m}$ of error, where the failing trajectory
families live.

\textbf{Where no baseline is inherited, the action contract is the safety
argument.} The deployed policy is not a residual: a zero action leaves the arm
stationary, and nothing is summed with the policy output at runtime. Authority
is therefore bounded by the chain the action passes through rather than by
proximity to a nominal controller, with a rate limit, a filter, a shield that
fades authority as travel headroom is consumed, a command scale, and an
anti-windup cap on how far the integrated target may lead the measured
extension. That last cap is load-bearing and axis-dependent: relaxing it from
$\unit[18]{mm}$ to $\unit[50]{mm}$ halved lateral error on the families needing a
sustained standing offset, while the same relaxation on the head-pivot cylinder
drove alignment from $0.9507$ to $0.4636$.

\subsection*{Plant Adaptation}

\textbf{Measure before you build, and route the latent to the consumer that
rewards it.} Two cheap measurements precede any estimator. The identifiability
envelope establishes whether plant identity is present in the data the machine
produces; the delivered-stress check establishes whether the perturbation
reaches the plant and whether the correction has authority left to act. Either
can end the effort before it is incurred, and on this task the second would have
shown that a correction placed on the inverse model's output could not move a
command already resting on its own limit, before the estimator, the adapter and
the conditioned policy were built. That conversion of an open-ended engineering
effort into a measurement is the design contribution this thesis most expects to
transfer.

Where the estimate is used matters as much as whether it exists. Its value lies
in variation over time rather than in the plant identity it carries, by a factor
of about thirty-two, and the two consumers want opposite things from that
variation: a one-step predictor gains $13.6\,\%$ from its movement, while a
memoryless actor integrates nothing, so a moving latent changes the action tick
to tick. Filtering on the policy path alone, at fixed content, improves
cut-height tracking by $14.5\,\%$ on the median in $22$ of $33$ cells with an
interior optimum at $\tau = \unit[0.796]{s}$. Two qualifications belong with
that: the temporal variance of the latent as the actor reads it does not order
with $\tau$, so the proposed cause is not the measured one, and the optimum may
belong to a policy trained on the unfiltered signal.

\section{Contributions}

Section~\ref{sec:intro:contributions} stated five contributions. Their standing
at the end of the work is as follows.

\textbf{Transferable hydraulic actuator learning} is delivered. Both
representations were identified from Simscape and realised in IsaacLab, and the
inverse model transfers without deployment-specific retraining.

The \textbf{control-relevant identification and validation} procedure is
delivered, and it is the contribution most likely to transfer independently of
this machine. It
covers both the input-ablation test that exposed a forward model with no command
authority and the identifiability audit that established the excitation envelope,
including the mechanism behind the upper amplitude limit.

The \textbf{reduced-order actuator model} is delivered and carries everything
downstream, being accurate enough to serve as the deployment plant and cheap
enough to evaluate inside GPU-parallel training, which the stiff implicit solver
of the high-fidelity model it replaces cannot support. The qualification stated throughout stands. It was calibrated against
IsaacLab and is therefore the reference condition rather than an independent
competitor to the inverse and forward pair.

\textbf{Policy-only control through learned hydraulics} is delivered for both
representations, with the classical controller absent from the deployed loop.

The measurements of \textbf{robustness and the limits of adaptation} are
delivered, and their main product is the discipline that produced them rather
than any single number. Two findings exist only because of it: that a selection
window covering $8.9$ of $26$\,m certifies a third of an episode, and that a
quantity which is gated but not priced is traded away by the optimizer. The
causal plant estimator and the bounded adaptation path behave as specified, with
no privileged access, no future information, bounded output, and reversion to
the robust actuator path on invalid input. They remain enablers of this
contribution rather than claims in their own right.

The \textbf{eight-gate promotion criterion} is delivered as the non-reward
criterion the training loop and the checkpoint selection both use, which is the
role it was built for and the role every result here gives it. One property is
recorded for reuse: five of the eight gates separate the matched pair and carry
the comparative claims, while lateral, hard impacts and tool acceleration do not
(Section~\ref{sec:sys:gates}). Re-deriving those three against the
widened trajectory library is specification work rather than control work.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion:limitations}

The evidence is simulation-to-simulation, and the deployment plant is itself a
learned model. No claim here is a claim about hardware.

The differential pressure the estimator consumes is produced by the actuator
model rather than measured, so transferring the model-level result to hardware
requires instrumentation the FME~500 does not carry. An observation contract
remains unresolved alongside this: every result uses the pressure-inclusive row,
while the stricter deployable alternative excludes measured differential
pressure. Since the estimator's advantage is concentrated in that channel, the
deployable variant should be expected to perform worse, and by how much is not
established.

The closed-loop comparisons resolve differences to a few per cent over $33$
paired conditions and do not establish equivalence. The available plant
variation bounds the disturbance rather than the architecture, and more tightly
than the sweep's labels suggest: a stack shown to absorb a $40\%$ reduction in
valve effectiveness and a nominal doubling of every deployment factor has been
shown to tolerate what actually reached it, which on the inverse/forward plant
is a mechanical perturbation only. The three
stacks are matched on evaluation but not on training budget, so the comparison
describes three deployed controllers rather than three equally tuned training
procedures.

Finally, the bound is a bound on this loop. A commanded step cannot complete
faster than $417$\,ms at the command rate limit while the plant answers in
$117$\,ms, so the task response is set downstream of anything a latent could
change within a transient. That is close to the least favourable case for
adaptation understood as \emph{response to an event}, and it is consistent with
the measurement: the adaptive arm's advantage does not widen when the load
moves. It says nothing against adaptation understood as continuous conditioning
on the operating point, which is the form that pays here. The finding
generalises through the mechanism rather than through the number.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl:future}

The evidence in this thesis is simulation-to-simulation, and the step that would
extend it furthest is the one it does not take: placing the learned actuator
models and the distilled policy on the machine they were built for.

That step begins with instrumentation, because the FME~500 as delivered carries
no cylinder-chamber pressure transducers, and the differential pressure the
models propagate exists here only as an actuator-model state. Fitting pressure
transducers, extension sensing and a rod load pin converts three modelled
quantities into measured ones. The collection instrument is already designed and
validated: the open-loop excitation schedule of Chapter~\ref{chap:data} was built
to make the valve causally identifiable, and the identifiability audit of
Section~\ref{sec:data:openloop-collection} applies to machine data unchanged.

Fine-tuning on that data is then a well-posed problem rather than an open-ended
one. The inverse model transferred across the deployment plant family with a
spread of only a factor of $1.09$, so the gap to a real machine is plausibly a
bounded offset rather than a different function, and the mechanism for absorbing
such an offset has already been validated once: a correction capped at
$\unit[3]{mm}$ and gated to vanish at zero command, with every hydraulic
parameter frozen and the original data retained as replay. The input-ablation
test of Section~\ref{sec:models:fd-identifiability} decides whether that
suffices, since a correction obliged to carry command authority is no longer a
correction. For the policy, the reference-anchored construction of
Section~\ref{sec:ctrl:anchored} transfers with its guarantee intact, and that
guarantee is worth more on a machine than in simulation: it holds the policy
within a bounded divergence of a checkpoint verified against physical acceptance
and restores it atomically when an update violates that bound.
