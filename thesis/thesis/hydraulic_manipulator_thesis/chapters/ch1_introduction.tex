\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{The Role of Mechanised Verge Maintenance}
\label{sec:intro:verge}

Road verges are cut because they are infrastructure. Vegetation left to grow
closes sight lines at junctions and bends, obscures signs and barriers, blocks
the drainage channels that keep water off the carriageway, and encroaches on the
running surface itself. A national road network carries tens of thousands of
kilometres of verge, and each kilometre is cut several times in a growing
season, so the work is continuous, repetitive and performed almost entirely by
machine.

It is also performed from a moving vehicle. Stopping to cut each section would
require closing the lane, so a carrier drives the road at a few kilometres per
hour while a boom-mounted mowing head works the verge alongside it. The head
must hold a cut height of a few centimetres over ground that rises and falls
continuously, follow a grass line that curves with the road, and do so without
striking the surface hard enough to damage the tool or lift the machine.

\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.82\textwidth]{images/fme500_operation}
  \caption{MULAG FME~500 front mower on a Unimog during roadside verge
  maintenance. Source: \cite{mulag2026fme500}.}
  \label{fig:intro:fme500}
\end{figure}

The demands on the manipulator follow from that. The machine studied here,
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro:fme500}, carries a heavy rotating tool at the end
of a long boom, works in intermittent contact with the ground,
and must place that tool accurately while its own base is in motion. Hydraulic
actuation is the standard answer at this scale: it supplies the force density
and the tolerance to shock loading that electric drives do not, and it does so
with components rugged enough for continuous outdoor operation. Precision,
however, is not what hydraulics are good at, and that tension is the subject of
this thesis.

\section{Advances in Learning-Based Control for Hydraulic Manipulators}
\label{sec:intro:advances}

Two developments have changed what is achievable on machines of this kind.

The first is that policy optimisation in simulation has become practical for
contact-rich manipulation. GPU-parallel physics engines run thousands of
environments simultaneously, which makes reinforcement learning feasible on
tasks that would take years of real-machine experience, and randomising the
simulator across a family of plants produces policies that transfer without
being tuned to one machine. The second is that the actuator itself can be
learned. Rather than deriving valve and cylinder behaviour from first
principles and fitting its parameters, a network trained on recorded motion can
represent the command-to-motion relationship directly, including the
nonlinearities that analytical models approximate away.

Combining the two is attractive and not straightforward. A learned actuator
model must be cheap enough to evaluate inside the training loop, which rules out
the high-fidelity models used for offline analysis, and it must remain valid
across the family of machines the policy will meet. This has motivated a line of
work in which a compact code describing the current plant is inferred from
recent interaction history and supplied to the controller, so that a single
policy can serve a family rather than a single machine. Such methods report
substantial gains in legged locomotion and in manipulation, and they are the
direct antecedent of the architecture examined here.

\section{Challenges of Precise Tracking with Hydraulic Mowing Arms}
\label{sec:intro:challenges}

A valve command does not produce a proportional cylinder motion. The
relationship is nonlinear, depends on load and supply pressure, and is subject
to dead time between the command and any measurable response. It also differs
between machines: seal condition, fluid temperature, hose compliance and the
attachment fitted all change it, so two machines of the same model respond
differently and one machine responds differently across a season. A controller
that inverts only the geometry therefore leaves the largest source of tracking
error unaddressed.

That difficulty admits two responses, and they are routinely conflated. The
first is robustness: train across a randomised family so that a single fixed
model is adequate everywhere. It costs nothing at run time and gives up the
difference between the family and the individual. The second is adaptation:
infer which member of the family is present from the interaction history and
condition the model or the policy on that estimate. It promises to recover that
difference, at the cost of an estimator that must work from observable signals
alone, since a deployed machine has no access to its own plant parameters.

The second response is only worth pursuing under conditions that are rarely
checked before the work is done. Plant identity must be recoverable from the
data the machine actually produces, which depends on how the machine is being
driven while that data is collected. And the difference between knowing the
plant and not knowing it must be large enough to matter once the estimate
reaches a feedback controller. Neither condition is guaranteed, both are
specific to the task, and both can be measured before an estimator is built.
Establishing those measurements, and applying them to a hydraulic mowing arm, is
the problem this thesis addresses.

\section{Contribution of This Thesis}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

The difficulty described in Section~\ref{sec:intro:challenges} is a modelling
problem before it is a control problem. A hydraulic actuator can be simulated
accurately, but the models that do so are too slow to sit inside a
reinforcement-learning loop that requires millions of environment steps. The
alternative usually taken is to discard the hydraulics and command joint
positions directly, which removes the load and pressure dependence that makes
the machine difficult to control. What is needed is a representation that is
cheap enough to train against and faithful enough that the resulting policy is
controlling something close to the real actuator.

Learned actuator models and latent-conditioned adaptation are both established
techniques, and Section~\ref{sec:intro:advances} described the progress that
makes them practical on machines of this class. What remains unclear is how such
models should be identified so that they retain the command dependence a
controller needs, whether they transfer between simulators without being
retrained, and whether online adaptation adds anything once a policy has been
trained against them. This thesis therefore treats representation and transfer
as the primary questions, and treats online adaptation as the last of them
rather than the first. The research statement of the thesis is as follows:

\researchstatement{To determine how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from
high-fidelity simulation can be reduced, transferred into a computationally
efficient robotic simulation and used for reinforcement-learning control, and
under what conditions online adaptation improves on the robustness those learned
models already provide.}

The statement decomposes into six research questions, which are answered in the
order given. The first three concern how the actuator is represented, the fourth
concerns control through that representation, and the last two concern what
remains for adaptation once the first four are settled. The six questions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How can data from a high-fidelity Simscape model be used to learn
    computationally tractable representations of hydraulic actuation while
    preserving the command dependence a controller requires?
  \item To what extent do actuator models identified from Simscape transfer to
    an IsaacLab manipulator without deployment-specific retraining?
  \item Can a physics-structured reduced-order hydraulic model supply dynamics
    that are accurate and stable enough for GPU-parallel policy training?
  \item Can a policy trained from a privileged classical teacher become the sole
    deployed controller while acting through learned actuator dynamics?
  \item How does the choice of actuator representation affect tracking, command
    behaviour, latency and robustness under plant variation?
  \item Where plant variation survives that robustness, is it identifiable from
    recent observations, and does conditioning the controller on it improve
    closed-loop performance?
\end{enumerate}

The first is answered in Chapters~\ref{chap:data} and~\ref{chap:models}, the
second and third in Chapter~\ref{chap:models}, the fourth in
Chapter~\ref{chap:control}, and the last two in
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

RQ6 is the question named in the title, and it is deliberately placed last. It
is only worth asking once the preceding questions have established that the
learned models transfer, that a policy can control through them, and how much
plant variation the resulting closed loop already absorbs unaided. A small
answer to RQ6 is then a result about the system rather than a failure of the
method, because accurate models and a robust policy leave less for adaptation to
recover. The work rests on the following contributions:

\textbf{Transferable Hydraulic Actuator Learning:} A pipeline that identifies
hydraulic actuation from high-fidelity Simscape data and transfers it into
IsaacLab through two representations, a modular inverse and forward chain and a
physics-structured reduced-order model. The inverse model is trained on Simscape
data and evaluated on deployment runs drawn entirely outside that training
distribution, where it reproduces most of the valve-command variance with an
accuracy that is nearly flat across plant domains. It requires no
deployment-specific retraining and remains frozen in every downstream stack. The
reduced-order model is transferred differently and the difference is stated
wherever it matters: it receives a small bounded calibration against the
deployment plant, which is why it serves as a reference condition rather than as
an independent competitor to the inverse and forward pair.

\textbf{Control-Relevant Identification and Validation:} A demonstration that
predictive accuracy can conceal the loss of exactly the property a controller
needs, together with the procedure that detects it. The first forward model
reached a near-perfect supervised score while learning to ignore the valve
command and reconstruct motion from the state history instead. The validation
loss did not show this; ablating the command did. Redesigning the excitation and
retraining recovered genuine command sensitivity, and the pairing of excitation
design with input ablation is established here as a required test for learned
hydraulic models intended for control rather than for prediction.

\textbf{Reduced-Order Actuator Model:} A command-conditioned grey-box actuator
in which pressure, force, velocity and extension propagation remain explicit
while learning covers valve realisation, unmodelled loading and bounded residual
effects. It is accurate enough to serve as the deployment plant, stable in
recursive rollout, and cheap enough to evaluate inside GPU-parallel policy
training. Zeroing or shuffling its command collapses its extension and velocity
accuracy, which establishes that the command authority is real rather than
inferred from context.

\textbf{Policy-Only Control Through Learned Hydraulics:} A damped least-squares
controller supplies privileged demonstrations during training and is then
removed, so that the distilled policy emits desired cylinder motion directly
into a learned actuator representation and constitutes the entire deployed
controller. Both representations support closed-loop terrain following in this
configuration, although two learned models placed in series could have
compounded their errors.

\textbf{Robustness and the Limits of Adaptation:} A matched evaluation protocol
that scores the resulting stacks on tracking, control effort, latency,
disturbance rejection and sensitivity to the magnitude of plant variation,
together with a procedure that decides in advance whether online plant
conditioning will repay the effort. The first of its two measurements
establishes whether plant identity is present in the data the machine produces.
The second supplies the true plant parameters through a privileged encoder and
measures what the closed loop does with them. Both precede estimator training,
and either can end the effort before it is incurred.

The components on which these contributions rest, namely the causal plant
estimator, the bounded adaptation path and the eight-gate evaluation harness,
are described where they are used. They are technical enablers rather than
separate claims.

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:intro:structure}

This thesis investigates how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from
high-fidelity simulation can be reduced, transferred into a computationally
efficient robotic simulation and used for reinforcement-learning control,
grounding its investigation in the identifiability of the actuator models and in
the robustness of the controllers built on them. The document is organized as
follows:

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals}} establishes the background against
which the remainder is read. It introduces the machine class and its
electro-hydraulic actuation, recalls the kinematic tools underlying the
classical controller, and reviews how conventional model-based control handles
hydraulic uncertainty. It then treats the learning methods this work builds on,
namely data-driven dynamics models and the pitfalls of identifying them from
closed-loop data, reduced-order and grey-box modelling, domain randomisation and
latent adaptation, and policy optimisation with structured priors. The chapter
closes by stating the gap that remains and the position of the three control
stacks examined here.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:system}} analyses the platform, using the MULAG
FME~500 mounted on a Unimog carrier as the case study. It describes the rigid
bodies and the serial chain they form, the coordinate mappings between task
space and cylinder space, and the closed kinematic loops introduced by the
cylinder mounts. It specifies what the controller can observe, separating
measured quantities from quantities produced by a model, and presents the
high-fidelity Simscape model from which the actuator data derives. The chapter
also states the task, the plant capability limits and the acceptance criteria
against which every later result is scored.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:data}} presents the data generation strategy and
answers the first research question. It describes the excitation design, the
randomised factor set defining the family of plants, and the procedures that
keep training, validation and reserved test configurations separate. It then
reports the identifiability audit applied to the resulting collection, including
the measured finding that excitation gathered under a task controller does not
separate plants, and the open-loop collection that repairs this.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:models}} develops the learned models and answers the
first three research questions. It presents the inverse model that maps a
desired cylinder motion to a valve command, the forward model that predicts the
response, and the evidence that supervised accuracy alone does not establish
that either model retains command authority. It then presents the
command-conditioned reduced-order model that serves as the deployment plant
inside GPU-parallel training. The chapter closes with the material required for
the final research question, namely the privileged latent and its measured
limits, the causal estimator that infers a deployable substitute from observable
history, and the bounded adapter through which that estimate reaches the
actuator path.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:control}} describes the control framework and its
integration with the simulator, and answers the fourth research question. It
presents the damped least-squares teacher used for demonstration and data
collection, the observation and action interfaces shared by all three stacks,
the distillation of the teacher into a student policy, and the
reference-anchored fine-tuning that makes further policy optimisation admissible
without discarding the distilled behaviour. It then assembles the three deployed
control stacks and states the rules under which they may be compared.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}} reports the experimental evaluation and
answers the last two research questions. It establishes the excitation envelope
within which plant identity is recoverable, the sufficiency of the frozen
inverse model, and what the inferred latent encodes. It measures latency through
the actuation chain and the response of each stack to a commanded change, then
compares the three deployed controllers on closed-loop tracking and control
effort. It closes by measuring the disturbance envelope of each controller, an
oracle reference for what plant-identity conditioning contributes within this
architecture, and the sensitivity of each stack to the magnitude of plant
variation.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}} concludes the thesis. It summarises what
the work establishes and what it does not, states the design implications that
follow from the measured results, records the limitations of the evidence, and
identifies the measurements that should be taken next.

\textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:system-parameters}} provides the system parameters and
the coordinate mappings between task space, joint space and cylinder space
referred to in Chapter~\ref{chap:system}.
\textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}} lists the dataset manifests and the factor
ranges that define the family of plants used in
Chapters~\ref{chap:data} and~\ref{chap:evaluation}.
\textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:models}} records the model architectures, the
normalisers and the checkpoint hashes for every learned model reported in
Chapter~\ref{chap:models}.
\textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:ppo-config}} documents the PPO and IsaacLab
configuration used for the training described in Chapter~\ref{chap:control}.
\textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:results}} collects supplementary results supporting
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.
