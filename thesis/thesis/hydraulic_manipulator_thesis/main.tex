\RRLABabstract{Abstract}{%
Hydraulic manipulators are hard to control precisely. The actuator response is
nonlinear, depends on load and supply pressure, and varies between machines.
Simulating those effects faithfully is possible but far too slow for
reinforcement learning, while commanding joint positions directly discards the
behaviour that makes the machine difficult.

This thesis asks how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from a high-fidelity
Simscape model of a verge mowing arm can be reduced, transferred into an
efficient robotic simulation and used for reinforcement-learning control. Two
actuator representations are identified from the same data: a modular inverse
and forward chain, which transfers frozen, and a structurally constrained
reduced-order model, which receives a bounded calibration against the deployment
plant. A model can score well on supervised prediction while ignoring the valve
command a controller must use, so excitation design and command ablation are
established as admission tests for a learned actuator. Both representations
carry a distilled policy that replaces the classical teacher entirely, and in a
matched comparison over held-out meshes, trajectory families and reserved plant
configurations the two produce different tracking and command behaviour.

Online adaptation is examined last. The deployed policy carries a payload
estimate computed at runtime from the task observation it already receives,
never from a privileged read. Only the context columns of an otherwise frozen
policy are trained, so zeroing the estimate recovers the frozen controller
exactly. All three training seeds place their entire context weight on the
payload channel unprompted. Where a four to fivefold payload costs the frozen
controller ground contact, the adaptive policy improves cut height, lateral
tracking and ground contact together, paired over a held-out mesh with every
pooled confidence interval excluding zero, though two seeds of three carry the
effect. At a payload the frozen controller still handles, the same
conditioning costs lateral accuracy, so the mechanism belongs in the loaded
regime rather than everywhere. Permuting the payload across environments,
which preserves its distribution and destroys only its pairing, is beaten by
the correct estimate but recovers most of the gain alone: the benefit is part
conditioning on a plausible load and part online identification. The true
payload is not an upper bound: at the largest load it can drive the tool off
the ground, because the estimate's gradual arrival is itself part of the
mechanism. Effective control of this machine therefore depends not only on
model accuracy and policy robustness, but on conditioning the controller on a
load it infers for itself.
}
