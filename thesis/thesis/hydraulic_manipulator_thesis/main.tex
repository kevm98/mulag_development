\RRLABabstract{Abstract}{%
Hydraulic manipulators are difficult to control precisely, because the actuator
response is nonlinear, depends on load and supply pressure, and varies between
individual machines. Simulating that response faithfully is possible, but the
models that do so are far too slow to be evaluated inside reinforcement
learning. Ignoring the hydraulics and commanding joint positions directly avoids
that cost, but discards the behaviour that makes the machine difficult to
control in the first place.

This thesis investigates how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from
high-fidelity simulation can be reduced, transferred into a computationally
efficient robotic simulation, and used for reinforcement-learning control of a
verge mowing arm. Two actuator representations are identified from randomised
simulation campaigns. The first is a modular chain of an inverse model, which
maps a desired cylinder motion to a valve command, and a forward model, which
predicts the resulting response. The second is a physics-structured
reduced-order model, in which pressure, force and extension propagation remain
explicit while learning covers valve realisation and unmodelled loading. Both
representations are realised in the deployment simulator and used as the
actuation interface for policy learning. A damped least-squares controller
provides privileged demonstrations during training and is removed afterwards, so
that the distilled policy becomes the sole deployed controller.

The inverse and forward representation is shown to transfer to the deployment
simulator without deployment-specific retraining, while the reduced-order model
is transferred with a small bounded calibration against the deployment plant.
Supervised accuracy is shown to be an unreliable guide to control,
because a forward model achieved a high predictive score while learning to
ignore the valve command. Excitation design and input ablation are established
as the tests that expose this failure. Both representations then support
closed-loop terrain following, and the choice of representation is shown to
change the control strategy that the policy learns. Online adaptation is
examined last, against the robustness already obtained. Plant identity proves
recoverable only from deliberate excitation and not from the data the task
controller produces, and the trained stacks absorb the plant variation studied
here without assistance, which bounds the benefit that conditioning on plant
identity can provide. The two measurements that establish this bound, an
identifiability envelope and an oracle ceiling read from the true plant
factors, are cheap and both precede estimator training. Together they form a
decision procedure for whether online adaptation is worth building on a given
machine, which this thesis takes as its principal contribution.
}
