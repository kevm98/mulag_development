\RRLABabstract{Abstract}{%
Hydraulic manipulators are difficult to control precisely: the actuator response
is nonlinear, depends on load and supply pressure, and varies between machines.
Faithful simulation is possible but far too slow for reinforcement learning,
while commanding joint positions directly discards the behaviour that makes the
machine difficult.

This thesis investigates how hydraulic actuator dynamics learned from
high-fidelity simulation can be reduced, transferred into an efficient robotic
simulation, and used for reinforcement-learning control of a verge mowing arm.
Actuator data from a high-fidelity Simscape model are used to identify a modular
inverse and forward model chain and a physics-structured reduced-order model.
The former maps desired cylinder motion to valve commands and predicts the
resulting response; the latter keeps pressure, force and extension propagation
explicit while learning valve realisation and unmodelled loading. A damped
least-squares controller supplies privileged demonstrations and is then removed,
leaving the distilled policy as the sole deployed controller.

Both actuator representations transfer into the deployment simulation and
support closed-loop terrain following. The evaluation also shows why supervised
accuracy alone is insufficient: a model can predict motion well while ignoring
the valve command that a controller must use. Excitation design and input
ablation are therefore treated as necessary checks before a learned actuator
model is admitted to control.

Online adaptation is examined only after transfer, control and robustness have
been established. The trained stacks absorb much of the randomised plant
variation without adaptation, while a more abrupt plant change exposes the
remaining gap. A bounded residual conditioned on causal history improves
tracking when it acts directly on a valve command with authority to change.
A capacity-matched control without live history does not produce the same
effect. Delivering adaptation through an inverse-model output instead produces
no closed-loop change because the command is already saturated. The improvement
is not consistent across all training replicates, so the evidence establishes a
mechanism rather than a fully reliable method. The thesis contributes a
decision procedure for testing whether online adaptation is worth building and
shows that, on a saturating actuator, the placement of the correction can matter
more than the quality of the estimate.
}
